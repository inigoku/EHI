# Act One · The Lighthouse
## 1 · Ada

I wake before the alarm, the way I almost always do since I started living alone.

In Madrid it never happened. There I slept until someone woke me, and that someone decided the time, too. Here my body wakes on its own, at half past six, as if it were in a hurry to check that the sea is still where it belongs.

I lie still for a moment under the duvet, listening. The wind is still gentle. The gulls haven't started. All I can hear is the sea, down below, breathing against the rocks with that slow rhythm that wouldn't let me sleep at first and that now I'm not sure I could sleep without.

I put my feet on the floor and the cold of the tiles climbs up my legs. I love that cold. It reminds me that the house is mine, that nobody has put the heating on for me or told me to put some slippers on.

I pick up my phone to check the buoy. Thirteen degrees.

I smile to myself. My grandmother would say I'm *tola*. That swimming in October is for madwomen and Germans. Since I'm a quarter German, a quarter of it is forgiven.

---

The path down to the cove runs between heather and loose stones. At this hour it's still night, so I wear a head torch, like a miner. I know by heart the three spots where, if you put a foot wrong, you go over. The first few months I went down with my heart in my mouth. Now I go down thinking about other things.

It smells of salt, of seaweed, of wet heather. It smells of cold.

I leave my towel on the usual rock and take off my coat. Underneath I have on only my swimsuit. The air raises goosebumps over my whole skin, my arms, the back of my neck, my belly. I hold there for a second, eyes closed, feeling my body shrink and wake up at the same time.

No one has touched me in a year.

I don't think about it often. I think about it in moments like this, when the wind passes over my skin and I realise it's the only thing that has brushed against me in days. And it hurts in a strange way. It isn't desire, or not only. It's more a hunger for something I don't know how to name.

I go into the water without thinking, because if you think, you don't go in. The cold bites my ankles, my knees, my thighs. When it reaches my waist I let out a cry nobody hears. And I dive.

For three seconds nothing exists. Not Madrid, not work, not him. Only my heart pounding like mad and my skin burning with cold.

Then the calm comes.

It's hard to explain. It's as if the sea were holding me up. As if, for a moment, I didn't have to hold myself up alone. I turn over and float on my back, arms open and ears under the water, and look at the sky that's beginning to turn lilac in the east.

In the west, though, over the horizon, there's a band of cloud, low and dark. Flat on top, as if cut with a ruler.

I don't like it at all.

I saw it last night on the screen, small and far away, and I convinced myself it would break up before it got here. I was wrong. And it won't be the only thing I'm wrong about this week.

---

I walk back to the lighthouse shivering, the towel over my shoulders and my lips purple.

I've been living here for a year, in the keeper's house at the foot of the tower. Three rooms, a wood-burner, a sofa with a tartan blanket I bought at a shop in Kirkwall, and an enormous window that looks out on the Atlantic with nothing in between. Up at the head of the tower, the big lamp sleeps under a grey canvas cover. It hasn't shone for anyone in decades.

Sometimes I think we're alike, she and I.

I have a hot shower, a long one, until I stop shaking. When I get out, the mirror is steamed up. I wipe it with my hand and look at myself.

Thirty-two years old. Dark hair, wet, longer than it's ever been. Collarbones sharp. My grandfather's eyes, grey, which my grandmother says are the best thing I have.

In Madrid I wore my hair short. Now I'm letting it grow, and every time I dry it in front of the mirror I feel a small victory I couldn't explain to anyone.

---

I'm a meteorologist. I work from the kitchen, with three monitors on the wooden table where the keepers, I suppose, used to eat. The company is based in Bilbao and sells forecasts to wind farms and shipping lines, people who lose a lot of money if the wind blows when it shouldn't. My boss says I'm the meteorologist with the best view in Europe.

I make myself a *café con leche* and open the European model.

Every night it runs fifty-one times, each time with the data changed a little, to see how wrong it can be. In the trade they're called ensemble members. I've called them iterations since university, because it sounds to me like something that comes back. Usually they spread apart like the strands of an undone plait. I like watching them. I like thinking that the future has fifty-one shapes and only one of them will come to pass.

This morning almost all of them point to the same place. Here.

A low south of Iceland, deepening so fast that I set my cup down on the table without noticing. Explosive cyclogenesis. In Orkney nobody needs it explained. Ten-metre waves. Roofs on the road. Boats that don't come back. By morning the Met Office will have given it a name, and the next name on the list is Ailsa.

It will arrive in two days. Maybe less.

I feel the tingle I feel with big storms, a mix of fear and fascination I've never confessed to anyone. It's happened since I was a girl. My mother used to lock herself in the bathroom when it thundered and I would sneak off to the window to see it all.

At the morning meeting I show it to Marcos and Lucía.

'It's going to run right over you, Ada,' Marcos says.

'The lighthouse has been there a hundred and forty years.'

'The lighthouse, yes. I meant you.'

I say nothing. Lucía changes the subject to rescue me, as always. But when the call ends she messages me privately.

*Are you really all right out there on your own? If you like I'll ring you tonight.*

I send her a heart and a photo of the sea. It's easier than answering.

Lucía was the one who encouraged me to come here. She says the sea cures almost everything. I don't know if that's true, but so far it hasn't done me any harm.

---

At four a voice message comes in from my grandmother.

I listen with the phone pressed to my ear, standing by the window.

*Peedie lass, the radio says a big one's coming. Fasten those shutters well, eh? And don't swim tomorrow, I know you. Wednesday I'm expecting you for lunch, I'm making* empanada. *Un bico, miña rula.*

*Miña rula.* My little dove. She's called me that since I was born.

My eyes fill with tears for no reason. Or for every reason at once. There are days when tenderness catches me off guard and disarms me more than any blow.

I answer with another voice message, saying yes to everything, though we both know I'm going to swim tomorrow.

---

In the afternoon, when I've finished, I do what I always do. I open the sketchbook.

In the wardrobe I have a box full of black sketchbooks, all the same. Twenty-three years of sketchbooks. Almost all of it is clouds, because I draw the sky the way other women draw flowers. But in the margins, in the corners, in any empty space, there he is.

I know how it sounds. A grown woman who has spent her whole life drawing the same man. I started drawing him at nine, when he was still a boy, and he grew up with me. I've never given him a name. Almost no one has seen him.

I sit by the window, legs tucked up and a blanket over my knees. The afternoon light comes in grey and soft. I sharpen the tip of my pencil.

I start with the eyes, as always.

Dark. Patient. Eyes that look at you as if they'd been waiting for you a long time and wouldn't mind waiting a little longer.

No one has ever looked at me like that. That's why I draw them.

Then the line of the jaw. The hair, a little long, falling over his forehead. The mouth, which never comes out the way I want. I stop there, pencil in the air, and catch myself imagining what his voice would be like. Whether he'd speak softly. Whether he'd laugh at my bad jokes. Whether his hands would be warm.

I blush, alone, in my own kitchen. Ridiculous.

But I don't stop. I keep looking at him longer than anyone spends on a drawing, with that tug in my chest I sometimes feel when I finish him. As if I were missing something I've never had. As if I missed him without ever having met him.

Then my phone buzzes.

---

I turn it over thinking it'll be Lucía.

It isn't Lucía. It's a number with no name.

I deleted it from my contacts almost a year ago, the day I left Madrid. Deleting it didn't do much good. I know those nine digits by heart, the way you know the phone number of a house you no longer live in.

*Still in Orkney?*

I look at the screen. Three words, no greeting, as if we'd spoken yesterday. I feel something in my chest. Tiredness, maybe. Or sadness. A whole year learning not to wait for his messages, and three words are enough to set everything moving inside again.

Even in three words I can hear his voice. The way he had of saying my name: the first *a* drawn out, the second falling, the way you'd say it to a little girl who has stained the tablecloth again. *Aaada.* For three years I thought it was affection.

I haven't told anyone in Madrid where I live now. I wonder who told him. Then I wonder why it matters so much to me.

I set the phone face down on the table, on top of the sketchbook, as if I could cover it up.

I don't answer.

That night I don't eat any supper.

I put a pan of water on for tea and watch it until it boils, without moving, and then I turn off the hob and don't make it. My stomach is clenched like a fist. Not even the smell of the bread I bought this morning tempts me.

I tidy the kitchen, which is already tidy. I sort the sketchbooks in the box by year. I do all the things a woman does when she doesn't want to think about something, and that something sits down beside her anyway, patiently, to wait until she's done.

I try to breathe the way the psychologist taught me. Four seconds in, four holding, four out. On the third round I realise I'm counting too fast, as if I wanted to get it over with. I give up.

The house is making noises I've never heard. Or that I've always heard and never listened to. The floorboards creak on their own. A loose shutter is knocking somewhere, softly, like someone rapping with their knuckles and thinking better of it. The wind is beginning to slip under the door with a thin whistle, almost a voice.

I curl up on the sofa with the blanket up to my chin. I turn on only the small lamp, the one on the table. I like that light. It makes the house seem smaller and more mine.

And I open the sketchbook again.

I don't know why. For the same reason, I suppose, that as a girl I used to crawl under the sheets with a torch when there was a storm. Because it's mine. Because it's the only thing in the house nobody can touch.

There he is, in the corner of the page, surrounded by cumulus. The dark eyes. The unfinished mouth.

I look at him for a long time. In the lamplight he seems almost alive, with shadows I didn't draw. I run my fingertip along the edge of the paper, without touching him, so as not to smudge him.

'You'd wait for me,' I tell him quietly.

I hear myself and feel embarrassed. A thirty-two-year-old woman talking to a drawing in the middle of the night. If anyone saw me, they'd think I was going mad. Maybe they'd be right.

But there's no one. Only me, the wind, and him.

I hug the sketchbook to my chest and rest my head against the back of the sofa. I think I won't be able to sleep. I think about the message, about the nine digits, about his way of writing without a greeting, as if one weren't needed. I think about everything I left there, and everything I brought with me without realising.

And then I think about the eyes in the drawing. About how they wait without hurry. About what it would be like for someone to look at me like that, for real, just once.

I don't know when I fall asleep.

I wake at three in the morning to a gust of wind that makes the windowpanes shake. The lamp is still on. The sketchbook has slipped into my lap, open at the same page.

I get up and go to the window, barefoot, with the blanket over my shoulders.

I can't see the sea. I can only hear it. Louder than ever, closer, as if it had climbed the cliff to have a look.

And the band of cloud is no longer a band.

It's the whole sky.

### Maruxa · Vigo, March 1966

My mother didn't cry at the station. She cried afterwards, my sister told me, on the bus back, the whole way home. But not on the platform. On the platform she straightened the collar of my coat, slipped an embroidered handkerchief into my pocket, and told me to eat.

'You're going to waste away to nothing, Maruxiña. Over there they eat potatoes with everything.'

That was the last thing she said to me. To eat.

I was nineteen, with a borrowed coat, a cardboard suitcase tied with string, and a slip of paper with an address I didn't know how to pronounce. *Stuttgart-Feuerbach.* I'd practised it all week in front of the mirror and it still came out wrong.

There were a lot of us on the platform. Girls from Ourense, from Lugo, from the Coast. Some with their sweethearts, who kissed them on the mouth in front of everyone, without shame, because it didn't matter any more. Some with small children who were staying behind with their grandmothers. I had only my mother and my little sister, who held on to my skirt as if she could stop the train from moving.

I had no sweetheart. That made me a little envious and a little relieved. No one would be waiting for me. No one would write me letters that made me cry. I was going to earn the money we needed at home, because my father couldn't manage the boat any more and the sea doesn't give enough for everyone, and then I'd come back. Two years. Three at most.

That's what I thought.

When the train whistle blew, my sister started wailing. She was seven. She grabbed my skirt with both hands and there was no getting her loose. My mother had to prise her fingers open one by one, very patiently, while she told her that *Tata* would be back soon, that *Tata* would bring her chocolate from Germany.

I climbed into the carriage with my face burning and my skirt crumpled where she'd held it. I leaned out of the window. My mother raised her hand. She didn't wave it. She only raised it, open, and left it like that, still, in the air, as the train began to move.

I raised mine too.

We stayed like that, both with our hands open, until the curve of the track took her away.

---

The train took thirty-one hours to reach the border at Irún, and then another whole day to Germany.

I didn't sleep. I sat on a wooden bench, with my suitcase between my legs so no one would steal it, watching a Spain I didn't know go by the window. Castile, yellow and flat as a table. Then France, which was green like Galicia but in a different way, tidier, as if someone combed it every morning.

Beside me sat a girl from Lugo called Pilar. She was twenty, with a round face and huge hands from milking cows. She offered me chorizo wrapped in newspaper and told me her sweetheart was waiting for her there, in Stuttgart, that he'd gone a year before and had written to say there was work for both of them.

'And you? Who have you got there?'

'No one.'

Pilar looked at me with pity. Then she gave me another piece of chorizo, as if that would fix it.

At night, when the carriage went dark and all you could hear was the clatter and the snoring of a man at the far end, I rested my forehead against the cold glass of the window and looked out. There was nothing to see. Only, now and then, the lights of some village going by fast, small, yellow, like the lamps of boats at sea.

I thought of home. Of the wood-burning stove. Of my father smoking on the bench by the door. Of the smell of the harbour in the morning, of salt, of diesel, of fish just landed.

And for the first time I felt afraid. A big fear, the kind that gets into your chest and won't let you breathe. Afraid of not coming back. Afraid of coming back different. Afraid that over there no one would know who I was.

I started singing softly, without realising. One of the songs my mother sang when she hung out the washing. A very old one, the kind that belongs to no one.

Pilar woke up and elbowed me.

'*Cala, muller*, you're going to wake the whole train.'

I stopped. I hadn't even noticed I was singing.

That happened to me a lot. It still does. My husband used to say I sang the way other people breathe, without thinking, and that was why I sang so well: because nobody was listening to me, not even me.

But that was later. Much later.

On that train, in the dark, with my forehead against the glass, I didn't yet know he existed. He was there, fifteen hundred kilometres away, asleep in a small room in a boarding house in Feuerbach, not knowing I existed.

And the train kept bringing us closer, kilometre by kilometre, in the dark.

## 2 · Ada

I don't wait for Wednesday.

When I get up, my neck sore from sleeping on the sofa, the sky is still covered from end to end and the wind is no longer a breeze. It's a constant shove, insistent, that makes the cracks round the windows whistle. I check the model with my first coffee. The low is still deepening. Still coming.

By midday tomorrow at the latest, the serious part will begin. And I want to see my grandmother before then.

I don't put it to myself that way. I tell myself I have to buy batteries and diesel for the generator, that the fridge is empty, that on Wednesday, with the storm on top of us, I won't be able to get down. All of it is true. But the truth underneath is something else. I need someone to hold me, and the only person in the world I can ask without explaining anything lives in a house with blue windows by the harbour.

---

The road into Stromness is two and a half miles of single-track between drystone dykes, sheep and fields of a green that doesn't exist in Madrid. No trees to speak of. I drive it in my old car, both hands on the wheel because the wind shoves it towards the ditch at every gap in the dykes.

The town is on edge. You can feel it in everything. The men at the harbour are doubling up the mooring lines and hauling the small boats up the slipway with a tractor. The women are bringing their flowerpots inside. There's a queue at the petrol station, and at the bakery someone has handwritten a sign: *Shut the morn*.

I love Stromness when it's getting ready for a storm. It's like watching a family get ready for a difficult visit. Everyone knows what has to be done without saying it.

I buy what I have to buy. Batteries, candles, two jerrycans of diesel, bread for three days, a bag of mandarins because they're beautiful. Then I leave the car by the harbour and walk to the last house before the breakwater.

My grandmother's house is grey stone, small, with shutters painted cobalt blue. She repaints them every spring whether they need it or not. She says blue scares off sadness. I don't know if that's true, but every time I turn the corner and see them, something in my chest loosens.

I don't have time to knock. The door opens on its own.

'I knew you'd come today,' she says. 'Come in, come in, before the wind carries you off.'

She's wearing her flowered apron and her hands are covered in flour. She hugs me like that, without wiping them, and leaves two white prints on the back of my coat. She smells of slow-cooked onion, of bay leaf, and of the rose cologne she's worn for as long as I can remember.

I hold on to her a little longer than usual. She just pats my back, like when I was little and woke up from a nightmare.

'You're cold as a fish,' she says at last. 'Did you swim?'

'No.'

'Liar. Your hair's wet.'

I laugh. It's true. I went down to the cove at seven, though the sea was already too rough to swim far. I only went in up to my waist. But I went in.

'You're *tola*,' she says, and pushes me towards the kitchen. 'Just like your grandfather.'

---

My grandmother's kitchen is the warmest place in the world. I mean that literally. It has a range that's been lit since 1976 and only goes out in August, and a wooden bench against the wall where I used to sit as a girl with my legs dangling.

I sit on the bench. My legs don't dangle any more, but the feeling is the same.

My grandmother moves back and forth without stopping. She's seventy-nine and has an energy that shames me. Her name is Maruxa. Well, her name is María, but nobody has called her that since the day she was baptised.

She's making *empanada*. A day earlier than planned, for me.

'I knew you'd come,' she says again, as if that explained everything.

I have a theory about my grandmother. That she knows everything before it happens. That she has a kind of radar for the people she loves, the way I have one for storms. She says it isn't radar, it's the habit of looking.

While the dough rests, she sets two cups of pot-brewed coffee on the table and sits down across from me. She looks at me over her glasses, up and down, with that gaze that undresses you without taking anything off.

'You're thin.'

'I'm the same.'

'You're thin and you've got circles under your eyes. Are you sleeping?'

'Some.'

'Some.' She clicks her tongue. 'At your age I slept like a log. Of course, at your age I already had your mother and your uncle, and I got into bed so tired I fell asleep praying.'

It amuses me to picture her young, worn out, praying. It amuses me and makes me envious. A life so full it leaves you no time to think about what's missing.

---

On the wall, above the sideboard, is the same old photo.

My grandparents on their wedding day, in black and white. She in a simple dress, long-sleeved, with a smile so big it looks as if it's about to burst off her face. He, very tall, blond, in a suit with sleeves too short for him, looking at her instead of at the camera.

Klaus Koch. My grandfather. He died six years ago, but in this house he never quite left. His pipe is still in the green glass ashtray. His corduroy jacket is still on the coat stand by the door. My grandmother says she doesn't put them away because she doesn't feel like it. I know she's lying, and she knows I know.

They married in her village in Galicia. Five years later, when the oil came to Flotta and the terminal wanted men who understood engines, they came to Stromness with my mother in a carrycot, and stayed.

'Tell it to me again,' I ask her.

'Tell what?'

'You know what.'

She plays hard to get, as always. She loves it when I ask. And I love hearing it, even though I've known it by heart since I was five, even though I could recite it word for word.

'I was nineteen,' she begins, her hands round her cup. 'I went to Germany the way so many girls went, to work in a factory in Stuttgart. Car parts. I didn't know one word of German. I cried every night that first month, hugging my pillow so the girls in my room wouldn't hear me.'

'And then he turned up.'

'Then he turned up. He was the line supervisor. Very serious, very blond, very German. He'd walk behind us checking everything and never said a thing. I thought he had it in for me, because he was looking at me all the time.'

'And he didn't have it in for you.'

'No.' A young laugh escapes her, a girl's laugh. 'It took him nine months to speak to me. Nine months, Ada. And when he finally came up to me, red as a tomato, he told me in horrible Spanish that I sang very pretty while I worked.'

'And you didn't know you were singing.'

'No idea. I sang without noticing. Things from home, my mother's songs, *alalás* from when we harvested. And he'd spent nine months listening to me. Nine months learning my songs without understanding a word.'

Her eyes are shining. They always shine at that part. And I always get a lump in my throat.

Because that's the part I love most. Not the wedding. Not the happy ending. That. A man who listens to you for nine months without asking you for anything, just because he likes how you sound when you don't realise anyone's listening.

'We were together five years over there,' she goes on. 'And then your great-grandmother fell ill and I had to come back. I told him I'd understand if he didn't come. That it was another country, another language, another life. That I wasn't asking him for anything.'

'And?'

She gets up to look at the dough.

'And that's for another day, my *empanada*'s going to be ruined.'

She always does the same thing. She gets to the platform in Stuttgart, to herself alone with her suitcase, and she gets up. She's told me a thousand times and she's never told me that part. I know he came, because he's in the photo on the sideboard and because my surname is Koch. But I don't know how. Or when. Or whether he hesitated. When I ask her, she laughs and tells me there are things you don't tell, you sing them, and I don't sing any more.

Koch is my mother's surname. I've carried it first since the winter my father left for Vigo. My mother went to the civil registry that same week to ask them to swap the order of my surnames, and my father signed without a word of protest, the way he signed everything that took him further away from us. My father's name stayed behind, where no one sees it. I haven't missed it for a single day.

We fall silent. Outside, the wind is rattling the windowpanes.

When I was little, I thought that was what love was. That someday someone would cross half of Europe to knock on my door. Now I'm thirty-two and I don't think that any more. Or I think it, but I'm embarrassed to.

'And you?' my grandmother asks out of nowhere.

'Me what?'

'Nobody?'

'*Abuela.*'

'I'm only asking. A grandmother has the right to ask.'

'I'm fine on my own.'

She gives me the look she gives when she knows you're handing her the short version.

'Being fine on your own is one thing,' she says. 'Being on your own so you won't be hurt is another.'

I don't answer. I get up to check the oven, though it doesn't need checking.

'That one from Madrid, is he still writing to you?' she asks behind my back.

Something inside me shrinks. I think of the phone face down. Of the three words.

'That *empanada* smells burnt,' I say.

'That *empanada* smells like heaven, and you change the subject just like your mother.'

But she doesn't push. My grandmother never pushes. She leaves the question on the table, like a plate, and waits for you to get hungry.

To fill the silence she tells me, for the umpteenth time, how my grandfather learned Galician. With an oilcloth-covered notebook where he wrote down the words he heard at the harbour, spelled however he pleased. *Carallo* spelled with a *k*. *Morriña* with two *r*s at the end. He'd learn them by heart and come out with them at the worst possible moments, in his Stuttgart accent, and the whole village would split their sides laughing.

'He sang the *alalás* terribly,' she says, her eyes wet from laughing. 'Off-key like a pig. And he didn't care. He'd stand in the bow of your uncle's boat and sing at the top of his lungs, and the men on the pier would cover their ears.'

'And you?'

'I'd die of embarrassment.' She's quiet for a moment, looking at the pipe in the ashtray. 'And now I'd give anything to hear him sing off-key again.'

I take her hand across the table. Her skin is thin as tissue paper, covered in spots, and warm. Always warm.

We say nothing more. There's no need.

---

We eat by the window, watching the water in the harbour grow darker and darker. The *empanada* is perfect, the crust crisp and the bonito filling juicy. I eat two and a half pieces, and my grandmother watches me eat with the satisfaction of someone who has won a small war.

Afterwards we do the washing-up together, side by side, without talking. She washes and I dry. It's what we've always done. There's something in that shared silence, with our hands busy, that heals me more than any conversation.

When I've dried the last glass, she wipes her hands on her apron and goes to the larder. She comes back with an enormous flask and a parcel wrapped in newspaper.

'*Caldo*,' she says, pointing at the flask. 'For tomorrow, you won't feel like cooking. And good candles, the ones from the shop don't last a breath.'

'Abuela, I've got candles.'

'You've got candles from the shop.'

She stuffs it all into my bag. Then she helps me into my coat, as if I were eight, and does up the top button.

The sketchbook is peeking out of my pocket. I always carry it with me. She sees it and touches it with her fingertip.

'And that sketchbook?'

'Clouds. The usual.'

She looks me in the eye. For a second I have the feeling she can see through the black covers, through the clouds, all the way to the corner of the page where a pair of dark eyes is waiting for me.

Only once, in my whole life, has my grandmother seen that drawing. I was twelve and spending the summer in this house. I left the sketchbook open on the kitchen table and, when I came back from the beach, she was looking at it. She didn't scold me. She only asked who he was. I told her nobody, that I'd made him up, red as a tomato.

*Well, you make him up very well*, she told me then. *He has the face of a good person.*

She has never mentioned it since. In twenty years.

Until this afternoon. She kisses my forehead, her lips dry and warm, and looks at me with that face of knowing something I haven't told her.

'Are you still drawing that lad?'

### Maruxa · Stuttgart, April 1966

The hostel was a red-brick barracks beside the railway line. They called it the *Wohnheim*, which took me a month to learn to say, and it had a very long corridor with doors on both sides, like a hospital.

I got a room with three iron beds and a window that faced a wall. I shared it with Pilar, the girl from Lugo, and with a Portuguese woman called Fátima who never spoke. Fátima was thirty, with a husband in Lisbon and two little daughters. She had their photos pinned to the wall above her bed, and every night, before she slept, she gave them a kiss with her fingertips.

I had no photos. I'd left in such a hurry it didn't even occur to me to bring any.

What I remember most about that first month is the cold. A cold different from the one at home. In Galicia the cold is damp, it gets into your bones, but it smells of something, of firewood, of the sea, of wet earth. The cold in Stuttgart smelled of nothing. It was dry and clean and cut your face like a new knife.

And the food. *Ay*, the food. Potatoes with everything, just as my mother said. Boiled potatoes, fried potatoes, potatoes in salad with vinegar, which was the worst thing I'd ever tasted in my life. And a black bread, hard, that weighed like a stone. I dreamed of my mother's *empanada*. I really dreamed of it, at night, and woke up with the taste of the crust in my mouth and tears on my pillow.

I cried every night of that first month. Every one.

I cried hugging the pillow, biting it so no one would hear. Pilar slept like a log, but Fátima didn't. One night I heard her move in the dark. I thought she was going to tell me to be quiet.

She said nothing. She got up, sat on the edge of my bed, and put her hand on my back. Just like that, without speaking. A big hand, warm, the hand of a woman who has raised children.

She stayed like that until I fell asleep.

We never talked about it. In the morning she still didn't speak and I still didn't know what to say to her. But from that night on, every time I saw her kiss her daughters on the wall, I felt that I belonged a little to that family too.

---

The factory was a fifteen-minute walk away. We made car parts. I didn't know what they were for. I only knew they were small, metal, with holes, and that you had to check them one by one before putting them in the boxes. If one had a burr, out. If a hole was crooked, out.

It was a job for the eyes. Eight hours looking at metal under a white light that hummed. At the end of the day my head ached and I saw parts with my eyes closed.

There were thirty of us women on the line. Spanish, Portuguese, Italian, two Turkish women who kept their hair covered with a scarf. No one spoke German. We understood each other with gestures, with laughter, with the few words we were picking up. *Danke. Bitte. Schnell.* Above all *schnell*. Fast. It was what they said to us most.

The supervisors were German. Serious men, in grey coats, who walked behind us with a clipboard in hand, checking everything.

There was one who frightened me more than the others.

He was very tall. So tall he had to stoop a little to pass under the pipes on the factory floor. Blond, his hair cut almost to the scalp, and light eyes, grey, like the Stuttgart sky. He never smiled. He never spoke. He only walked behind the line, unhurried, and watched.

And he watched me.

More than the others. I was sure of it. Every time I looked up from the parts, there he was, at the far end of the factory floor, with his clipboard, watching me. And the moment our eyes met, he looked down and wrote something.

My stomach would knot up. I thought he was going to sack me. That he'd seen me talking to Pilar, or laughing, or getting a part wrong. That he was writing down my mistakes on his clipboard so he could dismiss me at the end of the month and send me back to Galicia with my cardboard suitcase empty.

One night I told Pilar.

'That German has it in for me.'

'The tall one?' Pilar laughed. 'That one doesn't have it in for anybody. That one hasn't got blood in his veins.'

I wasn't so sure.

Because there was something in the way he looked at me that wasn't cold. I didn't know what it was. But it wasn't cold.

## 3 · Ada

I don't answer her. I give her a kiss, tell her to fasten the shutters, and leave with the bag of broth pressed against my chest.

But the question comes with me. It sits in the passenger seat for the two and a half miles of single-track, with the wind shoving the car, and it stays there, perfectly calm, watching me drive.

*Are you still drawing that lad?*

Yes, Abuela. I am. Twenty-three years later, I still am.

---

I spend the afternoon getting the lighthouse ready for what's coming.

It's a routine I've learnt the hard way, winter by winter, even though I've only had one. Check the shutters one by one and close the ones facing west. Haul firewood up from the shed and stack it by the stove, all the way to the ceiling. Fill the tank of the generator, which is old and temperamental and coughs like a smoker before it starts. Bring in the chair from the porch, the pot of rosemary, the washing line.

I work with the wind against me, my hair a mess, my cheeks burning with cold and my hands full of splinters. I like it. There's something about getting a house ready for a storm that makes you feel the house is truly yours. That you're protecting it. That it's protecting you.

Before it gets dark I go down to the cove one last time.

Not to swim. The sea is already too rough, with waves reaching the rock where I leave my towel and pulling back, dragging stones with a sound like teeth. I go down only to look at it. To say goodbye to it calmly, the way you say goodbye to someone before a fight you know is going to happen.

I sit at the top of the path, on a flat stone, hugging my knees to my chest. The wind blows my hair into my face and makes my eyes water. Below, *a Vella* takes the blows of the waves, black and gleaming, unmoved, the way she's been taking them since before Stromness existed.

And then I see it. The other one.

A small rock, to the left of *a Vella*, half hidden in the foam. Round on top, flat, with a kind of step on one side, like a seat. I've been swimming here for a year and have never noticed it.

I freeze.

It's the rock. The one from my first drawing, when I was nine. The rock where the boy sat to look at the sea. With its round shape and its step, identical, as if I'd traced it.

It can't be. At nine I had never been out to Breckness. Holidays at my grandmother's were down by the harbour, or at Warebeth, on the town side of the headland. I had never climbed up to the lighthouse. I had never come down to this cove.

I tell myself all rocks look alike. That my memory is playing tricks on me, the way it always does. That I'm cold and tired and have spent two days thinking about him too much.

I tell myself that several times as I climb back up to the lighthouse, with the wind pushing at my back.

I don't turn round to look at it. I'm afraid it will still look the same.

By the time I finish, it's night. I have a shower, put on my flannel pyjamas and my thick socks, and heat up a bowl of my grandmother's broth. It tastes of turnip greens, of potato, of Sundays when I was little. I drink it standing by the window, both hands round the bowl, looking at the dark.

You can't see anything. You can only hear it. The sea has changed its voice. It isn't breathing any more. It's roaring.

At nine Lucía video-calls me, as she promised yesterday. She appears on the screen with her hair up in a chaotic bun, a glass of wine in her hand and her little boy asleep on the sofa behind her, mouth open.

'Let's see this lighthouse,' she says. 'Show me. I want to see if it's still standing.'

I give her a tour with my phone. The stove, the firewood stacked to the ceiling, my grandmother's candles lined up on the table like soldiers, the black window.

'You look like a Brontë novel,' she says. 'All you need is a dark gentleman knocking on the door in the middle of the storm.'

I laugh. I laugh a lot, more than the joke deserves, and Lucía looks at me through the screen with narrowed eyes.

'What?'

'Nothing. I wish.'

'You wish what? You wish for a dark gentleman?' She leans so close to the camera that all I can see is her nose. 'Ada Koch. Is there someone? Some sailor? Some scallop diver with arms like this?'

'There's no one, Lucía. There's a generator that coughs and a grandmother who sends me broth.'

'Well, that's a shame.' She leans back on the sofa and takes a sip from her glass. 'When you look so pretty with your hair long.'

I go quiet for a second. Nobody has told me I look pretty in a long time. Not even me.

We talk a while longer. About work, about Marcos and his checked shirt, about her son's school, about a series I absolutely have to watch. Small things, warm things, the kind that remind you there's a normal life somewhere, waiting for you.

Before hanging up, she turns serious.

'Promise me something. If the power goes off tomorrow, don't play the hero. If you're scared, go to your grandmother's.'

'I promise.'

We both know I'm not going anywhere.

---

I can't sleep.

I try. I get into bed at eleven with a book I don't read and turn off the light at twelve. At one I'm still lying there with my eyes open, listening to the wind try the windows one by one, like someone looking for the right key on a ring.

My phone is on the bedside table, face down. I haven't looked at it since yesterday afternoon. I haven't answered. I'm not going to answer.

At half past one I give up. I pull the thick sweatshirt on over my pyjamas, go down to the kitchen, switch on the small lamp and take out the sketchbook.

Drawing is the only thing that truly calms me. More than swimming. More than breathing and counting the way the psychologist taught me. When I draw, there's no past and no future. Only the line I'm making, and the next one, and the next. It's like swimming in the October sea, but without the cold.

I open the sketchbook to a blank page. Not a corner, not a margin. A whole page, clean, the kind I usually save for the important clouds.

And I start to draw him.

All of him. For the first time in twenty-three years.

---

The first time I drew him I was nine.

My parents were separating. I didn't really understand what was happening, but I understood the raised voices behind the kitchen door and the silences at breakfast, which were worse. I spent my afternoons shut in my room, drawing, because it was the only thing I could do without anyone asking me to choose.

One afternoon I drew a boy. A boy my age, with dark, tousled hair, sitting on a rock, looking at the sea. I'd never really seen the sea except on holiday, at my grandmother's. But I drew it from memory, with its little waves and its straight horizon.

And when I finished, I thought: this one stays.

Just like that, in those words. This one stays. Not like my father, who left that winter to live in Vigo with a suitcase and a box of records. Not like my mother, who left without moving, shut up in her sadness as if in a room with no windows.

This one stays.

From then on I drew him in every sketchbook. He grew up with me, without my planning it. At eleven I gave him a scar on his left eyebrow, because the boys in adventure books always had one. I thought it was the most romantic thing in the world. At thirteen I rubbed it out, embarrassed, and never gave it back to him.

At sixteen I drew him for the first time as a boy I liked. With his lips parted, looking at me. I spent a whole afternoon on that drawing, my heart beating in my ears. And then I tore out the page and ripped it into tiny pieces, red with shame, as if someone could see inside me.

Then came the real boyfriends. The ones from university, the ones from Madrid. I fell in love, fell out of love, cried, forgot. And he stayed there, in the margins, without complaint. Waiting. He never needed anything. He never asked me for anything. He was just there.

Only once did I try to show him to someone.

I was twenty and head over heels for a boy in my faculty who played guitar at parties and wrote me bad poems on napkins. One night, in his student flat, I showed him my sketchbooks. The clouds first. And then, with my heart in my mouth, a page where he was, sitting on a jetty, looking at the water.

'And who's this?' he asked, laughing. 'Your imaginary boyfriend?'

He didn't mean it badly. I know that. He was a sweet, clumsy boy who didn't know what he was holding. But I shut the sketchbook so fast I caught my finger, and I never showed it to him again. Not to him or anyone.

Since then the sketchbooks have lived in a box at the back of the wardrobe, like love letters you never send.

I never gave him a name. Giving him a name felt like too much. Like admitting something I didn't want to admit even to myself.

---

Tonight I draw him standing, his hands in the pockets of a dark jacket. His hair a little long, wet, stuck to his forehead, as if he's just come in from the rain. His weight on one leg, tired, like a man who has walked a long way to get here.

It takes me hours. The wind is battering the house and I don't hear it.

I draw the collar of his shirt, a little open. The line of his collarbone, just visible underneath. I draw his shoulders, broad but not too broad, the kind you imagine resting your head on. I draw his hands, which are always hard for me, which never come out right. Tonight they come out right. Big, long-fingered, with prominent knuckles. The hands of someone who knows how to do things.

I look at them for a long time. I wonder what they would feel like on my skin. Whether they'd be rough or gentle. Whether they'd be slow.

I feel my cheeks burning and laugh to myself, softly, in my kitchen, at three in the morning. How silly. A grown woman blushing over a pair of graphite hands.

But I keep going.

I go back to the face. To the mouth.

The mouth is the one thing I haven't liked for years. It always comes out too serious, with a hard line at the corner, like a man waiting for you to explain yourself. I don't know where that line comes from. It just comes out that way.

Tonight, at last, I understand what's wrong with it. I don't want a serious man. I don't want a man who's waiting for explanations.

I want one who's about to smile at me.

I rub out the hard line very carefully, without tearing the paper. And I draw it again, softer. With the corner lifted a little, as if he's about to smile and doesn't quite dare yet. As if he's seen me walk in and is glad, but doesn't want it to show too much.

Like that.

I set down the pencil and look at him.

It's him. It's him, the one he's always been. The boy on the rock and the boy at sixteen and the man of now. The one who stays. He looks at me from the paper with those dark, patient eyes, with that smile about to be born, and I feel something I haven't felt in a long time.

I feel that someone is waiting for me.

I run my fingertip along the edge of his jaw. Barely touching the graphite, so I won't smudge it. It's a silly gesture. A little girl's gesture. But the paper is warm from the lamp, and for a second, just for a second, it feels like skin.

I pull my hand away.

I close the sketchbook. I leave both hands on its black covers, as if I were keeping in something that might escape.

It's half past three in the morning. Outside, the wind isn't whistling any more. It's roaring. I look out of the window and see that the rain has started falling sideways, almost horizontal, hitting the glass like handfuls of gravel.

The model said midday tomorrow.

The storm has come early.

### Maruxa · Stuttgart, June 1966

In June I stopped crying at night.

It didn't happen all at once. It was like getting over a cold, when one day you realise you haven't coughed in a while. One morning I got up, washed my face at the basin in the corridor in icy water, and thought that I hadn't cried that night. Or the night before. Or the one before that.

I don't know if it was because I got used to it or because I got tired of crying. I think both.

In June I also learnt to say *Feuerbach* without them laughing at me in the bakery. And I learnt that if you asked for the black bread cut in thin slices and put butter and sugar on it, it almost tasted like something. And that the Neckar, on Sunday afternoons, filled up with German families out walking with their children, and that if you sat on the grass by the bank and closed your eyes, the sound of the water was a little like, just a little like, the sound of the ría.

I went almost every Sunday. Alone, because Pilar went with her boyfriend and Fátima stayed in writing letters to Lisbon.

I sat on the grass and looked at the water. Sometimes I sang. Softly, to myself.

---

He was still watching me.

It didn't frighten me so much any more, but it made me nervous in a different way. It was like having a pebble in your shoe that doesn't hurt but that you feel all the time.

I had started noticing things. That he always passed behind my station twice a shift, never three times, never once. That when he passed, he slowed down. That he never wrote anything on his clipboard when he was near me, only afterwards, at the far end of the floor, with his head bent over the paper, very focused.

One afternoon he came closer than ever. I had a part in my hand, one of those with a crooked hole that had to be thrown out, and I didn't realise he was behind me until I saw his shadow fall across the belt.

I went still. With the part in my hand. Not breathing.

I felt the heat of his body at my back. He smelt of soap, good soap, not the kind from the residence, and of something metallic, like everyone in the factory. He was so close I could hear his breathing over the noise of the machines.

He bent down a little. He reached over my shoulder, a big hand, long-fingered, the nails cut very short, and took the part from my fingers very carefully. Without touching me. Not even a brush.

He held it up to the light. He nodded. He dropped it in the bin for the bad parts.

And then he did something I had never seen him do.

He looked me in the eyes. Straight at me. Without looking down or writing anything.

It was a second. Maybe less. But in that second I saw something in his grey eyes that left my legs weak. Something like a question he didn't dare ask.

Then he turned and went, with his grey coat and his clipboard, to the far end of the factory floor.

I fixed my eyes on the belt, my heart racing, and let four parts go by without checking them. One of them, most likely, with a crooked hole.

---

That night I couldn't sleep.

Fátima was snoring softly. Pilar had gone out with her boyfriend and hadn't come back. The window facing the wall was open because of the heat, and through it came the noise of the goods trains passing on the tracks, long, so slow, as if they were never going to end.

I thought about his hands. About how he'd taken the part from me without brushing against me.

I thought that he had been careful not to touch me. Very careful. Too careful for someone who didn't care.

I turned over in bed and covered my face with the sheet. It was burning.

What a silly girl you were, Maruxa! Nineteen years old, fifteen hundred kilometres from home, without a word of German, and thinking about the hands of a foreman who didn't even know your name.

Because he didn't. At the factory everyone called me María. That was what it said on my file, on my contract, on the card we punched when we came in. *María Fernández Lago.* Nobody in all of Germany knew I was Maruxa.

I thought that if one day someone called me that, by my real name, in that cold country, I would burst into tears.

I didn't know yet that I would be right.

## 4 · Ada

At seven in the morning the power goes off.

It doesn't surprise me. In Orkney the power goes off at any excuse, and this is no ordinary excuse. I wake to the strange silence the fridge leaves when it shuts off, that hole in the background noise you didn't know was there until it disappears.

I feel about on the bedside table for the torch. I go downstairs barefoot, the cold of the tiles biting my feet, and go to the back room to start the generator.

It coughs once. Twice. On the third try it starts with the roar of an old engine, and the kitchen bulbs flicker and come on. I give its casing a little pat, like a dog that's been good.

I've got diesel for two days, three if I don't switch on the monitors.

I switch on the monitors.

I'm a meteorologist. I've got the biggest low of the year right over my head, and since the small hours it has a Met Office name: Storm Ailsa. I'm not going to miss it to save diesel.

---

The barometer on the wall, the old needle one that came with the house, shows a pressure I've never seen on it. It has dropped thirty-two millibars in twenty-four hours. At this latitude, for a low to count as explosive, nineteen is enough.

It's a monster. A beautiful monster.

I open the satellite image and lose my breath. The centre is a hundred and fifty kilometres to the north-west, spinning on itself like water going down a drain. It's shaped like a comma, like a seashell, with its tail of cloud curling inward in a perfect spiral. It glows white over the dark sea.

If I didn't know what it was, it would seem the most beautiful thing I've ever seen in my life.

I know. And it seems that way anyway.

It's something I've never told anyone. That storms move me. That when I see one of these on the screen I feel a lurch in my stomach like the one you feel when you like someone. A mix of fear and wanting. Of wanting to get closer and knowing you shouldn't.

I send the morning report. Gusts of a hundred and forty kilometres per hour on the coast. Twelve-metre waves. Recommendation: stop everything. Ports closed, turbines stopped, boats moored.

At ten past nine the internet goes down.

At quarter past nine I try to ring my grandmother and my phone has no signal. Not a single bar. The mast on the hill has gone with the wind, or with the power, or with both.

I sit looking at the screen. *No service.*

I feel strangely light. As if someone has closed a door that's been open for days, letting in a draught.

---

I spend the morning doing small things with my hands.

I fill jugs with water in case the pump fails. I set my grandmother's candles on the table, next to the box of matches. I check the shutters again, one by one, with the wind pinning me against the wall every time I put my head out of the door. The rain soaks me in ten seconds.

At midday I climb the tower.

It's a hundred and twelve spiral steps, stone worn down in the middle by a hundred years of lighthouse keepers going up and down. I climb them with my hand on the cold wall. The higher I go, the louder the noise grows. The wind whistles through the cracks in the lantern, at the very top, like an out-of-tune organ.

Up here, the lamp is still under its canvas cover, tied down with ropes. I check the knots. They're fine.

Then I go to the glass and look at the sea.

It isn't blue. It isn't grey. It's white.

All white, foam from end to end, as far as I can see, as if the whole ocean were boiling. The waves come in rows, enormous, slow, and break against the cliff with a dull blow that gets into my chest and makes my bones vibrate. The water runs up the rock thirty, forty metres, and falls on the path like salt rain.

The tower is trembling. A little. Just enough to feel it in the soles of my feet.

I hold on to the iron railing and look until I'm cold to the bone. I'm afraid, of course. But it's a clean fear. A fear I understand. The sea is dangerous because it's the sea. It doesn't lie. It doesn't pretend to be something else.

I think of the sailors who, for a hundred years, looked for this tower's light on nights like this. Of their wives waiting at the harbour. Of the lamp turning and turning, telling them not this way, not this way, here's the rock.

Now nobody looks for it. And I'm up here, alone, watching the storm from inside a lamp that's out.

Before I go down I do something I haven't done in a year.

I untie one of the ropes of the cover and lift the canvas by a corner.

Underneath is the lens. I've never seen it up close. It's huge, taller than me, a glass beehive made of stacked rings and prisms, hundreds of pieces cut by hand surrounding an empty space in the centre, where the light used to be. Even though nothing is lit, the glass gathers the little grey daylight of the storm and gives it back multiplied, in reflections that tremble on my hands and on my face.

I put my palm on one of the rings. It's ice-cold. Smooth as ice, as still water.

I think it's the most beautiful thing I've ever touched. I think it has been waiting a hundred years for someone to put something inside it.

I lower the canvas again carefully and tie the rope with a double knot. My fingers are trembling a little, I don't know if from the cold.

I'll never tell anyone, but this afternoon, going down the hundred and twelve steps, I make myself a promise. That one day I'll light it. Not for the boats. For me.

I don't know yet how far away that day is.

---

At five in the afternoon it gets dark.

I switch off the monitors to save diesel. I light the stove and two candles. I heat up the rest of my grandmother's broth and have it on the sofa, wrapped in the checked blanket, my feet tucked under the cushion.

The noise is constant. Wind, sea, wind. Things banging outside and I don't know what they are. A shutter has come loose somewhere and starts banging, one, two, one, two, like a heart out of rhythm.

I don't get up to fasten it. I don't dare open the door.

I've got the sketchbook on my knees, closed. I don't open it. I'm a little embarrassed to open it, after last night. Like when you've sent someone a message that was too honest and the next day you don't dare look at your phone.

So I don't move. I listen.

At one point I think I hear a voice. Outside, between the gusts. A man's voice, calling something. I sit up, my heart in my throat, and hold my breath.

Wind. Sea. The loose shutter.

Nothing else.

It happens to me a lot. I hear things and then I don't know if I heard them. I see something move out of the corner of my eye and when I turn my head there's nothing. My mother used to say I had too much imagination. I suppose she was right. I suppose that's why I draw.

I lie back again. I pull the blanket up to my chin.

To keep from thinking, I take out my phone and listen again to the voice messages my grandmother sent this week. I've got no signal, but the messages are still there, saved. Her voice fills the kitchen, small and tinny through the speaker. *Peedie lass, I made* filloas *today. Peedie lass, I ran into Mrs Flett and she asked after you. Peedie lass, don't go swimming, I know you.*

I listen to all of them, one after another, with my eyes closed. Then I start again.

When they run out, the kitchen is emptier than before.

I wonder what I'd do if someone really did knock. I think about it seriously, like a game, to distract myself. If it was a lost fisherman, I'd give him broth and let him have the sofa. If it was a hillwalker, I'd lend him dry clothes and scold him for not checking the forecast. If it was someone handsome—and here I laugh to myself, softly, in the dark—if it was someone handsome, I'd comb my hair before I opened the door.

I touch my hair. It's a mess, frizzy from the damp, with a strand stuck to my cheek. I tuck it behind my ear.

How ridiculous. Nobody's going to come. Nobody climbs up to a lighthouse in the middle of an explosive cyclogenesis.

But I leave it behind my ear anyway.

---

I don't know how much time goes by. An hour. Two. The kitchen clock stopped when the power went, its hands stuck at two minutes past seven, and I don't feel like looking at my phone.

The candles burn down. The stove does too. I don't get up to add wood.

I think of my grandmother, alone in her house with the blue windows. I picture her sitting in her kitchen with the battery radio, praying softly, or not praying, or singing without realising it the way she used to sing in the factory in Stuttgart.

I think of my grandfather, who one day knocked on my grandmother's door in a way she has never wanted to tell me about.

It was he who taught me the clouds.

I was eight and spending the summer in Stromness. One afternoon he took me to the end of the breakwater, sat me beside him with my legs dangling over the water, and pointed at the sky with the stem of his pipe.

'*Schäfchenwolken*,' he said. 'Little sheep clouds. Look. They look like a flock, see? Tomorrow the weather will change.'

And the next day it changed.

From that summer on, every afternoon, he taught me a new cloud. The cumulus, which look like cauliflowers and mean good weather. The cirrus, which are angel hair and warn you something is coming. The cumulonimbus, which grow like towers and bring storms. He told me the names in German and in Latin, and I repeated them, and he gave me a mint for every one I got right.

He never knew I became a meteorologist because of him. Or maybe he did. When I finished my degree, he sent me a postcard from Stromness with a photo of the lighthouse and a single sentence written on the back, in his German handwriting, all spikes: *Für mein Wolkenmädchen.* For my cloud girl.

I've got it stuck on the fridge. It's gone dark, like everything else, but I know it's there.

I wonder what cloud tonight would have been. I wonder if he'd have told me to be afraid, or to look.

I think he'd have told me to look.

I think that I've never had that. That I've had other things. Loves that leave your heart in pieces and loves that leave your head full of doubts. But never that. Never someone who would cross half of Europe just to stay.

I hug the sketchbook to my chest, without opening it, as if it were a teddy bear. Ridiculous. A thirty-two-year-old woman hugging a sketchbook in the middle of a storm.

But I don't let go.

I think, for the first time in a long time, that I'm very alone. Not alone the way I like to be, with the sea and my sketchbooks and my silence. Truly alone. Alone the way you are in a small boat in the middle of the night.

And I want, want terribly, for someone to knock on the door.

Then, between one gust and the next, I hear it.

It isn't the shutter. It isn't the sea. It isn't my imagination.

It's three sharp knocks on the door.

### Maruxa · Stuttgart, December 1966

The first Christmas it snowed.

I had never seen it really snow. In Galicia, on the coast, snow is something that happens in the mountains, far away, on postcards. So when I got up on the morning of the twenty-fourth and saw the wall outside the window white, and the sky white, and enormous flakes falling slowly, without a sound, like feathers from a burst pillow, I stood for a long while with my nose pressed to the glass.

It was the most beautiful thing I had ever seen in my life. And I was alone to see it.

Fátima had gone to Lisbon to spend the holidays with her daughters. She had saved all year for the ticket. I watched her pack her suitcase for a whole week, folding and unfolding the same clothes, and the afternoon she left she kissed me on each cheek and left in my hand a medal of Our Lady of Fátima, tiny, made of brass.

'So you're not alone,' she said. It was the longest sentence I heard her say all year.

Pilar went to dinner at the house of some people from her boyfriend's village, on the other side of the city. She invited me to go. I told her no, I had a headache. Nothing hurt. I just didn't want to be the one too many at somebody else's table.

And I had no money for the ticket. Everything I earned I sent home, except just enough to eat. My father needed a new engine for the boat. My sister needed shoes for school. My mother needed not to worry.

So I stayed in the empty residence. Me and three Turkish girls from the end of the corridor who didn't celebrate Christmas and who looked at me with curiosity when I passed them in the bathroom.

---

I spent Christmas Eve sitting on my bed, with my coat on because the heating in the residence went off at ten, and with a candle I had bought at the church in Feuerbach.

For supper I had black bread with butter and sugar. And an orange Fátima had saved for me.

I peeled the orange very slowly, the way my mother did, all in one go, without breaking the peel, so it stayed whole like a flower. The smell filled the room. It smelt of home. Of Christmas Eve in the kitchen with the wood stove, my father cracking walnuts with his fingers and my sister asleep on the bench and my mother singing while she put the salt cod to soak.

And then I cried. For the first time since June.

I cried as much as I wanted, without biting the pillow, because there was nobody to hear me. I cried for my mother and for the *empanada* and for the sea. I cried for the snow, which was so beautiful and which I had no one to show.

I took the pencil out of his hand. Without thinking.

And as I took it, my fingers brushed his.

We both went still. His hand was icy on the outside, like mine. But underneath, when my fingers stayed a second too long on his wrist, I felt his pulse. Fast. Very fast. Like a bird's when you hold it in your hand.

He wasn't made of stone. Pilar was wrong. He had so much blood in his veins it was pounding.

I took my hand away. It was trembling a little. I crossed out *corason* and wrote beside it, in my round convent-school hand: *corazón*.

He looked at the word for a long time. Then he repeated it softly, with his accent of rolling stones.

'*Corazón.*'

'*Corazón*,' I repeated.

And we both looked at the river, very red in the face, without saying anything more, for a long time. The water went by, grey, with its plates of ice. Night fell. Neither of us was in a hurry.

That night, at the residence, Pilar was waiting for me, sitting on her bed with her arms crossed and a grin from ear to ear.

'And that scarf?'

'It's mine.'

'It's yours. Of course. And I'm the Queen of England.'

I threw myself face down on the bed, my face in the pillow, so she wouldn't see me. But I was laughing. Laughing on my own, unable to stop, with the scarf still on and its smell of soap filling my nose.

Fátima looked up from her letter to Lisbon. She looked at me for a long while, over her glasses. Quiet, as always.

But before turning out the light she came over to my bed, kissed me on the forehead, the way she kissed the photos of her daughters, and whispered a single word in Portuguese.

'*Finalmente.*'

I didn't understand it until many years later. Until I, too, had a granddaughter whose face I saw light up for the first time.

I never gave back the scarf. Not that day, not ever.

I still have it. It's in the wardrobe in my room, folded, on the top shelf. It stopped smelling of him many years ago. But some nights I take it out and put it around my neck anyway, just in case.

## 7 · Ada

When some light comes in through the crack in the shutter, grey and dirty, I can't take it any more.

I open the sketchbook.

My heart is beating in my fingertips as I turn the pages. Clouds. Clouds. An August cumulonimbus. And then the full page, the one from Tuesday night.

There he is.

Standing, hands in the pockets of the dark jacket. Wet hair over his forehead. His weight on one leg. His mouth about to smile.

'It's my house.'

'Right.'

'Coffee.'

I hide behind my cup.

Then I sit across from him, like last night. I put the sketchbook on the table, between us. Closed.

And then I do the only thing I can think of. What any scientist would do.

Compare.

---

I open the sketchbook to Tuesday's page and turn it towards the light. I look at him. At the drawing. At him.

It's the same face. Not similar. The same.

The same jaw, which took me years to find. The same nose, a little crooked to the left, because at fourteen I decided a perfect nose was boring. The same cowlick, right above his forehead, that makes one lock of hair always stick up on the same side.

I reach out without thinking and smooth it down. The lock springs back up.

He doesn't move. He only closes his eyes for an instant when I brush his forehead, a very short instant, as if my fingers had hurt him, or the other way round.

I pull my hand back. Clear my throat.

'Turn your face to the left,' I say.

He does it without asking.

I look for his eyebrow.

At eleven I drew him a scar right there, on his left eyebrow. A thin white line that split it in two. I drew it in every sketchbook for two years, proud of it. At thirteen it seemed like a little-girl thing and I rubbed it out. I never gave it back to him.

His eyebrow is whole. Not a mark. Not a shadow.

I don't know if that relieves me or scares me more.

Now the mouth.

On Tuesday night I rubbed out the hard line at the corner, the one that had been coming out on its own for years. And I drew it again, softer, about to smile.

I look at his mouth. Slowly. Longer than I should.

It's the new one.

Not the one from the last ten years. Not the one from all the sketchbooks I keep in the box in the wardrobe. The one from that night. The one I drew a few hours before someone knocked on my door.

I have to grab the edge of the table.

---

'What's wrong?' he asks.

I don't answer. I can't. I get up, go to my room and come back with the cardboard box from the wardrobe. I set it on the table, between the cups, and open it.

Twenty-three years of black sketchbooks. They smell of old paper, of graphite, of the damp in the wardrobe.

He looks at them with an expression I can't read.

I start taking them out and flipping through them fast, going backwards. Him at twenty, sitting on a jetty. Him at sixteen, in profile. Him with the scar, at twelve, with a wooden sword that makes me laugh in spite of everything. Him without the scar. Him at nine, the very first, sitting on his rock, looking at the sea.

Each sketchbook holds a period of my life. I see it there, with the sketchbooks open on the table like cards from a pack. The one from the year of my university entrance exams, full of nervous clouds and of him studying next to me, with an open book I never finished drawing. The one from Hamburg, the Erasmus winter, with him walking through the snow by the river, his jacket collar turned up, because I was so cold and so lonely that I drew him with me. The one from Madrid, the first year, when everything was lovely, with him almost hidden, tiny, in the corners, as if he were embarrassed to be there.

And then, the one from Madrid in the last few years.

Almost empty. Clouds without him. Whole pages without a single margin filled. It surprises me to see it like that, so clearly, like a gap in a row of teeth. I don't remember stopping drawing him. I don't know when it happened.

I turn those pages fast.

He says nothing. But when I look up, he's looking at me, not at the sketchbooks. And there's something in his eyes that looks a lot like sorrow.

A knot forms in my throat.

And then I remember the mole.

The small mole below his left shoulder, the one I saw last night when he took off his wet shirt.

I look for it. I search through every sketchbook, page by page, for more than an hour, while he watches me in silence and the coffee goes cold in the cups. I search the backs, the shoulders, the quick sketches in the margins.

I don't find it.

I tell myself I must have missed it. That in twenty-three years of drawings my pencil must have slipped at some point. That no one remembers a dot of graphite.

I tell myself that several times. I don't believe it.

'Can I see it again?' I ask him. My face is burning. 'The mole. In the light.'

He doesn't ask why. He stands up, turns round and pulls the fisherman's jumper up his back, slowly, to his shoulder blades.

The grey light from the window falls on his skin. It's a real back. With pores, with fine pale hair at the base of the spine, with a smaller freckle near the waist that I don't remember either. And there, below the left shoulder, the mole. Dark, round, the size of a lentil.

I move closer. Reach out a finger. Touch it.

It isn't graphite. It doesn't smudge. It's skin, warm, alive. Under my fingertip I feel goosebumps rise across his whole back, from the nape of his neck to his waist, in a small wave that runs over his skin the way wind runs over water.

He holds his breath. So do I.

We stay like that for a second. Two. My finger on his back and the whole world silent, even the wind.

Then I take my hand away and he pulls the jumper down without a word, and sits down again, and I sit down again, and the two of us look at the sketchbooks instead of at each other.

My hands are shaking. I hide them under the table.

---

I pick up Tuesday's sketchbook again. I open it to the first page, without thinking.

There it is, in my usual handwriting, the same as in every sketchbook since I was a girl:

*This sketchbook belongs to Ada Koch. If you find it, please give it back to me.*

My grandmother taught me to write it, because when I was little I lost them everywhere. On the bus, at the beach, in the school canteen. Ever since, I write it in all of them.

I look at my name.

*Do you know who I am?*

*Ada.*

He didn't guess it. It wasn't magic, or not that kind of magic. It wasn't a trick.

He knows it the way he knows the shape of his nose. The way he knows his eyebrow without the scar. Because it's written in the same place he comes from.

---

'Tell me what you remember,' I ask him. My voice comes out very low. 'Anything. The first thing you remember.'

He closes his eyes. He stays like that for a long time. Outside, the wind has turned into gentle rain, and you can hear it falling on the roof like sand.

'A rock,' he says at last. 'I'm sitting on a rock. In front of me is the sea.'

My breath catches.

'What else?'

'Clouds. Lots of clouds.' He opens his eyes and looks at me, a little lost. 'There are a lot of clouds, aren't there? Everywhere.'

I almost laugh. I almost cry. I do a little of both, at the same time, covering my mouth with my hand.

'Anything else?'

He hesitates. He looks down at his own hands, open on the table.

'A hand,' he says. 'A hand that makes me. That makes me slowly. Sometimes it stops for a long time, and I wait. Sometimes it rubs me out a little, and it hurts, but then it comes back.'

He raises his eyes to me.

'It's your hand, isn't it?'

I can't answer. I sit there looking at him through blurred eyes, my hand still covering my mouth.

Because I understand.

He doesn't know where he comes from because I've never drawn where he comes from. He doesn't know his age because I've never decided it. He has no name because I've never given him one.

He knows what's in the sketchbook. A rock. The sea. Clouds, heaps of clouds. My name on the first page.

And my hand.

He only knows what I've drawn.

He is what I've drawn.

### Maruxa · Stuttgart, February 1967

For a month we saw each other every afternoon. By the river, if it wasn't raining. If it was, in a little café in Feuerbach with crocheted curtains and a fat owner who looked at us as if she knew things.

We understood each other through the oilcloth notebook. He wrote a word in German, I wrote it in Spanish. I drew a boat, he wrote *Boot*. I taught him Galician words that weren't in any dictionary, *morriña*, *saudade*, *orballo*, and he wrote them down very carefully, in his spiky handwriting, and then repeated them so badly that my belly ached from laughing.

The notebook filled up. It was ours. It was the only house we had.

But there was a part he never showed me.

The last pages. Whenever I turned the leaves towards the end, he put his hand on top, gently, and closed it. Without getting angry. He just closed it and changed the subject, with that red face he would get.

I didn't insist. But I was dying of curiosity.

---

One afternoon in February he left the notebook on the café table to go to the toilet.

I shouldn't have done it. I know. My mother would have given me a smack.

I opened it at the end.

And it wasn't single words. It was songs.

My songs. Written in his spiky handwriting, the way they sounded, without understanding what they said. My mother's *alalás*. The chestnut song. My grandmother's lullaby. All written wrong, his way, the way a German hears Galician: *Ala la la, ala la la, kuando tsei lo mar.*

And under every song, a date.

The first was the fourteenth of April, 1966. My third week at the factory. I didn't even remember ever singing that song on the line.

I turned the pages with my hands shaking. April. May. June. Dates and dates and dates. Whole songs, others in pieces, some with only one line. Sometimes a note in the margin, in German, that I couldn't understand.

Nine months. Nine months of my songs, written down one by one, at the far end of the factory floor, his head bent over the paper. What I had thought were my black marks.

The last page was almost full. At the top was a date: *24.12.1966*.

And underneath, in a long list, all the songs I sang that Christmas Eve. All of them. In order. The carols, the *alalás*, my grandmother's lullaby.

At the end of the list there was a single sentence in German, written more slowly than the others, the letters rounder.

---

When he came back from the toilet he found me with the notebook open in my hands and my face covered in tears.

He stood beside the table. Very still. Red up to his hair.

I couldn't speak. I only pointed to the sentence.

He sat down. He looked at the sentence. He looked at me. And then, slowly, searching for each word in his head like stones at the bottom of a river, he translated it for me.

'She… sings…' He stopped. Searched. 'Sings for nobody. And I… am nobody. Tonight.'

I took his hand on top of the café table, in front of the fat owner and the two old men playing cards in the corner. I squeezed it so hard I left my nail marks on it.

He had been there. All along. While I felt like the loneliest woman in the world, crying with my arms round my pillow, singing at the top of my lungs in an empty corridor, someone had been listening. Someone writing down every note in a notebook, so it wouldn't be lost.

I have never again felt so accompanied as I did that afternoon, knowing I hadn't been alone for a single day.

Then I asked him about the notes in the margin. The ones in German, beside some of the songs.

He went red again. He turned the pages looking for one, and pointed to it. It was beside my grandmother's lullaby, written down in October.

'Here…' he said. 'Here you… sad. You sing sad.'

And the next one, beside an *alalá* from August.

'Here, happy. Letter from home, I think.'

It was true. In August I'd had a letter from my mother telling me my father finally had the new engine for the boat. I spent the whole day happy, not knowing it showed. Not knowing you could hear it.

He had written it all down. Not just my songs. How I was when I sang them. My good days and my bad days, one after another, with their dates, in a black oilcloth notebook, for nine months.

Someone had been reading me from the inside without my knowing.

I didn't feel afraid. Maybe I should have. A girl alone, far from home, and a man who knew things about her she hadn't told him.

But no. What I felt was something very different. It was like when I was little and got lost at the fair in Padrón and then found out my father had been following me the whole time, from a distance, without saying anything, letting me believe I was brave.

## 8 · Ada

The storm lasts all that day and a good part of the following night.

Later I won't remember the hours well. I'll remember loose things, like photos from a roll of film someone developed out of order.

I'll remember putting the sketchbooks back in the box, one by one, with a care I've never had before. As if each one were now a house with someone inside.

I'll remember lighting the stove with him watching me, very attentive, following every gesture with his eyes. How I open the little door. How I set the kindling underneath and the logs on top. How I blow. When I finish, he crouches beside me and watches the fire catch with the same look of wonder as last night.

'It burns,' he says.

'Yes. It burns.'

'You made it burn.'

He says it as if it were a miracle. I laugh, and he watches me laugh the way he watched the fire.

---

At midday I heat a tin of lentils on the hob, because I haven't the head to cook anything else. I serve them in two soup bowls and cut some bread. I sit across from him.

He holds the spoon the way I hold it. Just like me, with his index finger stretched along the handle, a habit I've had since I was a girl and that my mother tried to break me of for years. I wonder if he's learnt it by watching me just now or if I drew him that way at some point without realising.

He tastes the first spoonful and goes still. His eyes very wide.

'Is it bad?' I ask him.

'No.' He takes another spoonful. 'It's that I didn't know things had a taste.'

I have to look away. I've got a knot in my throat over a tin of lentils.

He likes the bread even more. He tears it with his hands, into small pieces, and eats them with his eyes closed. I give him one of the tangerines I bought in Stromness and he takes five minutes to peel it, removing every white thread with infinite patience, and when he puts the first segment in his mouth and the juice bursts inside, he laughs.

It's the first time I've heard him laugh.

It's a low sound, surprised, a little hoarse. It goes straight to my stomach.

I've never drawn his laugh. You can't draw a laugh. But if I could have, it would have sounded like that.

After lunch he goes over to the big window, the one that faces the sea, and stands there for a long time.

I've opened the shutter a little to let the light in. Outside, the storm is still going, but you can look at it now. The sky is a dirty grey, low, racing east at full speed. And beneath it, the sea. Still white, enormous, breaking against the cliff in explosions of spray that rise and rise and come apart in the air.

He rests the palm of his hand on the glass.

'I want to touch it,' he says.

'The sea? Don't even think about it. It would drag you off in two seconds.'

'But it's the sea.' He turns to look at me, and for the first time I see something like impatience in his face, like longing, a desire of his own. 'It's the same one as the rock. It is the same one, isn't it?'

I go over and stand beside him. So close that our arms brush through the wool. Neither of us moves away.

'It's the same one,' I tell him. 'When the storm passes, I'll take you to the cove. I'll teach you to swim.'

'Is it cold?'

'Very. Thirteen degrees.'

'And you like it?'

'I love it.'

He nods, very serious, as if he's just made an important decision.

'Then I'll like it.'

We stand watching the waves, shoulder to shoulder, without talking. Outside, a gull is fighting the wind, motionless in the air, beating its wings without gaining a yard. He follows it with his eyes until the wind tears it loose and carries it off.

I'm not watching the gull. I'm watching him watch it.

I think it's been a long time since I shared a window with anyone.

---

In the afternoon the internet comes back for a while, in fits and starts.

I turn on the generator and the monitors, more out of habit than anything. I need to do something normal. Something that makes sense, that has numbers.

The centre of the low is over land now, weakening. The worst is over. I send a short report, without thinking much. No one is going to read it anyway.

He stands behind me. At a prudent distance at first. Then a little closer. So close that I feel the warmth of his body on my back, through the jumper, and smell his smell. He smells of salt, of wet wool. And of something like paper.

I have to concentrate very hard on the screen.

'What's that?' he asks. His voice reaches me very close to my ear.

'The storm. Seen from above.'

'It's a spiral.'

'Yes.'

'It's beautiful.'

I turn to look at him. He says it without irony, with the same simplicity he says everything. His face is lit by the screen, and the low is reflected in his eyes, spinning.

'I think so too,' I admit. 'Don't tell anyone.'

'Who would I tell?'

I laugh. He doesn't. He asked it seriously.

I open the iterations. The fifty-one versions of the model, each with its own coloured line, all tangled over the map like strands of coloured wool a cat has been unravelling.

'And this?'

'They're versions of the same future.' I try to explain it without technical words. 'You give the computer today's data and ask it to calculate tomorrow's weather. But the data's never perfect. There's always a small error. So you run it fifty-one times, changing the data a tiny bit each time. And each time a different future comes out.'

'Fifty-one futures.'

'Fifty-one iterations. That's what I call them. Everyone else says ensemble members, which sounds like an orchestra. You look at them all together and see which ones are alike and which ones drift apart. That's how you know how much you can trust it.'

He stares at the lines for a long time. His brow a little furrowed. With that concentration of his that I'm beginning to know.

'And which one is the real one?'

'None of them,' I say. 'The real one is the one that happens. The others are just possibilities.'

He says nothing more. But I see he's thinking. And I know, without his telling me, what he's thinking about.

I'm thinking about it too.

---

That night I don't lock my room.

It isn't that I've decided to trust him. It's that I've understood something. If he's what I've drawn, he can only be what I've drawn. And never, not once in twenty-three years, have I drawn him hurting anyone.

I've drawn him waiting. Looking at the sea. Reading. Sleeping with his head resting on his arm. Smiling, or about to smile.

We sit by the stove, each in our own chair. Outside, the wind is starting to tire. The gusts are longer, farther apart, like the breathing of someone falling asleep. The sea is still roaring, but it isn't climbing the rocks as high any more.

Our knees almost touch. Almost.

'I have to call you something,' I tell him.

He looks at me. The fire paints his face orange and shadow.

'I can't keep saying "you". Or "him". I'm going mad.' I hear myself say it and laugh, a little. 'Madder.'

'What do you want to call me?'

I think about it.

I think of real names. Men's names, names from novels, the names of boys I fancied at school. None of them works. They're all too much. Giving him a real name means admitting he's real. That he's here. That this is happening.

And I'm not ready for that. Not yet.

I look at the dark computer screen, on the other side of the kitchen. The coloured lines are still in my head. Fifty-one futures. Fifty-one versions of what could happen.

And one more. The one no one has calculated. The one that isn't in any model.

'Iter,' I say.

'Iter?'

'From iteration.' I shrug, as if it doesn't matter. 'It's temporary.'

He repeats it softly, trying it out in his mouth the way he tried the lentils.

'Iter.'

And then he smiles.

Not all the way. Just the corner, lifted a little. As if he were about to smile and didn't dare yet.

The smile I drew two nights ago.

I look at it. I think I could spend my whole life looking at that smile. And it frightens me so much to have thought it that I get up to put wood on the fire, even though it doesn't need any.

When I sit down again, he's looking at the sketchbook, closed on the table.

'Will you draw me tomorrow?' he asks.

He says it without anxiety.

'I don't know,' I say, and it's true. 'It scares me.'

'Why?'

Because I don't know what I do to you when I draw you, I think. Because I don't know if I change you. Because I don't know if I rub you out a little every time I use the rubber. Because it scares me to have so much power over someone.

I don't tell him. I just shrug.

He looks at the fire for a while. Then he speaks quietly, without looking at me.

'When you don't draw me, I wait.' He pauses. 'I don't mind waiting. It's what I do best.'

I feel something break inside me. Something small and old, like a seam that has been pulled tight for years.

Twenty-three years in the margins. Waiting.

'You don't have to wait any more,' I tell him.

And I don't know if I'm promising it to him or to myself.

---

The day dawns clear.

That's what happens after the big lows. The air is left clean, cold, transparent as freshly washed glass. And the sea, still enormous, turns a blue so intense it doesn't look real.

I wake on the sofa. I don't remember falling asleep.

The tartan blanket is on top of me, tucked in snugly under my feet, the way my grandmother used to tuck it in when I was a girl. I didn't cover myself like that. I lie very still, my cheek against the cushion, and feel a warmth rise from my chest up to my face.

He covered me.

I imagine it. I can't help it.

Me asleep on the sofa, my mouth a little open, probably, and my hair a rat's nest. Him getting up from his chair in the dark, the stove almost out. Coming closer on tiptoe so as not to wake me. Picking the blanket up off the floor, where it must have fallen. Spreading it over me. Tucking it under my feet with those big hands I've drawn so many times and that now, for real, have been so close.

I wonder how long he stayed looking at me. If he looked at me the way I look at him on paper. If he thought I was pretty, or strange, or simply someone.

I cover my face with the blanket. It smells of wool, of wood smoke and, very faintly, of rain. Of him.

I stay like that for a whole minute, breathing in that smell, my heart doing things I haven't let it do in a long time.

Then I uncover myself, sit up and make myself look like a normal person.

Iter is by the window. He's opened the shutter and is looking out. The morning light falls full on his face and lights his dark hair with chestnut glints I've never drawn. That I didn't know he had.

My phone buzzes on the table. Once. Again. Again. The signal is back and all the messages are coming in, one after another, like birds returning to the tree after the storm.

Lucía, asking if I'm alive. Marcos, with a lighthouse emoji. Three voice messages from my grandmother.

And one more. From the number with no name.

*Ada. Sorry about the other day, that was no way to write to you after so long. I know you don't want anything to do with me and I understand. I saw the storm on the news. I just wanted to know you're OK. I won't bother you again.*

I read it three times.

It's him. The one from the first months. The one who remembered I don't take sugar and waited for me with an umbrella outside the metro. For a second I'm glad he remembers me. For the next second I hate myself for being glad.

I don't answer. I don't delete it either.

I listen to my grandmother's first voice message with the phone pressed to my ear. *Peedie lass, are you all right? Say something, I can't sleep. Miña rula, say something.*

I answer her with a text. My fingers are shaking a little.

*I'm fine, Abuela. It was long, but I'm fine. Alone and calm.*

I look at the word 'alone' for a long time before sending it.

Iter turns from the window. He looks at me, with the sun behind him, and says good morning in that hoarse voice I'm already starting to wait for.

And I decide, right then, that I'm not going to tell anyone. Not my grandmother. Not Lucía. Not the therapist. No one.

Not for his sake.

For mine. Because if I told someone and that someone looked at me the way you look at a madwoman, I'd start to believe it.

And I don't want him to disappear.

I press send.

### Maruxa · Stuttgart, March 1967

It was a year since I'd arrived in Germany, on a sunny Sunday.

### Maruxa · Lake Constance, July 1967

The hand.

It takes me a long time. Hands are always hard for me. And this time I can't make a mistake. The long fingers. The prominent knuckles. The shape of the thumb. I do it very slowly, while he keeps his other hand over mine and looks at his empty wrist.

It doesn't form all at once. It keeps appearing with the pencil. First an outline, like mist. Then the knuckles. Then the fingers, one by one, as I draw them, as if I were knitting them in the air.

When I finish, he closes it. Opens it. Moves his fingers.

Then he looks at it, front and back. For a long time.

And his face changes.

'It's gone,' he says.

'What?'

He turns his hand towards me. The back. The knuckles.

Clean. Perfect. Not a scratch.

The scar from the wire is gone. The little one, the one from the first day without me, the one from the shutter. So are the cuts from the rocks, from looking for me. And the blood.

It's the hand I drew. Only that.

'It was mine,' he says, looking at his clean knuckles. 'The scar. It was the first thing I'd made on my own.'

I don't know what to say. I've just given him back his hand and I've taken away the only thing in it that was his.

He goes on looking at the back of his hand for a long time. Then he closes it into a fist and presses it against his chest.

'It's all right,' he says. And I realise he's comforting me. 'I'll make others.'

Then he takes the pencil from me.

He takes it from my fingers gently, with the new hand. And he takes the sketchbook from me too. He closes it. He sets it on the floor, between the two of us. And he opens it at the end. At the last blank page.

'What are you doing?'

He doesn't answer.

He starts to draw.

I've never seen him draw. It's never occurred to me that he could. He holds the pencil wrong, gripping it too hard, like a child. His lines come out shaky, crooked, thick. He crosses things out. He starts again.

He looks at me. He draws. He looks at me.

It takes him half an hour. Outside, night falls. I turn on my phone's torch so he can see, and hold it over the paper with a trembling hand.

When he finishes, he turns the sketchbook towards me.

It's me.

Badly drawn. With a nose too big and one eye higher than the other. With hair like a storm cloud.

And laughing.

Laughing with my mouth open, my eyes closed, my head thrown back. A huge laugh, overflowing, the kind that doesn't fit on your face. The one from the cove, the day he sneezed. The one from Lucía's green face mask.

I've never seen myself like that.

In thirty-two years, in every photo, in every mirror, I've never seen myself like that. I didn't even know my face could do that.

'This is how I see you,' he says.

I tell him there, on the floor of the gallery, with the light from the phone between us.

Not everything. I can't tell him everything. Not yet.

I tell him yes. That I told the jumper-cables man. That there's someone in Madrid who wants to see me, and that it scares me, and that I don't know why. That I told a stranger in a kitchen that smelled of peat smoke because I hadn't drawn him, because he doesn't know me, because telling him something cost nothing.

And that with him, with Iter, it costs me everything.

He listens without interrupting. When I finish, he's quiet for a while.

'And what scares you?' he asks. 'Do you know?'

I shake my head. And it's true. And it isn't. There's something down there, far down, underneath everything, that I don't want to look at.

'Will you tell me someday?'

'Someday.'

He nods. He doesn't ask for more.

And I know, in that moment, that I've just made him another promise. And that this one is the hardest of the three.

---

We come down from the tower at ten at night, hand in hand. His, the new one, without a scar.

We don't turn on the light.

I don't know who kisses whom in the hallway. Both at once, I suppose. I suppose we're both late.

He pulls the other man's jumper over my head and lets it drop to the floor, and he doesn't look at it. I take off his salt-soaked T-shirt, still damp, and run my hands over his cold chest, his shoulders, his back, until I find the mole under his left shoulder. That one is still there.

It isn't mine. It never has been.

He leads me to my room without letting go of my mouth. We fall onto the bed any old how, laughing, bumping into each other, half-dressed. And then we stop laughing.

It's slow. It's clumsy. It's the first time for him and not for me, and yet I'm the one trembling. He touches me the way he has always looked at me: learning. As if my skin were a weather map and he had to read all of it. Every time I hold my breath, he stops, and looks at me, and waits. And then he goes on.

At some point, in the dark, I take his hand. The new one. I run my lips over the clean knuckles.

'You'll make others,' I tell him.

'I'm already making one,' he says, very low, against my neck.

And he doesn't tell me where.

### Maruxa · Stromness, Today

*Carallo* with a k. *Morriña* with two r's. *Bo día*, which he said *bodia*, all one word. The men laughed at him. He laughed with them, red in the face, and kept writing.

On the tenth day, the engine of my father's boat died in the middle of the *ría*.

The new engine. The one we had all paid for together, with the money I sent from Germany, with the overtime. It stopped with a clatter of iron and there was no way to start it. They towed it back to the harbour, and my father sat on the gunwale with his head in his hands.

Klaus went down to the quay. He looked at the engine. He looked at my father.

He took off his grey coat. He rolled up his shirtsleeves. He asked for a spanner with gestures.

Five years as a foreman on a line making engine parts. Five years checking crooked holes and burrs.

It took him two hours, with half the harbour watching. When he finished, he had grease up to his elbows and a black smear on his forehead.

The engine started first time.

My father stood looking at it for a long time. Then he took the packet of cigarettes out of his shirt pocket. He offered him one.

Klaus didn't smoke. He took it anyway.

'*Bo día*,' my father said to him. It was six in the evening.

'*Bodia*,' Klaus answered.

And the two of them sat on the gunwale and smoked, without saying another word, looking at the water.

From that day on, my father called him *o alemán*. Thirty years. Until he died. He never called him Klaus.

He said it the way you say *o meu fillo*.

## 29 · Ada

The day dawns clean. The way it always does after.

He's asleep on the sofa, the tartan blanket up to his chin and the three-day beard I drew on him. His mouth is slightly open. He snores once, softly, and turns over.

I've never drawn him snoring.

I sit on the floor beside him, my back against the sofa and a mug of coffee in my hands, and wait for him to wake up.

I've spent the whole night thinking about a name.

No. That's a lie. I've spent twenty-three years not wanting to think about a name. Last night I just stopped running.

---

He wakes up slowly. He opens one eye. He sees me. He opens the other.

'Hi,' he says, his voice hoarse with sleep.

'Hi.'

He sits up. He runs his hand over his face, over his beard. He looks at the back of his hand, the half-moon. He smiles.

I hand him the coffee. Weak.

He takes it. He tastes it. He looks at me over the mug.

'You look like you're about to say something.'

'I look like I'm about to say something.'

He sets the mug on the floor. He holds still. Waiting.

The way he always waits.

---

I've prepared a speech. I rehearsed it at four in the morning in front of the stove. The part about iterations, the part about the fifty-one futures, the part about how Iter was a word for something that repeats and he doesn't repeat.

None of it comes out.

'There's a mathematical figure,' I say. 'A fractal. It's called the Julia set. It depends on a number. You change that number a tiny bit, one little digit, and you get a completely different figure. Like the weather models. You can spend your whole life drawing it and it always surprises you.'

He's listening with his brow slightly furrowed. The way he listens to the clouds.

'It's the same with you,' I say. 'Change one little thing and you're someone else. I've spent a month trying to draw you and you surprise me every morning.'

I swallow.

'Julian.'

He goes very still.

And then I say what I haven't rehearsed. What comes out of a place I didn't know I had, the same place *you're not real* came out of, but from the other side.

'I'm not putting it on you. I'm giving it to you. The names you put on things are so they won't go away. This one is yours, so you can take it with you wherever you want to go.'

He doesn't say anything.

His eyes fill.

I've never seen him cry. I've never drawn him tears. Two fall, one from each eye, slowly, down into his beard.

'Julian,' he repeats.

Trying it. The way he tried the lentils, the coffee, the tangerine, the word *Iter*. But this time he doesn't try it in his mouth. He tries it in his chest. I see it.

'Julian.'

He puts his hand on his chest. In the centre. Where he once told me the thing I couldn't draw was.

'Then I'm staying,' he says.

'You don't have to—'

'I know.' He takes my hand. 'That's why I'm staying.'

---

At eleven there's a knock at the door.

Not three knocks. One. And a bark.

I open it.

The jumper-cables man, in the orange rain jacket, dry now, with a plastic bag in his hand. And Lúa beside him, sitting, very proper, her tongue hanging out.

'Low tide at nine,' he says, just like that. 'I went down to the rocks in your cove.'

He holds out the bag.

Inside there are pages. Loose pages, crumpled, soaked, stained with salt and seaweed. Seventeen; I'll count them later. Pages from the sketchbook that the wind tore out and the sea left among the stones with the tide.

A cloud. Another cloud. Him at twelve with the wooden sword. Him on the quay in Hamburg. My handwriting on the first page: *This sketchbook belongs to Ada Koch. If you find it, please give it back to me.*

He's done it. He's found it and given it back to me.

I can't speak.

'The man from Madrid spent the night at the police station in Kirkwall,' he says, looking at the sea, as if he were giving the forecast. 'Housebreaking. Your grandmother's already been in to make a statement about the car, and I did the one about the pole. What happened inside, only you can tell. You need to go to Kirkwall.'

'I'll go,' I say.

And I say it without clenching my teeth.

---

Julian appears behind me in the hall. Barefoot. In the fisherman's jumper.

Lúa stands up.

I tense. I wait for the growl. The raised hackles. The teeth.

Lúa looks at him. She stretches out her neck. She sniffs his feet, his knees, the hand he holds out to her slowly, palm up. The one with the mark.

And she flops onto the floor belly up, legs in the air, at his feet, her tail thumping against the tiles.

The jumper-cables man looks at his dog. Then he looks at Julian. For a long time.

I don't know what he understands. He doesn't tell me. I don't ask him.

Julian crouches down and scratches Lúa's belly. Then he stands up, looks the man in the eye, at the same height, and holds out his hand again. Like at the door, three days ago.

But this time he says something more.

'My name is Julian.'

It's the first time he has said it out loud to anyone but me.

The jumper-cables man shakes his hand.

'Thank you for pulling me out of the sea,' Julian says. 'What's your name?'

And the air goes out of me.

Because I've gone a month without asking it. Because he pulled me up from the bottom of the sea, carried me in his arms up a cliff, dressed me in his dead father's jumper, listened to me tell him what I hadn't told anyone, gave me a carpenter's pencil so I could bring another man back to life. And I haven't asked him. Not once.

Because asking would have been starting to want to know.

'Brais,' he says.

Just that. Nothing more. Looking at Julian, not at me.

Brais.

I wasn't the one to ask. Now I know anyway.

And I realise, with a small chill at the back of my neck, that I don't know what I'm going to do with that.

---

He goes off down the road with Lúa, on foot, without looking back.

Julian and I stay in the doorway, watching him go.

'He's a good person,' Julian says.

'Yes.'

'And he's free.'

He doesn't say it the way he did on the gallery, with that plain statement of fact that froze my blood. He says it differently. Like someone saying that he is too.

He takes my hand. The one with the mark, in mine.

'Wednesday,' he says.

'What's happening on Wednesday?'

'Your grandmother's making *empanada*.' He looks at me, very serious. 'For three.'

---

After lunch I sit in the car, in front of the lighthouse, with the keys in my hand. And I don't start it.

I unlock my phone. I go to blocked contacts. He's there. No name. Nine digits.

I think about the blue coat buttoned button by button on the pier. It was just like one his father used to have. His father, who used to leave. He didn't die: he left. He'd take the car one Tuesday and come back a month later, or three, with presents and an explanation that was never the same. He told me about it only once, in Madrid, in the small hours, as if he were confessing to a crime. That as a boy he used to memorise the timetables of everything, the trains, the meals, his mother's shifts, so that nothing would ever catch him by surprise again. That he bought the coat with his first pay packet, because it was the one his father was wearing the last time he came back.

Back then I thought that was tenderness. Later I thought it was something else. Now, in the car, with the phone in my hand, I think it's both. That we both lost a father. I started drawing someone who stayed. He learned to make sure nobody could leave.

And I remember the Perseids. That first August. I'd told him meteorologists don't look at the stars, because stars don't make weather. And he turned up at three in the morning with a flask of cocoa and a blanket and took me to a patch of waste ground outside Guadalajara to look at them anyway. He counted forty-two. I fell asleep at thirty. He woke me with a kiss on the forehead and told me he'd made forty-two wishes and every one of them was me.

That was true. That happened.

I loved him. For a while I loved him with everything I had.

I unblock him.

It's that easy. One finger. The conversation opens by itself, with his last message at the very bottom: *See you on the 12th.* And below it, my *No.* Two letters. All of a sudden they seem like very few.

I start typing.

*I'm sorry. It's all been so strange. I'm really on edge, I don't know what*

I stop. I read it. It's my old handwriting. The one for apologising for things I hadn't done, thumb moving fast, before he got angrier.

I delete it letter by letter.

I type it again, shorter. *Can we talk?*

And he'd answer in less than a minute, because he always answers in less than a minute. He'd say it was fine. That he took it all back. That he'd wait for me in Kirkwall. I see myself climbing the stairs to the flat on Calle Ibiza with my suitcase, putting in the key, which would still turn the same way, a little stiff at the end. The grey striped sheets. The dinners with his friends, where everyone laughed at his jokes and so did I, a second after the others. My mother would go back to ringing me on Sundays. My mother adored him. *A boy like that, Ada. Difficult as you are.*

My mother, who when my father left shut herself up in her sadness as if in a room with no windows and never came out again, because inside, at least, she knew where the walls were.

It would be so easy. Not because I want it. Because I know it by heart. Because there I knew where everything was, me included.

I look at myself in the rear-view mirror.

My face is puffy from not sleeping and my hair is still stiff with salt. And I have eyes I don't like. The eyes of someone working out how much it would cost her to give up. I look ugly. Not because of the dark circles. Because of that other thing. I see my mother. The same tight mouth. The same eyes doing sums.

I hold my finger over *send* for a long time.

Then I think of him crouched in the grass, looking at the palm of his hand as if it belonged to someone else. Three years at my side knowing I drew, and never knowing whom.

That man didn't know who I was. And I've come close to not knowing it again either.

I delete *Can we talk?* I block him again. I put the phone in the glove compartment. I start the car.

On the way, without quite deciding it, I know what I'll answer if anyone asks me what he is to me.

---

In the afternoon I drive to Kirkwall to sign.

I go alone. Julian offered to come and I said no. Not because I didn't want him there. Because this is something I have to do myself.

The station smells of vending-machine coffee and paper. A young constable, with glasses, sits me down in front of an old computer and reads aloud to me what they already have: my grandmother, who saw the grey car going up the hill; Brais, who found it stuck by the pole. Then he asks for mine. I tell him. The three knocks. The door. A sketchbook thrown into the sea from the cliff. He types it all with two fingers.

'Now I need your details,' he says. 'And those of the accused. Full name.'

I open my mouth. Nothing comes out.

I haven't said it in months. Not out loud, not inside. I've erased it from my contacts, from my photos, from my emails. I promised myself never to write it. Not even here. I wasn't going to give him even that.

And now a twenty-five-year-old lad is asking me for it with his finger over the key.

I think of Julian this morning, his hand on his chest, trying on his name the way you try on a new coat. I think of Brais, saying his without looking at me. Names are given. Names are received.

And some, I realise, are given back.

'Álvaro,' I say. 'Álvaro Lasheras Gil.'

The constable types it. Slowly. With two fingers. He asks me to spell it and I spell it for him, letter by letter, my voice steady.

'Relationship to the accused?'

I don't answer.

Three years. The blue coat. The folder, cleaned page by page. *I miss you, Ada. So much.*

'None,' I say.

The constable looks up from the screen.

'None?' He says it slowly, like a warning. 'If he's your ex-partner, it should go on record. It changes things.'

'None.'

He looks at me a second longer. Then he types it.

That's all.

The ground doesn't shake. The lights don't go out. He doesn't appear in the doorway. It's a name. Three words on one line of a form, between the date of birth and the address. An ordinary man's name, the kind there are forty of in any office in Madrid.

I made the monster myself. By keeping quiet.

I sign with the station's biro, which is tied to the desk with a little chain.

On the way out, in the car park, with the north-westerly in my face, I say it once more. Out loud. To check.

'Álvaro.'

Nothing. The wind takes it, the way it takes everything here.

Before starting the car I ring Lucía.

She picks up on the first ring, as if she's been holding the phone in her hand for months. Maybe she has.

'Ada? You never ring me.'

'I'm fine.'

'That's what people say when they're not fine.'

'Lucía. I said his name to a police officer. Out loud. And he wrote it down on a piece of paper.'

Silence on the other end. A long silence, a Madrid silence, with traffic in the background.

'And?'

'And nothing. Nothing happened.'

Lucía bursts into tears. And then into laughter. And then both, the way she does, with no transition.

'I'm coming at Christmas,' she says when she can. 'I don't care if you've got room. I'll sleep on the sofa, on the stairs, inside the lamp. And you're going to introduce me to the towel guy properly, because on the video call I only saw half his face and that was already too much.'

I laugh. In the car park of a police station in Kirkwall, in the north-westerly, I laugh.

'OK,' I say.

'OK what?'

'OK to everything.'

I drive back along the road with the window down, even though it's cold. I don't think of him until Stenness. And only to realise I haven't thought of him.

---

### Maruxa · Costa da Morte, September 1971

We were married in the harbour church on a Saturday in September, by the same old priest as always, who had to ask the bishop's permission because the groom was a Protestant, and the bishop took three weeks to answer.

My mother came. In a wheelchair, pushed by my sister, in a blue dress with the holy card of the Virgen del Carmen in her handbag. She cried from the first minute.

My father walked me up the aisle on his arm in his funeral suit, with the face of a man who hadn't slept in three nights. When we got there, he gave my hand to Klaus and said to him, in Galician, in front of the whole village:

'*Cóidama, alemán.*'

Look after her for me.

Klaus didn't quite understand it. He nodded anyway, very serious, his eyes shining.

The ring was the silver one with the little blue stone. The one from the Neckar. He took it out of the pocket of his grey coat, where he had carried it for two years, jingling against his keys. His hands were shaking so much it took him three tries to get it on me.

He didn't sing at the wedding. I made him promise he wouldn't sing.

I sang. At the reception, in the harbour tavern, after the *pulpo*. My mother's *alalá*. And he sat very still in his chair, glass in hand, listening to me the way he used to listen to me in the factory, at the far end of the floor, with his head tilted a little.

The photographer took a picture of us as we came out of the church. Just one. He told us to look at the camera.

I looked.

He didn't.

That's the photo that hangs above my sideboard. My granddaughter says it's the most beautiful photo in the world, and that she doesn't understand why her grandfather isn't looking at the camera.

I understand.

He'd gone five years unable to stop looking at me. He wasn't going to start that day.

## 30 · Julian

My name is Julian.

I have said it eleven times since this morning. I counted them. To the dog. To the bathroom mirror. To a gull on the gallery railing, which didn't flinch. To Brais. To Ada's grandmother, at the door of her house, with the smell of the *empanada* coming out from behind her like a welcome.

Abuela looks me up and down. The beard. The shadows under my eyes. The half-moon on my hand.

'*Xa era hora*,' she says.

And she steps aside to let me in.

---

The kitchen is small and warm. There is an oilcloth on the table with lemons on it. Three plates. Three glasses. A radio on very low, with the shipping forecast.

I sit where she tells me. Ada sits beside me. Her grandmother, across from us.

She cuts the *empanada* with a knife that is older than me. Much older. She gives me the biggest piece.

'Eat,' she says. '*Estás moi fraco*.'

I don't know what *fraco* is. I know from the way she looks at me. The same way Ada looked at me at the beginning, when she gave me lentils: like something that has to be held down to the earth with food.

I eat.

It is tuna, with pepper and onion, and the pastry falls apart. I burn my tongue. I don't mind. It is the first time I have eaten something made by someone who isn't Ada. The first time someone has put a plate in front of me without knowing where I come from.

Or knowing it and putting it in front of me anyway.

Abuela doesn't ask anything.

That is what impresses me most. Not where I am from. Not what I do. Not how I met her granddaughter. She eats without hurrying, fills my glass with wine twice, and over coffee, while Ada does the washing-up, she leans towards me across the oilcloth and says, very low:

'*Ti cantas?*'

I don't understand.

'Do you sing?' she repeats, in English.

I look at Ada. Ada has gone still at the sink, a plate in her hand, her back to us.

'I don't know,' I say. 'I've never tried.'

Abuela nods. As if that were a good answer. The best one possible.

'*Pois proba*,' she says. '*Que non sexa tarde.*'

---

We walk back to the lighthouse, with the evening coming down.

Ada is quiet. I take her hand. She squeezes it.

'She asked you about singing,' she says.

'Yes.'

'She asked my grandfather that once. Fifty-five years ago.'

'And did he sing?'

She laughs. For the first time in a week. With her head thrown back.

'Terribly. He sang terribly. But he stayed.'

---

That night I wait for her to fall asleep.

It doesn't take long. She hasn't really slept in days. She falls asleep in the bed upstairs with the red pencil still in her hand and the page of *The Boy Who Stays* face down on the bedside table, with me drawn on the back.

I take the pencil from her. I pull the duvet up over her. I go downstairs.

The iron trunk is at the top, in the lantern, under the lamp. The same one where Ada locked the sketchbook away with a padlock. It hasn't got a padlock any more. Inside there are things belonging to the keepers who lived here before her. A cap. A logbook with its covers swollen from the damp. And two manuals, in old-fashioned English, with pen-and-ink drawings: *Instructions for the Lighting Service* and *On the Auxiliary Generator Set*.

I have read them all the way through. Three times. On the nights she slept and I didn't.

I think even then I knew that one day I would want to do this.

---

The auxiliary generator for the lamp is in the shed out the back. Not the small one for the house, the one Ada turns on every time the power goes off: the other one, the big one, the one nobody has started since eighty-seven. Rusted. With a layer of dust and nests.

I clean it. I change the oil with a can that is on the shelf and smells old but works. I bleed the diesel. I check the wires one by one, with the torch in my mouth, the way the drawing on page forty shows.

I pull the cord.

Nothing.

Again.

Nothing.

The third time it coughs. It spits black smoke. It chokes.

I sit down on the floor of the shed, my hands black with grease, and realise I am smiling.

Because it has failed. Because nobody drew me knowing how to start a 1962 generator. Because I am learning it myself, alone, in the dark, with grease up to my elbows and a wet manual. Because if it starts, it is going to be mine.

The fourth time it starts.

A low sound, steady, that makes the sheet metal shake. The bulbs in the shed flicker. They stay on.

---

I climb the hundred and twelve steps of the tower.

I count them. I have climbed them many times with her, by day, to look at the sea. Never at night. Never alone.

At the top, the lantern. The big lens, of glass cut in rings, like a glass onion the size of a man. Ada took its cover off the week before, to show it to me. She said it was the most beautiful thing she had ever seen. She said nobody had lit it since 1987, when they automated the other lights in the Sound and this one was left without a job.

I close the circuit. The way page twelve says.

The bulb inside lights up. First orange. Then white. Then so white I have to shut my eyes.

And the lens begins to turn.

Slowly. With a creak of old gears that grows smoother. And the light comes out through the glass rings as a beam, a long white arm that sweeps the sea, the rocks, the cove, the road, the town down below with its four lights, the hill, and comes back to the sea.

Once every twelve seconds.

I watch it with my mouth open.

It is the first thing I have done in the world that nobody drew me doing.

---

I don't hear her come up.

I notice because, in the glass of the lantern, I see something move behind me. I turn round.

It is Ada. Barefoot, with my fisherman's jumper over her pyjamas, her hair a mess, out of breath from the hundred and twelve steps.

She comes over to the railing. She watches the light turn.

She has spent a month living in a dark lighthouse. She has spent her whole life living in the dark. I know because she has told me, in pieces, at night, by the light of the stove. I know what a cumulonimbus is and I know what a woman who has stopped singing is.

'I lit it so you'd see it when you came back,' I say. 'But you came back sooner.'

'Came back from where?'

'I don't know.' I shrug. 'From wherever you were.'

A sound escapes her. I don't know if it is laughing or crying. Both.

The beam passes over us. It lights up our whole faces for a second. It goes away again.

'Thank you,' she says.

'It's not for you.' I push her hair out of her face. 'Well, yes. But not only.'

'Who else is it for?'

I look down. At the town. At a small window by the harbour, the only one still lit at that hour, above the net house.

'For anyone who's looking.'

---

We sit on the iron floor, back to back. The generator purrs below. The sea breathes. The light turns.

'Someday I'll light it myself,' she says, all at once.

'I can teach you.'

'No.' She shakes her head against the back of my neck. 'I want to learn it on my own. Like you. With the wet manual and black hands. I want it to start for me the fourth time.'

I don't know how she knows about the fourth time. I haven't told her.

I don't ask. The same way she didn't ask Brais's name. There are things you don't ask because asking would be starting to want to know them, and others you don't ask because you already know them.

'OK,' I say.

'Promise me you won't leave it on until then.'

'Why?'

'Because the next time it's lit it has to be because I've come back. For real. All the way.'

I nod.

We stay like that a long time. Until it starts getting light behind the hill. Until she falls asleep again, against me, with her mouth a little open.

She snores once. Softly.

---

Before switching it off, I get up to look at the sea one last time from up here.

In the glass of the lantern, with the dawn behind it, I can see the reflection of the tower, the railing, Ada asleep on the floor wrapped in my jumper.

And a silhouette standing behind her. Tall. Still. Looking at her.

I freeze.

Then I raise my hand. The silhouette raises its hand.

My reflection. Of course. It is my reflection.

I lower my hand.

The silhouette takes one second longer to lower its own.

One second. Nothing. The glass is old, I tell myself. The rings bend the light. I have been up all night.

I switch off the circuit. The lens stops. The light goes out.

I pick Ada up in my arms and carry her down the hundred and twelve steps, one by one, without waking her.

I don't look at the glass again.

My name is Julian. That is the twelfth time I have said it. Today I said it only to myself.

---

### Maruxa · Stromness, Today

*Non durmo ben desde que morreu Klaus. Seis anos.*

For fifty-three years he woke up before me. At five, the hour of the boats. And he didn't get up: he lay there watching me sleep, like that morning in Stuttgart. Every time I felt him awake, I shut my eyes tighter and pretended to be asleep, so he could go on looking. I never told him. I think he knew, and pretended he didn't, so that I could go on pretending.

That is how we loved each other. Both of us pretending at once.

The first night without him I came home from the hospital in Kirkwall to a bed I had made myself that morning, because Klaus used to say an unmade bed is a house that has given up. I woke at five and there was no one watching me. I felt it before I opened my eyes: the air in the room was still in a different way. I lay there a long time pretending to be asleep. Out of habit. Just in case. Then I got up and made coffee. Two cups, without thinking. I drank them both.

I get up at three, at four, and sit at the kitchen window with the light off, looking at the harbour. The boats. The breakwater. The black headland against the black sky, and at the end of the headland, where the land runs out, the lighthouse. Across the Sound, the hills of Hoy.

Dark. Always dark. Since eighty-seven.

Not that night.

That night, at ten past two, I was at the window with a camomile tea and I saw it light up. First a little yellow light, like a candle. Then white. Then the arm of light that passed over the sea, over the harbour, over my window, over me.

Once every twelve seconds. I counted.

I put my hand to my mouth.

Klaus told me once that, when he was a boy, his mother would light a candle in the window on winter nights so his father would know where the house was when he came home. That it wasn't necessary. That the street had a thousand lights. But that the one in the window was the only one that was lit for him.

I don't know who lit the lighthouse. I know who lives there.

And I know, because I saw him today in my kitchen eating the *empanada* with his eyes closed, that the young man with the beard is not of this world. The old folk here would call him a selkie. He doesn't need to be of this world. Klaus wasn't either. He came with a dictionary and a grey coat to a village that didn't want him, and he stayed fifty-three years.

I stayed at the window until it got light and the lighthouse went dark.

Then I washed the cup. I got dressed. I went down to the harbour like every morning.

And on the breakwater, facing the sea, with no one to hear me, after six years, I sang.

# Glossary

**Galician**

*a Vella* — "the Old Woman". Ada's name for the rock at the mouth of her cove.

*alalá* (pl. *alalás*) — an old Galician song, slow and unaccompanied, sung by women at work.

*Bo día* — "Good day". Klaus wrote it down as he heard it: *bodia*.

*Carallo* — an all-purpose Galician oath.

*Cóidama, alemán* — "Look after her for me, German."

*filla* — daughter.

*fraco* — thin, run-down. *Estás moi fraco*: "You're far too thin."

*miña rula* — "my little dove". Maruxa's name for Ada since the day she was born.

*morriña* — the Galician homesickness, a longing for home that has no cure but going back.

*Non durmo ben desde que morreu Klaus. Seis anos.* — "I haven't slept well since Klaus died. Six years."

*o alemán* — "the German".

*o meu fillo* — "my son".

*orballo* — the fine Galician drizzle that soaks you without seeming to fall.

*Pois proba. Que non sexa tarde.* — "Then try. Before it's too late."

*rapaza* — girl, lass.

*ría* — a long sea inlet, a drowned river valley, of the kind that cuts into the Galician coast.

**Spanish**

*Abuela* — grandmother.

*café con leche* — coffee with hot milk.

*caldo* — Galician broth of greens, beans and potatoes.

*empanada* — a large Galician pie, baked in a tray, filled with tuna, meat or cockles.

*filloas* (sing. *filloa*) — thin Galician pancakes, like crêpes.

*orujo* — a strong Galician spirit distilled from grape skins.

*pasodoble* — a brisk Spanish dance in two-four time.

*pulpo* — octopus, boiled and served with paprika, salt and olive oil.

**German**

*Fräulein* — Miss.

*Mutter* — mother.

*Schäfchenwolken* — "little sheep clouds": altocumulus.

*Spanierin* — a Spanish woman.

*Wohnheim* — the workers' hostel.

**Portuguese**

*Um, dois, três* — "One, two, three."

*Outra vez* — "Again."

**Orcadian**

*peedie* — small, little. *Peedie lass*: Maruxa's endearment for Ada, picked up in fifty years of Stromness.

*the toon* — the town; in Orkney, Stromness or Kirkwall.

*selkie* — in Orkney lore, a seal that comes ashore, sheds its skin and walks as a human. Whoever hides the skin keeps the selkie; whoever finds it goes back to the sea.
