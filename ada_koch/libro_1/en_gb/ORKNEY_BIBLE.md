# Orkney edition — adaptation bible

*The Lovers of the Spiral* (Book I of *The Sketchbook of Ada Koch*), British English, relocated to Orkney.
You are adapting the existing US English translation (`en/The_Lovers_of_the_Spiral.md`). The Spanish original
(`Los_Amantes_de_la_Espiral.md`) is the authority on meaning; check it whenever the English seems off.

The author's brief, in four lines: British English throughout, no exceptions. Galician, Spanish, German and
Portuguese stay untranslated and in italics. Orkney is a real place, not a backdrop. The voices keep their soul;
they only change clothes.

---

## 1. The new geography (apply everywhere, consistently)

The present-day story moves from Galicia's Costa da Morte to **Stromness, Orkney**. Maruxa's past does **not**
move: Vigo 1966, Stuttgart 1966–71, her return to her Galician village in 1971 and the wedding there stay exactly
where they are. The bridge is this: in 1976, when the oil came to Flotta, the terminal needed men who understood
engines, and Klaus and Maruxa moved to Stromness with their small daughter (Ada's mother). Maruxa has lived in
Stromness ever since; Klaus died there six years ago.

## 2. Weather (Met Office usage)

- *borrasca* → a low / a depression. *anticiclón* → a high. *ciclogénesis explosiva* → explosive cyclogenesis. *mar de fondo* → swell / groundswell. *racha* → gust. *temporal* → the storm / a gale.
- The October storm of Act One (the night Iter arrives) has a Met Office name: **Storm Ailsa**. Ada watches it form (a low **south of Iceland**, not west of the Azores) and knows it will be named by morning.
- The November storm of Act Three (the night of the 12th, when Álvaro comes) is **Storm Brodie**.
- The four days of sun in Act Two: a **high parked over Scandinavia**, not over the Azores.
- Cloud names in Latin stay. The European model is ECMWF; "the fifty-one ensemble members" stays (Iter's name comes from *iteration*).

## 3. Measures, dates, money

- Ada thinks in metric at work (waves 10 metres, gusts 140 km/h, 13 °C at the buoy, millibars) and in imperial in daily life (**miles**, yards, feet and inches for people's height, pints). E.g. "four hundred metres" walking → "a quarter of a mile"; "two hundred metres" → "two hundred yards"; Klaus "two metres tall" in Ada's voice → "six foot six".
- **Maruxa keeps metric** — she is Galician and her fragments are her own voice.
- Dates: **12 November**, never November 12. Years in full.
- Money: £ in Orkney.

## 4. British English

- Spelling: metre, kilometre, colour, grey, realise, organise, recognise, apologise, centre, theatre, travelled, cancelled, defence, licence (n.), practise (v.), programme, catalogue, pyjamas, tyre, kerb, whisky. Use -ise consistently.
- Vocabulary: flat, lift, lorry, pavement, petrol, **torch**, **jumper** (the fisherman's sweater is now **the fisherman's jumper**; Brais's father's sweater → jumper), wardrobe, tap, rubbish, bin, biscuit, crisps, sweets, **queue**, mobile, holiday, autumn, film, bonnet, boot, windscreen, number plate, railway, chemist, garden, ground floor, trousers (not pants; pants = underwear), trainers (not sneakers), jogging bottoms (not sweatpants), duvet (not comforter), sofa, plasters (not Band-Aids), paracetamol (not acetaminophen), mum (Ada's mother as "Mum" when addressed; Maruxa's mother stays "Mamá" — that is Galicia), post (mail), ring (phone), have a shower, the washing-up, work out (figure out), ill (sick), cooker/hob, kitchen roll, hire car, motorway, single-track road, "half past six".
- Grammar: "in hospital", "at the weekend", "different from/to", "Have you got…", collective nouns as natural.
- **Dialogue in single quotes**: `'I'm not her cousin,' Iter said.` Quotes inside quotes: double. Interruptions and broken-off speech with an em dash: `'Iter, your hands—'`. Contractions in speech always.

## 5. Voice and tense (the author's decision: apply the localisation guide)

- **Ada narrates in the present tense**, first person. Convert every narrative past to present ("I wake before the alarm", "He knocks three times"). Retrospective comments that only make sense from later ("I've gone over it a hundred times since", "Later I'd understand…") become present-tense equivalents or are reframed ("I'll go over it a hundred times") — never lose their content. Flashbacks inside her narration (Madrid, childhood) go into the past as normal.
- **Iter / Julian narrates in the present tense** too. His voice stays elementary, concrete, discovering: short sentences, questions, counting, wonder. Never eloquent.
- **Maruxa narrates in the past**, as now. Oral, plain, coordinated sentences, warm, no self-pity.
- Syntax: alternate short and long sentences; **don't overuse fragments** — where three fragments in a row do the job of one sentence, you may join them. Keep the one-line paragraphs that land a beat (they are the author's signature). Never cut content, never add explanation.
- Register: intimate, restrained, dry humour. Romance readers: keep every sensual and emotional beat exactly.

## 7. Orkney folklore (light touch, ambiguous)

Add **only** these touches, each one or two sentences, no more:
1. Chapter 4/5, the night of Storm Ailsa, when the soaked man is on her doormat: Ada remembers in passing the old Orkney story her grandfather told her about **selkies**, seals that come ashore and take off their skins. She dismisses it.
2. Chapter 24, the three women in the bakery: after he leaves, one of them says something under her breath about him coming out of the sea, and another says *selkie* — he hears it, doesn't know the word, and keeps it.
3. The last Maruxa fragment ("Stromness, Today"), after "the young man with the beard is not of this world": the folk here would call him a selkie; Klaus wasn't of this world either.
Nothing else: no stoor worms, no trows, no finfolk.
