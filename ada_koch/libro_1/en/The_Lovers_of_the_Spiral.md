# Act One · The Lighthouse
## 1 · Ada

I woke before the alarm, the way I almost always do since I started living alone.

In Madrid it never happened. There I slept until someone woke me, and that someone decided the time, too. Here my body wakes on its own, at six thirty, as if it were in a hurry to check that the sea is still where it belongs.

I lay still for a moment under the comforter, listening. The wind was still gentle. The gulls hadn't started. All I could hear was the sea, down below, breathing against the rocks with that slow rhythm that wouldn't let me sleep at first and that now I'm not sure I could sleep without.

I put my feet on the floor and the cold of the tiles climbed up my legs. I love that cold. It reminds me that the house is mine, that nobody has turned the heat on for me or told me to put on some slippers.

I picked up my phone to check the buoy. Thirteen degrees.

I smiled to myself. My grandmother would say I'm tola. That swimming in October is for madwomen and Germans. Since I'm a quarter German, a quarter of it is forgiven.

---

The path down to the cove runs between gorse and loose stones. At that hour it's still night, so I wear a headlamp, like a miner. I know by heart the three spots where, if you step wrong, you go over. The first few months I went down with my heart in my mouth. Now I go down thinking about other things.

It smelled of salt, of seaweed, of wet eucalyptus. It smelled of cold.

I left my towel on the usual rock and took off my coat. Underneath I had on only my swimsuit. The air raised goose bumps over my whole skin, my arms, the back of my neck, my belly. I held there for a second, eyes closed, feeling my body shrink and wake up at the same time.

No one has touched me in a year.

I don't think about it often. I think about it in moments like this, when the wind passes over my skin and I realize it's the only thing that has brushed against me in days. And it hurts in a strange way. It isn't desire, or not only. It's more a hunger for something I don't know how to name.

I went into the water without thinking, because if you think, you don't go in. The cold bit my ankles, my knees, my thighs. When it reached my waist I let out a cry nobody heard. And I dove.

For three seconds nothing existed. Not Madrid, not work, not him. Only my heart pounding like crazy and my skin burning with cold.

Then the calm came.

It's hard to explain. It's as if the sea were holding me up. As if, for a moment, I didn't have to hold myself up alone. I turned over and floated on my back, arms open and ears under the water, and looked at the sky that was beginning to turn lilac in the east.

In the west, though, over the horizon, there was a band of cloud, low and dark. Flat on top, as if cut with a ruler.

I didn't like it at all.

I'd seen it the night before on the screen, small and far away, and I'd convinced myself it would break up before it got here. I was wrong. And it wasn't the only thing I was wrong about that week.

---

I walked back to the lighthouse shivering, the towel over my shoulders and my lips purple.

I'd been living there for a year, in the keeper's house at the foot of the tower. Three rooms, a woodstove, a couch with a plaid blanket I bought at the market in Cee, and an enormous window that looks out on the Atlantic with nothing in between. Up top, at the head of the tower, the big lamp slept under a gray canvas cover. It hadn't shone for anyone in decades.

Sometimes I think we're alike, she and I.

I took a hot shower, a long one, until I stopped shaking. When I got out, the mirror was fogged. I wiped it with my hand and looked at myself.

Thirty-two years old. Dark hair, wet, longer than it's ever been. Collarbones sharp. My grandfather's eyes, gray, which my grandmother says are the best thing I have.

In Madrid I wore my hair short. Now I'm letting it grow, and every time I dry it in front of the mirror I feel a small victory I couldn't explain to anyone.

---

I'm a meteorologist. I work from the kitchen, with three monitors on the wooden table where the keepers, I suppose, used to eat. The company is based in Bilbao and sells forecasts to wind farms and shipping lines, people who lose a lot of money if the wind blows when it shouldn't. My boss says I'm the meteorologist with the best view in Europe.

I made myself a café con leche and opened the European model.

Every night it runs fifty-one times, each time with the data changed a little, to see how wrong it can be. In the trade they're called ensemble members. I've called them iterations since college, because it sounds to me like something that comes back. Usually they spread apart like the strands of an undone braid. I like watching them. I like thinking that the future has fifty-one shapes and only one of them will come to pass.

That morning almost all of them pointed to the same place. Here.

A low west of the Azores, deepening so fast that I set my cup down on the table without noticing. Explosive cyclogenesis. On the Costa da Morte nobody needs it explained. Ten-meter waves. Roofs on the road. Boats that don't come back.

It would arrive in two days. Maybe less.

I felt the tingle I feel with big storms, a mix of fear and fascination I've never confessed to anyone. It's happened since I was a girl. My mother used to lock herself in the bathroom when it thundered and I would sneak off to the window to see it all.

At the morning meeting I showed it to Marcos and Lucía.

"It's going to run right over you, Ada," Marcos said.

"The lighthouse has been there a hundred and forty years."

"The lighthouse, sure. I meant you."

I said nothing. Lucía changed the subject to rescue me, as always. But when we hung up she messaged me privately.

*Are you really okay out there alone? If you want I'll call you tonight.*

I sent her a heart and a photo of the sea. It was easier than answering.

Lucía was the one who encouraged me to come here. She says the sea cures almost everything. I don't know if that's true, but so far it hasn't hurt me.

---

At four I got a voice message from my grandmother.

I listened with the phone pressed to my ear, standing by the window.

*Nena, the radio says a big one's coming. Fasten those shutters good, eh? And don't swim tomorrow, I know you. Wednesday I'm expecting you for lunch, I'm making empanada. Un bico, miña rula.*

Miña rula. My little dove. She's called me that since I was born.

My eyes filled with tears for no reason. Or for every reason at once. There are days when tenderness catches me off guard and disarms me more than any blow.

I answered with another voice message, saying yes to everything, though we both knew I was going to swim tomorrow.

---

In the afternoon, when I'd finished, I did what I always do. I opened the notebook.

In the closet I have a box full of black notebooks, all the same. Twenty-three years of notebooks. Almost all of it is clouds, because I draw the sky the way other women draw flowers. But in the margins, in the corners, in any empty space, there he is.

I know how it sounds. A grown woman who has spent her whole life drawing the same man. I started drawing him at nine, when he was still a boy, and he grew up with me. I've never given him a name. Almost no one has seen him.

I sat by the window, legs tucked up and a blanket over my knees. The afternoon light came in gray and soft. I sharpened the tip of my pencil.

I started with the eyes, as always.

Dark. Patient. Eyes that look at you as if they'd been waiting for you a long time and wouldn't mind waiting a little longer.

No one has ever looked at me like that. That's why I draw them.

Then the line of the jaw. The hair, a little long, falling over his forehead. The mouth, which never comes out the way I want. I stopped there, pencil in the air, and caught myself imagining what his voice would be like. Whether he'd speak softly. Whether he'd laugh at my bad jokes. Whether his hands would be warm.

I blushed, alone, in my own kitchen. Ridiculous.

But I didn't stop. I kept looking at him longer than anyone spends on a drawing, with that tug in my chest I sometimes feel when I finish him. As if I were missing something I've never had. As if I missed him without ever having met him.

Then my phone buzzed.

---

I turned it over thinking it would be Lucía.

It wasn't Lucía. It was a number with no name.

I'd deleted it from my contacts almost a year before, the day I left Madrid. Deleting it didn't do much good. I knew those nine digits by heart, the way you know the phone number of a house you no longer live in.

*Still in Galicia?*

I looked at the screen. Three words, no greeting, as if we'd spoken yesterday. I felt something in my chest. Tiredness, maybe. Or sadness. A whole year learning not to wait for his messages, and three words were enough to set everything moving inside again.

Even in three words I could hear his voice. The way he had of saying my name: the first a drawn out, the second falling, the way you'd say it to a little girl who has stained the tablecloth again. *Aaada.* For three years I thought it was affection.

I hadn't told anyone in Madrid where I lived now. I wondered who had told him. Then I wondered why it mattered so much to me.

I set the phone face down on the table, on top of the notebook, as if I could cover it up.

I didn't answer.

That night I didn't eat dinner.

I put water on for tea and watched the pot until it boiled, without moving, and then I turned off the burner and didn't make it. My stomach was clenched like a fist. Not even the smell of the bread I'd bought that morning tempted me.

I tidied the kitchen, which was already tidy. I sorted the notebooks in the box by year. I did all the things a woman does when she doesn't want to think about something, and that something sits down beside her anyway, patiently, to wait until she's done.

I tried to breathe the way the psychologist taught me. Four seconds in, four holding, four out. On the third round I realized I was counting too fast, as if I wanted to get it over with. I gave up.

The house was making noises I'd never heard. Or that I'd always heard and never listened to. The floorboards creaked on their own. A loose shutter was knocking somewhere, softly, like someone rapping with their knuckles and thinking better of it. The wind was beginning to slip under the door with a thin whistle, almost a voice.

I curled up on the couch with the blanket up to my chin. I turned on only the small lamp, the one on the table. I like that light. It makes the house seem smaller and more mine.

And I opened the notebook again.

I don't know why. For the same reason, I suppose, that as a girl I used to crawl under the sheets with a flashlight when there was a storm. Because it was mine. Because it was the only thing in the house nobody could touch.

There he was, in the corner of the page, surrounded by cumulus. The dark eyes. The unfinished mouth.

I looked at him for a long time. In the lamplight he seemed almost alive, with shadows I hadn't drawn. I ran my fingertip along the edge of the paper, without touching him, so as not to smudge him.

"You'd wait for me," I told him quietly.

I heard myself and felt embarrassed. A thirty-two-year-old woman talking to a drawing in the middle of the night. If anyone had seen me, they'd have thought I was going crazy. Maybe they'd have been right.

But there was no one. Only me, the wind, and him.

I hugged the notebook to my chest and rested my head against the back of the couch. I thought I wouldn't be able to sleep. I thought about the message, about the nine digits, about his way of writing without a greeting, as if one weren't needed. I thought about everything I'd left there, and everything I'd brought with me without realizing.

And then I thought about the eyes in the drawing. About how they waited without hurry. About what it would be like for someone to look at me like that, for real, just once.

I don't know when I fell asleep.

I woke at three in the morning to a gust of wind that made the windowpanes shake. The lamp was still on. The notebook had slipped into my lap, open to the same page.

I got up and went to the window, barefoot, with the blanket over my shoulders.

I couldn't see the sea. I could only hear it. Louder than ever, closer, as if it had climbed the cliff to have a look.

And the band of cloud was no longer a band.

It was the whole sky.

### Maruxa · Vigo, March 1966

My mother didn't cry at the station. She cried afterward, my sister told me, on the bus back, the whole way home. But not on the platform. On the platform she fixed the collar of my coat, slipped an embroidered handkerchief into my pocket, and told me to eat.

"You're going to waste away to nothing, Maruxiña. Over there they eat potatoes with everything."

That was the last thing she said to me. To eat.

I was nineteen, with a borrowed coat, a cardboard suitcase tied with string, and a slip of paper with an address I didn't know how to pronounce. *Stuttgart-Feuerbach.* I'd practiced it all week in front of the mirror and it still came out wrong.

There were a lot of us on the platform. Girls from Ourense, from Lugo, from the Coast. Some with their sweethearts, who kissed them on the mouth in front of everyone, without shame, because it didn't matter anymore. Some with small children who were staying behind with their grandmothers. I had only my mother and my little sister, who held on to my skirt as if she could stop the train from moving.

I had no sweetheart. That made me a little envious and a little relieved. No one would be waiting for me. No one would write me letters that made me cry. I was going to earn the money we needed at home, because my father couldn't manage the boat anymore and the sea doesn't give enough for everyone, and then I'd come back. Two years. Three at most.

That's what I thought.

When the train whistle blew, my sister started wailing. She was seven. She grabbed my skirt with both hands and there was no getting her loose. My mother had to pry her fingers open one by one, very patiently, while she told her that Tata would be back soon, that Tata would bring her chocolate from Germany.

I climbed into the car with my face burning and my skirt wrinkled where she'd held it. I leaned out the window. My mother raised her hand. She didn't wave it. She only raised it, open, and left it like that, still, in the air, as the train began to move.

I raised mine too.

We stayed like that, both with our hands open, until the curve of the track took her away.

---

The train took thirty-one hours to reach the border at Irún, and then another whole day to Germany.

I didn't sleep. I sat on a wooden bench, with my suitcase between my legs so no one would steal it, watching a Spain I didn't know go by the window. Castile, yellow and flat as a table. Then France, which was green like Galicia but in a different way, tidier, as if someone combed it every morning.

Beside me sat a girl from Lugo named Pilar. She was twenty, with a round face and huge hands from milking cows. She offered me chorizo wrapped in newspaper and told me her sweetheart was waiting for her there, in Stuttgart, that he'd gone a year before and had written to say there was work for both of them.

"And you? Who do you have there?"

"No one."

Pilar looked at me with pity. Then she gave me another piece of chorizo, as if that would fix it.

At night, when the car went dark and all you could hear was the clatter and the snoring of a man at the far end, I rested my forehead against the cold glass of the window and looked out. There was nothing to see. Only, now and then, the lights of some village going by fast, small, yellow, like the lamps of boats at sea.

I thought of home. Of the wood-burning stove. Of my father smoking on the bench by the door. Of the smell of the harbor in the morning, of salt, of diesel, of fish just landed.

And for the first time I felt afraid. A big fear, the kind that gets into your chest and won't let you breathe. Afraid of not coming back. Afraid of coming back different. Afraid that over there no one would know who I was.

I started singing softly, without realizing. One of the songs my mother sang when she hung out the wash. A very old one, the kind that belongs to no one.

Pilar woke up and elbowed me.

"Cala, muller, you're going to wake the whole train."

I stopped. I hadn't even noticed I was singing.

That happened to me a lot. It still does. My husband used to say I sang the way other people breathe, without thinking, and that was why I sang so well: because nobody was listening to me, not even me.

But that was later. Much later.

On that train, in the dark, with my forehead against the glass, I didn't yet know he existed. He was there, fifteen hundred kilometers away, asleep in a small room in a boardinghouse in Feuerbach, not knowing I existed.

And the train kept bringing us closer, kilometer by kilometer, in the dark.

## 2 · Ada

I didn't wait for Wednesday.

When I got up, my neck sore from sleeping on the couch, the sky was still covered from end to end and the wind was no longer a breeze. It was a constant shove, insistent, that made the cracks around the windows whistle. I checked the model with my first coffee. The low was still deepening. Still coming.

By midday tomorrow at the latest, the serious part would begin. And I wanted to see my grandmother before then.

I didn't put it to myself that way. I told myself I had to buy batteries and diesel for the generator, that the fridge was empty, that on Wednesday, with the storm on top of us, I wouldn't be able to get down. All of it was true. But the truth underneath was something else. I needed someone to hold me, and the only person in the world I could ask without explaining anything lived in a house with blue windows by the harbor.

---

The road from the lighthouse to the village is four kilometers of curves between pines, eucalyptus, and meadows of a green that doesn't exist in Madrid. I drove it in my old car, both hands on the wheel because the wind shoved it toward the ditch at every clearing.

The village was on edge. You could feel it in everything. The men at the harbor were doubling up mooring lines and hauling the small boats up the ramp with a tractor. The women were bringing their flowerpots inside. There was a line at the gas station, and at the bakery someone had handwritten a sign: *Mañá non abrimos*.

I love the village when it's getting ready for a storm. It's like watching a family get ready for a difficult visit. Everyone knows what has to be done without saying it.

I bought what I had to buy. Batteries, candles, two jerricans of diesel, bread for three days, a bag of mandarins because they were beautiful. Then I left the car by the harbor and walked to the last house before the breakwater.

My grandmother's house is gray stone, small, with shutters painted cobalt blue. She repaints them every spring whether they need it or not. She says blue scares off sadness. I don't know if that's true, but every time I turn the corner and see them, something in my chest loosens.

I didn't have time to knock. The door opened on its own.

"I knew you'd come today," she said. "Come in, come in, before the wind carries you off."

She was wearing her flowered apron and her hands were covered in flour. She hugged me like that, without wiping them, and left two white prints on the back of my coat. She smelled of slow-cooked onion, of bay leaf, and of the rose cologne she's worn for as long as I can remember.

I held on to her a little longer than usual. She just patted my back, like when I was little and woke up from a nightmare.

"You're cold as a fish," she said at last. "Did you swim?"

"No."

"Liar. Your hair's wet."

I laughed. It was true. I'd gone down to the cove at seven, though the sea was already too rough to swim far. I'd only gone in up to my waist. But I'd gone in.

"You're tola," she said, and pushed me toward the kitchen. "Just like your grandfather."

---

My grandmother's kitchen is the warmest place in the world. I mean that literally. It has a wood-burning stove that's been lit since 1974 and only goes out in August, and a wooden bench against the wall where I used to sit as a girl with my legs dangling.

I sat on the bench. My legs didn't dangle anymore, but the feeling was the same.

My grandmother moved back and forth without stopping. She's seventy-nine and has an energy that shames me. Her name is Maruxa. Well, her name is María, but nobody has called her that since the day she was baptized.

She was making empanada. A day earlier than planned, for me.

"I knew you'd come," she said again, as if that explained everything.

I had a theory about my grandmother. That she knows everything before it happens. That she has a kind of radar for the people she loves, the way I have one for storms. She says it isn't radar, it's the habit of looking.

While the dough rested, she set two cups of pot-brewed coffee on the table and sat down across from me. She looked at me over her glasses, up and down, with that gaze that undresses you without taking anything off.

"You're thin."

"I'm the same."

"You're thin and you've got circles under your eyes. Are you sleeping?"

"Some."

"Some." She clicked her tongue. "At your age I slept like a log. Of course, at your age I already had your mother and your uncle, and I got into bed so tired I fell asleep praying."

It amused me to picture her young, worn out, praying. It amused me and made me envious. A life so full it leaves you no time to think about what's missing.

---

On the wall, above the sideboard, was the same old photo.

My grandparents on their wedding day, in black and white. She in a simple dress, long-sleeved, with a smile so big it looks like it's about to burst off her face. He, very tall, blond, in a suit with sleeves too short for him, looking at her instead of at the camera.

Klaus Koch. My grandfather. He died six years ago, but in this house he never quite left. His pipe is still in the green glass ashtray. His corduroy jacket is still on the coat rack by the door. My grandmother says she doesn't put them away because she doesn't feel like it. I know she's lying, and she knows I know.

"Tell it to me again," I asked her.

"Tell what?"

"You know what."

She played hard to get, as always. She loves it when I ask. And I love hearing it, even though I've known it by heart since I was five, even though I could recite it word for word.

"I was nineteen," she began, her hands around her cup. "I went to Germany the way so many girls went, to work in a factory in Stuttgart. Car parts. I didn't know one word of German. I cried every night that first month, hugging my pillow so the girls in my room wouldn't hear me."

"And then he showed up."

"Then he showed up. He was the line supervisor. Very serious, very blond, very German. He'd walk behind us checking everything and never said a thing. I thought he had it in for me, because he was looking at me all the time."

"And he didn't have it in for you."

"No." A young laugh escaped her, a girl's laugh. "It took him nine months to speak to me. Nine months, Ada. And when he finally came up to me, red as a tomato, he told me in horrible Spanish that I sang very pretty while I worked."

"And you didn't know you were singing."

"No idea. I sang without noticing. Things from here, my mother's songs, alalás from when we harvested. And he'd spent nine months listening to me. Nine months learning my songs without understanding a word."

Her eyes were shining. They always shine at that part. And I always get a lump in my throat.

Because that's the part I love most. Not the wedding. Not the happy ending. That. A man who listens to you for nine months without asking you for anything, just because he likes how you sound when you don't realize anyone's listening.

"We were together five years over there," she went on. "And then your great-grandmother fell ill and I had to come back. I told him I'd understand if he didn't come. That it was another country, another language, another life. That I wasn't asking him for anything."

"And?"

She got up to look at the dough.

"And that's for another day, my empanada's going to be ruined."

She always does the same thing. She gets to the platform in Stuttgart, to herself alone with her suitcase, and she gets up. She's told me a thousand times and she's never told me that part. I know he came, because he's in the photo on the sideboard and because my surname is Koch. But I don't know how. Or when. Or whether he hesitated. When I ask her, she laughs and tells me there are things you don't tell, you sing them, and I don't sing anymore.

Koch is my mother's surname. I've carried it first since the winter my father left for Vigo. My mother went to the civil registry that same week to ask them to swap the order of my surnames, and my father signed without a word of protest, the way he signed everything that took him further away from us. My father's name stayed behind, where no one sees it. I haven't missed it for a single day.

We fell silent. Outside, the wind was rattling the windowpanes.

When I was little, I thought that was what love was. That someday someone would cross half of Europe to knock on my door. Now I'm thirty-two and I don't think that anymore. Or I think it, but I'm embarrassed to.

"And you?" my grandmother asked out of nowhere.

"Me what?"

"Nobody?"

"Abuela."

"I'm only asking. A grandmother has the right to ask."

"I'm fine on my own."

She gave me the look she gives when she knows you're handing her the short version.

"Being fine on your own is one thing," she said. "Being on your own so you won't be hurt is another."

I didn't answer. I got up to check the oven, though it didn't need checking.

"That one from Madrid, is he still writing to you?" she asked behind my back.

Something inside me shrank. I thought of the phone face down. Of the three words.

"That empanada smells burnt," I said.

"That empanada smells like heaven, and you change the subject just like your mother."

But she didn't push. My grandmother never pushes. She leaves the question on the table, like a plate, and waits for you to get hungry.

To fill the silence she told me, for the umpteenth time, how my grandfather learned Galician. With an oilcloth-covered notebook where he wrote down the words he heard at the harbor, spelled however he pleased. *Carallo* spelled with a k. *Morriña* with two r's at the end. He'd learn them by heart and come out with them at the worst possible moments, in his Stuttgart accent, and the whole village would split their sides laughing.

"He sang the alalás terribly," she said, her eyes wet from laughing. "Off-key like a pig. And he didn't care. He'd stand in the bow of your uncle's boat and sing at the top of his lungs, and the real Galicians would cover their ears."

"And you?"

"I'd die of embarrassment." She was quiet for a moment, looking at the pipe in the ashtray. "And now I'd give anything to hear him sing off-key again."

I took her hand across the table. Her skin was thin as tissue paper, covered in spots, and warm. Always warm.

We said nothing more. There was no need.

---

We ate by the window, watching the water in the harbor grow darker and darker. The empanada was perfect, the crust crisp and the bonito filling juicy. I ate two and a half pieces, and my grandmother watched me eat with the satisfaction of someone who has won a small war.

Afterward we washed up together, side by side, without talking. She washed and I dried. It's what we've always done. There's something in that shared silence, with our hands busy, that heals me more than any conversation.

When I'd dried the last glass, she wiped her hands on her apron and went to the pantry. She came back with an enormous thermos and a package wrapped in newspaper.

"Caldo," she said, pointing at the thermos. "For tomorrow, you won't feel like cooking. And good candles, the ones from the store don't last a breath."

"Abuela, I have candles."

"You have candles from the store."

She stuffed it all into my bag. Then she helped me into my coat, as if I were eight, and buttoned the top button.

The notebook was peeking out of my pocket. I always carry it with me. She saw it and touched it with her fingertip.

"And that notebook?"

"Clouds. The usual."

She looked me in the eye. For a second I had the feeling she could see through the black covers, through the clouds, all the way to the corner of the page where a pair of dark eyes was waiting for me.

Only once, in my whole life, had my grandmother seen that drawing. I was twelve and spending the summer in this house. I left the notebook open on the kitchen table and, when I came back from the beach, she was looking at it. She didn't scold me. She only asked who he was. I told her nobody, that I'd made him up, red as a tomato.

*Well, you make him up very well*, she told me then. *He has the face of a good person.*

She never mentioned it again. In twenty years.

Until that afternoon. She kissed my forehead, her lips dry and warm, and looked at me with that face of knowing something I hadn't told her.

"Are you still drawing that lad?"

### Maruxa · Stuttgart, April 1966

The residence was a red-brick barracks beside the railroad tracks. They called it the *Wohnheim*, which took me a month to learn to say, and it had a very long hallway with doors on both sides, like a hospital.

I got a room with three iron beds and a window that faced a wall. I shared it with Pilar, the girl from Lugo, and with a Portuguese woman named Fátima who never spoke. Fátima was thirty, with a husband in Lisbon and two little daughters. She had their photos pinned to the wall above her bed, and every night, before she slept, she gave them a kiss with her fingertips.

I had no photos. I'd left in such a hurry it didn't even occur to me to bring any.

What I remember most about that first month is the cold. A cold different from the one at home. In Galicia the cold is damp, it gets into your bones, but it smells of something, of firewood, of the sea, of wet earth. The cold in Stuttgart smelled of nothing. It was dry and clean and cut your face like a new knife.

And the food. Ay, the food. Potatoes with everything, just as my mother said. Boiled potatoes, fried potatoes, potatoes in salad with vinegar, which was the worst thing I'd ever tasted in my life. And a black bread, hard, that weighed like a stone. I dreamed of my mother's empanada. I literally dreamed of it, at night, and woke up with the taste of the crust in my mouth and tears on my pillow.

I cried every night of that first month. Every one.

I cried hugging the pillow, biting it so no one would hear. Pilar slept like a log, but Fátima didn't. One night I heard her move in the dark. I thought she was going to tell me to be quiet.

She said nothing. She got up, sat on the edge of my bed, and put her hand on my back. Just like that, without speaking. A big hand, warm, the hand of a woman who has raised children.

She stayed like that until I fell asleep.

We never talked about it. In the morning she still didn't speak and I still didn't know what to say to her. But from that night on, every time I saw her kiss her daughters on the wall, I felt that I belonged a little to that family too.

---

The factory was a fifteen-minute walk away. We made car parts. I didn't know what they were for. I only knew they were small, metal, with holes, and that you had to check them one by one before putting them in the boxes. If one had a burr, out. If a hole was crooked, out.

It was a job for the eyes. Eight hours looking at metal under a white light that hummed. At the end of the day my head ached and I saw parts with my eyes closed.

There were thirty of us women on the line. Spanish, Portuguese, Italian, two Turkish women who kept their hair covered with a scarf. No one spoke German. We understood each other with gestures, with laughter, with the few words we were picking up. *Danke. Bitte. Schnell.* Above all *schnell*. Fast. It was what they said to us most.

The supervisors were German. Serious men, in gray coats, who walked behind us with a clipboard in hand, checking everything.

There was one who frightened me more than the others.

He was very tall. So tall he had to stoop a little to pass under the pipes on the factory floor. Blond, his hair cut almost to the scalp, and light eyes, gray, like the Stuttgart sky. He never smiled. He never spoke. He only walked behind the line, unhurried, and watched.

And he watched me.

More than the others. I was sure of it. Every time I looked up from the parts, there he was, at the far end of the factory floor, with his clipboard, watching me. And the moment our eyes met, he looked down and wrote something.

My stomach would knot up. I thought he was going to fire me. That he'd seen me talking to Pilar, or laughing, or getting a part wrong. That he was writing down my mistakes on his clipboard so he could dismiss me at the end of the month and send me back to Galicia with my cardboard suitcase empty.

One night I told Pilar.

"That German has it in for me."

"The tall one?" Pilar laughed. "That one doesn't have it in for anybody. That one hasn't got blood in his veins."

I wasn't so sure.

Because there was something in the way he looked at me that wasn't cold. I didn't know what it was. But it wasn't cold.

## 3 · Ada

I didn't answer her. I gave her a kiss, told her to fasten the shutters, and left with the bag of broth pressed against my chest.

But the question came with me. It sat in the passenger seat for the four kilometers of curves, with the wind shoving the car, and it stayed there, perfectly calm, watching me drive.

*Are you still drawing that lad?*

Yes, Abuela. I am. Twenty-three years later, I still am.

---

I spent the afternoon getting the lighthouse ready for what was coming.

It's a routine I've learned the hard way, winter by winter, even though I've only had one. Check the shutters one by one and close the ones facing west. Haul firewood up from the shed and stack it by the stove, all the way to the ceiling. Fill the tank of the generator, which is old and temperamental and coughs like a smoker before it starts. Bring in the porch chair, the pot of rosemary, the clothesline.

I worked with the wind against me, my hair a mess, my cheeks burning with cold and my hands full of splinters. I liked it. There's something about getting a house ready for a storm that makes you feel the house is truly yours. That you're protecting it. That it's protecting you.

Before it got dark I went down to the cove one last time.

Not to swim. The sea was already too rough, with waves reaching the rock where I leave my towel and pulling back, dragging stones with a sound like teeth. I went down only to look at it. To say goodbye to it calmly, the way you say goodbye to someone before a fight you know is going to happen.

I sat at the top of the path, on a flat stone, hugging my knees to my chest. The wind blew my hair into my face and made my eyes water. Below, *a Vella* took the blows of the waves, black and gleaming, unmoved, the way she's been taking them since before the village existed.

And then I saw it. The other one.

A small rock, to the left of *a Vella*, half hidden in the foam. Round on top, flat, with a kind of step on one side, like a seat. I'd been swimming there for a year and had never noticed it.

I froze.

It was the rock. The one from my first drawing, when I was nine. The rock where the boy sat to look at the sea. With its round shape and its step, identical, as if I'd traced it.

It couldn't be. At nine I had never been to this coast. Vacations at my grandmother's were down by the harbor, at the big beach on the other side of the village. I had never climbed up to the lighthouse. I had never come down to this cove.

I told myself all rocks look alike. That my memory was playing tricks on me, the way it always does. That I was cold and tired and had spent two days thinking about him too much.

I told myself that several times while I climbed back up to the lighthouse, with the wind pushing at my back.

I didn't turn around to look at it. I was afraid it would still look the same.

By the time I finished, it was night. I took a shower, put on my flannel pajamas and my thick socks, and heated up a bowl of my grandmother's broth. It tasted of turnip greens, of potato, of Sundays when I was little. I drank it standing by the window, both hands around the bowl, looking at the dark.

You couldn't see anything. You could only hear it. The sea had changed its voice. It wasn't breathing anymore. It was roaring.

At nine Lucía video-called me, the way she'd promised the day before. She appeared on the screen with her hair up in a chaotic bun, a glass of wine in her hand and her little boy asleep on the sofa behind her, mouth open.

"Let's see this lighthouse," she said. "Show me. I want to see if it's still standing."

I gave her a tour with my phone. The stove, the firewood stacked to the ceiling, my grandmother's candles lined up on the table like soldiers, the black window.

"You look like a Brontë novel," she said. "All you need is a dark gentleman knocking on the door in the middle of the storm."

I laughed. I laughed a lot, more than the joke deserved, and Lucía looked at me through the screen with narrowed eyes.

"What?"

"Nothing. I wish."

"You wish what? You wish for a dark gentleman?" She leaned so close to the camera that all I could see was her nose. "Ada Koch. Is there someone? Some sailor? Some barnacle fisherman with arms like this?"

"There's no one, Lucía. There's a generator that coughs and a grandmother who sends me broth."

"Well, that's a shame." She leaned back on the sofa and took a sip from her glass. "When you look so pretty with your hair long."

I went quiet for a second. Nobody had told me I looked pretty in a long time. Not even me.

We talked a while longer. About work, about Marcos and his plaid shirt, about her son's school, about a show I absolutely had to watch. Small things, warm things, the kind that remind you there's a normal life somewhere, waiting for you.

Before hanging up, she got serious.

"Promise me something. If the power goes out tomorrow, don't play the hero. If you're scared, go to your grandmother's."

"I promise."

We both knew I wasn't going anywhere.

---

I couldn't sleep.

I tried. I got into bed at eleven with a book I didn't read and turned off the light at twelve. At one I was still lying there with my eyes open, listening to the wind try the windows one by one, like someone looking for the right key on a ring.

My phone was on the nightstand, face down. I hadn't looked at it since the afternoon before. I hadn't answered. I wasn't going to answer.

At one-thirty I gave up. I pulled the thick sweatshirt on over my pajamas, went down to the kitchen, switched on the small lamp and took out the notebook.

Drawing is the only thing that truly calms me. More than swimming. More than breathing and counting the way the psychologist taught me. When I draw, there's no past and no future. Only the line I'm making, and the next one, and the next. It's like swimming in the October sea, but without the cold.

I opened the notebook to a blank page. Not a corner, not a margin. A whole page, clean, the kind I usually save for the important clouds.

And I started to draw him.

All of him. For the first time in twenty-three years.

---

The first time I drew him I was nine.

My parents were separating. I didn't really understand what was happening, but I understood the raised voices behind the kitchen door and the silences at breakfast, which were worse. I spent my afternoons shut in my room, drawing, because it was the only thing I could do without anyone asking me to choose.

One afternoon I drew a boy. A boy my age, with dark, tousled hair, sitting on a rock, looking at the sea. I'd never really seen the sea except on vacation, at my grandmother's. But I drew it from memory, with its little waves and its straight horizon.

And when I finished, I thought: this one stays.

Just like that, in those words. This one stays. Not like my father, who left that winter to live in Vigo with a suitcase and a box of records. Not like my mother, who left without moving, shut up in her sadness as if in a room with no windows.

This one stays.

From then on I drew him in every notebook. He grew up with me, without my planning it. At eleven I gave him a scar on his left eyebrow, because the boys in adventure books always had one. I thought it was the most romantic thing in the world. At thirteen I erased it, embarrassed, and never gave it back to him.

At sixteen I drew him for the first time as a boy I liked. With his lips parted, looking at me. I spent a whole afternoon on that drawing, my heart beating in my ears. And then I tore out the page and ripped it into tiny pieces, red with shame, as if someone could see inside me.

Then came the real boyfriends. The ones from college, the ones from Madrid. I fell in love, fell out of love, cried, forgot. And he stayed there, in the margins, without complaint. Waiting. He never needed anything. He never asked me for anything. He was just there.

Only once did I try to show him to someone.

I was twenty and head over heels for a boy in my department who played guitar at parties and wrote me bad poems on napkins. One night, in his student apartment, I showed him my notebooks. The clouds first. And then, with my heart in my mouth, a page where he was, sitting on a dock, looking at the water.

"And who's this?" he asked, laughing. "Your imaginary boyfriend?"

He didn't mean it badly. I know that. He was a sweet, clumsy boy who didn't know what he was holding. But I shut the notebook so fast I caught my finger, and I never showed it to him again. Not to him or anyone.

Since then the notebooks have lived in a box at the back of the closet, like love letters you never send.

I never gave him a name. Giving him a name felt like too much. Like admitting something I didn't want to admit even to myself.

---

That night I drew him standing, his hands in the pockets of a dark jacket. His hair a little long, wet, stuck to his forehead, as if he'd just come in from the rain. His weight on one leg, tired, like a man who has walked a long way to get here.

It took me hours. The wind was battering the house and I didn't hear it.

I drew the collar of his shirt, a little open. The line of his collarbone, just visible underneath. I drew his shoulders, broad but not too broad, the kind you imagine resting your head on. I drew his hands, which are always hard for me, which never come out right. That night they came out right. Big, long-fingered, with prominent knuckles. The hands of someone who knows how to do things.

I looked at them for a long time. I wondered what they would feel like on my skin. Whether they'd be rough or gentle. Whether they'd be slow.

I felt my cheeks burning and laughed to myself, softly, in my kitchen, at three in the morning. How silly. A grown woman blushing over a pair of graphite hands.

But I kept going.

I went back to the face. To the mouth.

The mouth was the one thing I hadn't liked for years. It always came out too serious, with a hard line at the corner, like a man waiting for you to explain yourself. I don't know where that line came from. It just came out that way.

That night, at last, I understood what was wrong with it. I didn't want a serious man. I didn't want a man who was waiting for explanations.

I wanted one who was about to smile at me.

I erased the hard line very carefully, without tearing the paper. And I drew it again, softer. With the corner lifted a little, as if he were about to smile and didn't quite dare yet. As if he'd seen me walk in and was glad, but didn't want it to show too much.

Like that.

I set down the pencil and looked at him.

It was him. It was him, the one he'd always been. The boy on the rock and the boy at sixteen and the man of now. The one who stays. He looked at me from the paper with those dark, patient eyes, with that smile about to be born, and I felt something I hadn't felt in a long time.

I felt that someone was waiting for me.

I ran my fingertip along the edge of his jaw. Barely touching the graphite, so I wouldn't smudge it. It was a silly gesture. A little girl's gesture. But the paper was warm from the lamp, and for a second, just for a second, it felt like skin.

I pulled my hand away.

I closed the notebook. I left both hands on its black covers, as if I were keeping in something that might escape.

It was three-thirty in the morning. Outside, the wind wasn't whistling anymore. It was roaring. I looked out the window and saw that the rain had started falling sideways, almost horizontal, hitting the glass like handfuls of gravel.

The model said tomorrow at noon.

The storm had come early.

### Maruxa · Stuttgart, June 1966

In June I stopped crying at night.

It didn't happen all at once. It was like getting over a cold, when one day you realize you haven't coughed in a while. One morning I got up, washed my face at the basin in the hallway in icy water, and thought that I hadn't cried that night. Or the night before. Or the one before that.

I don't know if it was because I got used to it or because I got tired of crying. I think both.

In June I also learned to say *Feuerbach* without them laughing at me in the bakery. And I learned that if you asked for the black bread cut in thin slices and put butter and sugar on it, it almost tasted like something. And that the Neckar river, on Sunday afternoons, filled up with German families out walking with their children, and that if you sat on the grass by the bank and closed your eyes, the sound of the water was a little like, just a little like, the sound of the ría.

I went almost every Sunday. Alone, because Pilar went with her boyfriend and Fátima stayed in writing letters to Lisbon.

I sat on the grass and looked at the water. Sometimes I sang. Softly, to myself.

---

He was still watching me.

It didn't frighten me so much anymore, but it made me nervous in a different way. It was like having a pebble in your shoe that doesn't hurt but that you feel all the time.

I had started noticing things. That he always passed behind my station twice a shift, never three times, never once. That when he passed, he slowed down. That he never wrote anything on his clipboard when he was near me, only afterward, at the far end of the floor, with his head bent over the paper, very focused.

One afternoon he came closer than ever. I had a part in my hand, one of those with a crooked hole that had to be thrown out, and I didn't realize he was behind me until I saw his shadow fall across the belt.

I went still. With the part in my hand. Not breathing.

I felt the heat of his body at my back. He smelled of soap, good soap, not the kind from the residence, and of something metallic, like everyone in the factory. He was so close I could hear his breathing over the noise of the machines.

He bent down a little. He reached over my shoulder, a big hand, long-fingered, the nails cut very short, and took the part from my fingers very carefully. Without touching me. Not even a brush.

He held it up to the light. He nodded. He dropped it in the bin for the bad parts.

And then he did something I had never seen him do.

He looked me in the eyes. Straight at me. Without looking down or writing anything.

It was a second. Maybe less. But in that second I saw something in his gray eyes that left my legs weak. Something like a question he didn't dare ask.

Then he turned and went, with his gray coat and his clipboard, to the far end of the factory floor.

I fixed my eyes on the belt, my heart racing, and let four parts go by without checking them. One of them, most likely, with a crooked hole.

---

That night I couldn't sleep.

Fátima was snoring softly. Pilar had gone out with her boyfriend and hadn't come back. The window facing the wall was open because of the heat, and through it came the noise of the freight trains passing on the tracks, long, so slow, as if they were never going to end.

I thought about his hands. About how he'd taken the part from me without brushing against me.

I thought that he had been careful not to touch me. Very careful. Too careful for someone who didn't care.

I turned over in bed and covered my face with the sheet. It was burning.

What a silly girl you were, Maruxa! Nineteen years old, fifteen hundred kilometers from home, without a word of German, and thinking about the hands of a foreman who didn't even know your name.

Because he didn't. At the factory everyone called me María. That was what it said on my file, on my contract, on the card we punched when we came in. *María Fernández Lago.* Nobody in all of Germany knew I was Maruxa.

I thought that if one day someone called me that, by my real name, in that cold country, I would burst into tears.

I didn't know yet that I would be right.

## 4 · Ada

At seven in the morning the power went out.

It didn't surprise me. On the Costa da Morte the power goes out at any excuse, and this was no ordinary excuse. I woke to the strange silence the fridge leaves when it shuts off, that hole in the background noise you didn't know was there until it disappears.

I felt around on the nightstand for the flashlight. I went downstairs barefoot, the cold of the tiles biting my feet, and went to the back room to start the generator.

It coughed once. Twice. On the third try it started with the roar of an old engine, and the kitchen bulbs flickered and came on. I gave its casing a little pat, like a dog that's been good.

I had diesel for two days, three if I didn't turn on the monitors.

I turned on the monitors.

I'm a meteorologist. I had the biggest low of the year right over my head. I wasn't going to miss it to save diesel.

---

The barometer on the wall, the old needle one that came with the house, showed a pressure I'd never seen on it. It had dropped thirty-two millibars in twenty-four hours. At this latitude, for a low to count as explosive, nineteen is enough.

It was a monster. A beautiful monster.

I opened the satellite image and lost my breath. The center was a hundred and fifty kilometers to the northwest, spinning on itself like water going down a drain. It was shaped like a comma, like a seashell, with its tail of clouds curling inward in a perfect spiral. It glowed white over the dark sea.

If I hadn't known what it was, it would have seemed like the most beautiful thing I'd ever seen in my life.

I knew. And it seemed that way anyway.

It's something I've never told anyone. That storms move me. That when I see one of these on the screen I feel a lurch in my stomach like the one you feel when you like someone. A mix of fear and wanting. Of wanting to get closer and knowing you shouldn't.

I sent the morning report. Gusts of a hundred and forty kilometers per hour on the coast. Twelve-meter waves. Recommendation: stop everything. Ports closed, turbines stopped, boats moored.

At nine-ten the internet went down.

At nine-fifteen I tried to call my grandmother and my phone had no signal. Not a single bar. The tower on the hill had gone with the wind, or with the power, or with both.

I sat looking at the screen. *No service.*

I felt strangely light. As if someone had closed a door that had been open for days, letting in a draft.

---

I spent the morning doing small things with my hands.

I filled jugs with water in case the pump failed. I set my grandmother's candles on the table, next to the box of matches. I checked the shutters again, one by one, with the wind pinning me against the wall every time I stuck my head out the door. The rain soaked me in ten seconds.

At noon I climbed the tower.

It's a hundred and twelve spiral steps, stone worn down in the middle by a hundred years of lighthouse keepers going up and down. I climbed them with my hand on the cold wall. The higher I went, the louder the noise grew. The wind whistled through the cracks in the lantern, at the very top, like an out-of-tune organ.

Up there, the lamp was still under its canvas cover, tied down with ropes. I checked the knots. They were fine.

Then I went to the glass and looked at the sea.

It wasn't blue. It wasn't gray. It was white.

All white, foam from end to end, as far as I could see, as if the whole ocean were boiling. The waves came in rows, enormous, slow, and broke against the cliff with a dull blow that got into my chest and made my bones vibrate. The water ran up the rock thirty, forty meters, and fell on the path like salt rain.

The tower was trembling. A little. Just enough to feel it in the soles of my feet.

I held on to the iron railing and looked until I was cold to the bone. I was afraid, of course. But it was a clean fear. A fear I understood. The sea is dangerous because it's the sea. It doesn't lie. It doesn't pretend to be something else.

I thought of the sailors who, for a hundred years, had looked for this tower's light on nights like this. Of their wives waiting at the harbor. Of the lamp turning and turning, telling them not this way, not this way, here's the rock.

Now nobody looked for it. And I was up there, alone, watching the storm from inside a lamp that was out.

Before I went down I did something I hadn't done in a year.

I untied one of the ropes of the cover and lifted the canvas by a corner.

Underneath was the lens. I had never seen it up close. It was huge, taller than me, a glass beehive made of stacked rings and prisms, hundreds of pieces cut by hand surrounding an empty space in the center, where the light used to be. Even though nothing was lit, the glass gathered the little gray daylight of the storm and gave it back multiplied, in reflections that trembled on my hands and on my face.

I put my palm on one of the rings. It was ice cold. Smooth as ice, as still water.

I thought it was the most beautiful thing I had ever touched. I thought it had been waiting a hundred years for someone to put something inside it.

I lowered the canvas again carefully and tied the rope with a double knot. My fingers were trembling a little, I don't know if from the cold.

I haven't told anyone, but that afternoon, going down the hundred and twelve steps, I made myself a promise. That one day I would light it. Not for the boats. For me.

I didn't know yet how far away that day was.

---

At five in the afternoon it got dark.

I turned off the monitors to save diesel. I lit the stove and two candles. I heated up the rest of my grandmother's broth and had it on the sofa, wrapped in the plaid blanket, my feet tucked under the cushion.

The noise was constant. Wind, sea, wind. Things banging outside and I didn't know what they were. A shutter came loose somewhere and started banging, one, two, one, two, like a heart out of rhythm.

I didn't get up to fasten it. I didn't dare open the door.

I had the notebook on my knees, closed. I didn't open it. I was a little embarrassed to open it, after the night before. Like when you've sent someone a message that was too honest and the next day you don't dare look at your phone.

So I didn't move. I listened.

At one point I thought I heard a voice. Outside, between the gusts. A man's voice, calling something. I sat up, my heart in my throat, and held my breath.

Wind. Sea. The loose shutter.

Nothing else.

It happens to me a lot. I hear things and then I don't know if I heard them. I see something move out of the corner of my eye and when I turn my head there's nothing. My mother used to say I had too much imagination. I suppose she was right. I suppose that's why I draw.

I lay back again. I pulled the blanket up to my chin.

To keep from thinking, I took out my phone and listened again to the voice messages my grandmother had sent that week. I had no signal, but the messages were still there, saved. Her voice filled the kitchen, small and tinny through the speaker. *Nena, I made filloas today. Nena, I ran into Ramiro's wife and she asked about you. Nena, don't go swimming, I know you.*

I listened to all of them, one after another, with my eyes closed. Then I started over.

When they ran out, the kitchen was emptier than before.

I wondered what I would do if someone really did knock. I thought about it seriously, like a game, to distract myself. If it was a lost fisherman, I'd give him broth and let him have the sofa. If it was a hiker, I'd lend him dry clothes and scold him for not checking the forecast. If it was someone handsome—and here I laughed to myself, softly, in the dark—if it was someone handsome, I'd comb my hair before I opened the door.

I touched my hair. It was a mess, frizzy from the damp, with a strand stuck to my cheek. I tucked it behind my ear.

How ridiculous. Nobody was going to come. Nobody climbs up to a lighthouse in the middle of an explosive cyclogenesis.

But I left it behind my ear anyway.

---

I don't know how much time went by. An hour. Two. The kitchen clock had stopped when the power went, its hands stuck at two minutes past seven, and I didn't feel like looking at my phone.

The candles burned down. The stove did too. I didn't get up to add wood.

I thought of my grandmother, alone in her house with the blue windows. I pictured her sitting in her kitchen with the battery radio, praying softly, or not praying, or singing without realizing it the way she used to sing in the factory in Stuttgart.

I thought of my grandfather, who one day knocked on my grandmother's door in a way she has never wanted to tell me about.

It was he who taught me the clouds.

I was eight and spending the summer in the village. One afternoon he took me to the end of the breakwater, sat me beside him with my legs dangling over the water, and pointed at the sky with the stem of his pipe.

"*Schäfchenwolken*," he said. "Little sheep clouds. Look. They look like a flock, see? Tomorrow the weather will change."

And the next day it changed.

From that summer on, every afternoon, he taught me a new cloud. The cumulus, which look like cauliflowers and mean good weather. The cirrus, which are angel hair and warn you something is coming. The cumulonimbus, which grow like towers and bring storms. He told me the names in German and in Latin, and I repeated them, and he gave me a mint candy for every one I got right.

He never knew I became a meteorologist because of him. Or maybe he did. When I finished my degree, he sent me a postcard from the village with a photo of the lighthouse and a single sentence written on the back, in his German handwriting, all spikes: *Für mein Wolkenmädchen.* For my cloud girl.

I have it stuck on the fridge. It had gone dark, like everything else, but I knew it was there.

I wondered what cloud tonight would have been. I wondered if he would have told me to be afraid, or to look.

I think he would have told me to look.

I thought that I had never had that. That I'd had other things. Loves that leave your heart in pieces and loves that leave your head full of doubts. But never that. Never someone who would cross half of Europe just to stay.

I hugged the notebook to my chest, without opening it, as if it were a teddy bear. Ridiculous. A thirty-two-year-old woman hugging a sketchbook in the middle of a storm.

But I didn't let go.

I thought, for the first time in a long time, that I was very alone. Not alone the way I liked to be, with the sea and my notebooks and my silence. Truly alone. Alone the way you are in a small boat in the middle of the night.

And I wanted, wanted terribly, for someone to knock on the door.

Then, between one gust and the next, I heard it.

It wasn't the shutter. It wasn't the sea. It wasn't my imagination.

It was three sharp knocks on the door.

---

### Maruxa · Stuttgart, December 1966

The first Christmas it snowed.

I had never seen it really snow. In Galicia, on the coast, snow is something that happens in the mountains, far away, on postcards. So when I got up on the morning of the twenty-fourth and saw the wall outside the window white, and the sky white, and enormous flakes falling slowly, without a sound, like feathers from a burst pillow, I stood for a long while with my nose pressed to the glass.

It was the most beautiful thing I had ever seen in my life. And I was alone to see it.

Fátima had gone to Lisbon to spend the holidays with her daughters. She had saved all year for the ticket. I watched her pack her suitcase for a whole week, folding and unfolding the same clothes, and the afternoon she left she kissed me on each cheek and left in my hand a medal of Our Lady of Fátima, tiny, made of brass.

"So you're not alone," she said. It was the longest sentence I heard her say all year.

Pilar went to dinner at the house of some people from her boyfriend's village, on the other side of the city. She invited me to go. I told her no, I had a headache. Nothing hurt. I just didn't want to be the one too many at somebody else's table.

And I had no money for the ticket. Everything I earned I sent home, except just enough to eat. My father needed a new engine for the boat. My sister needed shoes for school. My mother needed not to worry.

So I stayed in the empty residence. Me and three Turkish girls from the end of the hallway who didn't celebrate Christmas and who looked at me with curiosity when I passed them in the washroom.

---

I spent Christmas Eve sitting on my bed, with my coat on because the heating in the residence went off at ten, and with a candle I had bought at the church in Feuerbach.

For supper I had black bread with butter and sugar. And an orange Fátima had saved for me.

I peeled the orange very slowly, the way my mother did, all in one go, without breaking the peel, so it stayed whole like a flower. The smell filled the room. It smelled of home. Of Christmas Eve in the kitchen with the wood stove, my father cracking walnuts with his fingers and my sister asleep on the bench and my mother singing while she put the salt cod to soak.

And then I cried. For the first time since June.

I cried as much as I wanted, without biting the pillow, because there was nobody to hear me. I cried for my mother and for the empanada and for the sea. I cried for the snow, which was so beautiful and which I had no one to show.

When I was done, I blew my nose on the embroidered handkerchief my mother had slipped into my pocket at the station in Vigo. And I started to sing.

Not softly. Loud. For the first time since I came to Germany, I sang loud, with all my voice, without being afraid someone would tell me to be quiet. I sang Galician carols, the ones we sang as girls going from house to house asking for castanas. I sang my mother's alalás. I sang a lullaby my grandmother used to sing me that I hadn't remembered since I was little.

My voice bounced off the walls of the empty hallway and came back to me, different, bigger, as if a choir were singing.

Outside, the snow kept falling without a sound.

---

I sang for an hour. More.

I threw myself face down on the bed, my face in the pillow, so she wouldn't see me. But I was laughing. Laughing on my own, unable to stop, with the scarf still on and its smell of soap filling my nose.

Fátima looked up from her letter to Lisbon. She looked at me for a long while, over her glasses. Quiet, as always.

But before turning out the light she came over to my bed, kissed me on the forehead, the way she kissed the photos of her daughters, and whispered a single word in Portuguese.

"*Finalmente.*"

I didn't understand it until many years later. Until I, too, had a granddaughter whose face I saw light up for the first time.

I never gave back the scarf. Not that day, not ever.

I still have it. It's in the wardrobe in my room, folded, on the top shelf. It stopped smelling of him many years ago. But some nights I take it out and put it around my neck anyway, just in case.

## 7 · Ada

When some light came in through the crack in the shutter, gray and dirty, I couldn't take it anymore.

I opened the notebook.

My heart was beating in my fingertips as I turned the pages. Clouds. Clouds. An August cumulonimbus. And then the full page, the one from Tuesday night.

There he was.

Standing, hands in the pockets of the dark jacket. Wet hair over his forehead. His weight on one leg. His mouth about to smile.

He hadn't disappeared from the paper. I don't know what I'd expected. For the page to be blank, I guess. Like in the movies, where the portrait goes empty when the character steps out of the frame.

But no. He was still in the notebook. And, if nothing had changed during the night, he was also still in my kitchen.

I sat on the bed for a while, the notebook open on my knees, not daring to move. Outside, the wind had dropped a little. It wasn't roaring anymore. It moaned, long and tired, like someone who has screamed all night and has no voice left.

I moved the chair away from the door. Turned the key. The click of the lock sounded enormous in the silence.

I went out into the hallway barefoot, the notebook pressed against my chest.

---

He was sitting in the same chair, by the stove.

The stove had gone out hours ago and he hadn't lit it again. He didn't know how, I guess. He was wearing my fisherman's sweater, the sleeves a little short, and his hands were clasped over his knees.

He was looking at the window. The gray light fell on his face and drew shadows under his cheekbones. He looked tired. He looked like someone who has spent the whole night waiting for something.

When he heard me, he turned his head.

He looked at me. And something in his face loosened, as if he'd been holding his breath for hours and could finally let it out.

"You came back," he said.

"It's my house."

"Right."

He said nothing more. But he didn't take his eyes off me as I crossed the kitchen, and I felt his gaze on the back of my neck, on my shoulders, on my hands, like a soft warmth.

"Did you sleep?" I asked him.

He thought about it.

"I don't know. I've been here."

I lit the stove. My hands were shaking and I had to strike three matches. I put water on to heat. I made coffee, lots of coffee, more than we needed.

He watched me do it. Every gesture. How I unscrewed the moka pot, how I filled the water chamber up to the valve, how I tamped the coffee down with the spoon. When the pot started to gurgle and the kitchen filled with the smell, he lifted his head like an animal catching a scent.

"What's that?"

"Coffee."

"It smells like you."

I froze with the cup in midair. I suppose it was true. I suppose that after thirty-two years of morning coffees, I smelled of coffee. But no one had ever told me like that, as if discovering it.

I poured him a cup. He tasted it and wrinkled his nose, and then tasted it again, and then again, slowly, as if he were trying to understand why anyone would like something so bitter.

"It's like the sea," he said at last. "At first it hurts and then you can't stop."

I hid behind my cup.

Then I sat across from him, like the night before. I put the notebook on the table, between us. Closed.

And then I did the only thing I could think of. What any scientist would do.

Compare.

---

I opened the notebook to Tuesday's page and turned it toward the light. I looked at him. At the drawing. At him.

It was the same face. Not similar. The same.

The same jaw, which had taken me years to find. The same nose, a little crooked to the left, because at fourteen I decided a perfect nose was boring. The same cowlick, right above his forehead, that made one lock of hair always stick up on the same side.

I reached out without thinking and smoothed it down. The lock sprang back up.

He didn't move. He only closed his eyes for an instant when I brushed his forehead. A very short instant. As if my fingers had hurt him, or the other way around.

I pulled my hand back. Cleared my throat.

"Turn your face to the left," I said.

He did it without asking.

I looked for his eyebrow.

At eleven I had drawn him a scar right there, on his left eyebrow. A thin white line that split it in two. I drew it in every notebook for two years, proud of it. At thirteen it seemed like a little-girl thing and I erased it. I never gave it back to him.

His eyebrow was whole. Not a mark. Not a shadow.

I don't know if that relieved me or scared me more.

Now the mouth.

On Tuesday night I had erased the hard line at the corner, the one that had been coming out on its own for years. And I had drawn it again, softer, about to smile.

I looked at his mouth. Slowly. Longer than I should have.

It was the new one.

Not the one from the last ten years. Not the one from all the notebooks I keep in the box in the closet. The one from that night. The one I drew a few hours before someone knocked on my door.

I had to grab the edge of the table.

---

"What's wrong?" he asked.

I didn't answer. I couldn't. I got up, went to my room and came back with the cardboard box from the closet. I set it on the table, between the cups, and opened it.

Twenty-three years of black notebooks. They smelled of old paper, of graphite, of the damp in the closet.

He looked at them with an expression I couldn't read.

I started taking them out and flipping through them fast, going backward. Him at twenty, sitting on a dock. Him at sixteen, in profile. Him with the scar, at twelve, with a wooden sword that made me laugh in spite of everything. Him without the scar. Him at nine, the very first, sitting on his rock, looking at the sea.

Each notebook held a period of my life. I saw it there, with the notebooks open on the table like cards from a deck. The one from the year of college entrance exams, full of nervous clouds and of him studying next to me, with an open book I never finished drawing. The one from Hamburg, the Erasmus winter, with him walking through the snow by the river, his jacket collar turned up, because I was so cold and so lonely that I drew him with me. The one from Madrid, the first year, when everything was lovely, with him almost hidden, tiny, in the corners, as if he were embarrassed to be there.

And then, the one from Madrid in the last few years.

Almost empty. Clouds without him. Whole pages without a single margin filled. It surprised me to see it like that, so clearly, like a gap in a row of teeth. I didn't remember stopping drawing him. I don't know when it happened.

I turned those pages fast.

He said nothing. But when I looked up, he was looking at me, not at the notebooks. And there was something in his eyes that looked a lot like sorrow.

A knot formed in my throat.

And then I remembered the mole.

The small mole below his left shoulder, the one I had seen the night before when he took off his wet shirt.

I looked for it. I searched through every notebook, page by page, for more than an hour, while he watched me in silence and the coffee went cold in the cups. I searched the backs, the shoulders, the quick sketches in the margins.

I didn't find it.

I told myself I must have missed it. That in twenty-three years of drawings my pencil must have slipped at some point. That no one remembers a dot of graphite.

I told myself that several times. I didn't believe it.

"Can I see it again?" I asked him. My face was burning. "The mole. In the light."

He didn't ask why. He stood up, turned around and pulled the fisherman's sweater up his back, slowly, to his shoulder blades.

The gray light from the window fell on his skin. It was a real back. With pores, with fine pale hair at the base of the spine, with a smaller freckle near the waist that I didn't remember either. And there, below the left shoulder, the mole. Dark, round, the size of a lentil.

I moved closer. Reached out a finger. Touched it.

It wasn't graphite. It didn't smudge. It was skin, warm, alive. Under my fingertip I felt goose bumps rise across his whole back, from the nape of his neck to his waist, in a small wave that ran over his skin the way wind runs over water.

He held his breath. So did I.

We stayed like that for a second. Two. My finger on his back and the whole world silent, even the wind.

Then I took my hand away and he pulled the sweater down without a word, and sat down again, and I sat down again, and the two of us looked at the notebooks instead of at each other.

My hands were shaking. I hid them under the table.

---

I picked up Tuesday's notebook again. I opened it to the first page, without thinking.

There it was, in my usual handwriting, the same as in every notebook since I was a girl:

*This notebook belongs to Ada Koch. If you find it, please give it back to me.*

My grandmother taught me to write it, because when I was little I lost them everywhere. On the bus, at the beach, in the school cafeteria. Ever since, I write it in all of them.

I looked at my name.

*Do you know who I am?*

*Ada.*

He hadn't guessed it. It wasn't magic, or not that kind of magic. It wasn't a trick.

He knew it the way he knew the shape of his nose. The way he knew his eyebrow without the scar. Because it was written in the same place he came from.

---

"Tell me what you remember," I asked him. My voice came out very low. "Anything. The first thing you remember."

He closed his eyes. He stayed like that for a long time. Outside, the wind had turned into gentle rain, and you could hear it falling on the roof like sand.

"A rock," he said at last. "I'm sitting on a rock. In front of me is the sea."

My breath caught.

"What else?"

"Clouds. Lots of clouds." He opened his eyes and looked at me, a little lost. "There are a lot of clouds, aren't there? Everywhere."

I almost laughed. I almost cried. I did a little of both, at the same time, covering my mouth with my hand.

"Anything else?"

He hesitated. He looked down at his own hands, open on the table.

"A hand," he said. "A hand that makes me. That makes me slowly. Sometimes it stops for a long time, and I wait. Sometimes it erases me a little, and it hurts, but then it comes back."

He raised his eyes to me.

"It's your hand, isn't it?"

I couldn't answer. I sat there looking at him through blurred eyes, my hand still covering my mouth.

Because I understood.

He didn't know where he came from because I had never drawn where he came from. He didn't know his age because I had never decided it. He had no name because I had never given him one.

He knew what was in the notebook. A rock. The sea. Clouds, heaps of clouds. My name on the first page.

And my hand.

He only knew what I had drawn.

He was what I had drawn.

### Maruxa · Stuttgart, February 1967

For a month we saw each other every afternoon. By the river, if it wasn't raining. In a little café in Feuerbach, if it was, one with crocheted curtains and a fat owner who looked at us as if she knew things.

We understood each other through the oilcloth notebook. He wrote a word in German, I wrote it in Spanish. I drew a boat, he wrote *Boot*. I taught him Galician words that weren't in any dictionary, *morriña*, *saudade*, *orballo*, and he wrote them down very carefully, in his spiky handwriting, and then repeated them so badly that my belly ached from laughing.

The notebook filled up. It was ours. It was the only house we had.

But there was a part he never showed me.

The last pages. Whenever I turned the leaves toward the end, he put his hand on top, gently, and closed it. Without getting angry. He just closed it and changed the subject, with that red face he would get.

I didn't insist. But I was dying of curiosity.

---

One afternoon in February he left the notebook on the café table to go to the restroom.

I shouldn't have done it. I know. My mother would have smacked me.

I opened it at the end.

And it wasn't single words. It was songs.

My songs. Written in his spiky handwriting, the way they sounded, without understanding what they said. My mother's alalás. The chestnut song. My grandmother's lullaby. All written wrong, his way, the way a German hears Galician: *Ala la la, ala la la, kuando tsei lo mar.*

And under every song, a date.

The first was the fourteenth of April, 1966. My third week at the factory. I didn't even remember ever singing that song on the line.

I turned the pages with my hands shaking. April. May. June. Dates and dates and dates. Whole songs, others in pieces, some with only one line. Sometimes a note in the margin, in German, that I couldn't understand.

Nine months. Nine months of my songs, written down one by one, at the far end of the factory floor, his head bent over the paper. What I had thought were my demerits.

The last page was almost full. At the top was a date: *24.12.1966*.

And underneath, in a long list, all the songs I sang that Christmas Eve. All of them. In order. The carols, the alalás, my grandmother's lullaby.

At the end of the list there was a single sentence in German, written more slowly than the others, the letters rounder.

---

When he came back from the restroom he found me with the notebook open in my hands and my face covered in tears.

He stood beside the table. Very still. Red up to his hair.

I couldn't speak. I only pointed to the sentence.

He sat down. He looked at the sentence. He looked at me. And then, slowly, searching for each word in his head like stones at the bottom of a river, he translated it for me.

"She... sings..." He stopped. Searched. "Sings for nobody. And I... am nobody. Tonight."

I took his hand on top of the café table, in front of the fat owner and the two old men playing cards in the corner. I squeezed it so hard I left my nail marks on it.

He had been there. All along. While I felt like the loneliest woman in the world, crying with my arms around my pillow, singing at the top of my lungs in an empty corridor, someone had been listening. Someone writing down every note in a notebook, so it wouldn't be lost.

I have never again felt so accompanied as I did that afternoon, knowing I hadn't been alone for a single day.

Then I asked him about the notes in the margin. The ones in German, beside some of the songs.

He went red again. He turned the pages looking for one, and pointed to it. It was beside my grandmother's lullaby, written down in October.

"Here..." he said. "Here you... sad. You sing sad."

And the next one, beside an alalá from August.

"Here, happy. Letter from home, I think."

It was true. In August I'd gotten a letter from my mother telling me my father finally had the new engine for the boat. I spent the whole day happy, not knowing it showed. Not knowing you could hear it.

He had written it all down. Not just my songs. How I was when I sang them. My good days and my bad days, one after another, with their dates, in a black oilcloth notebook, for nine months.

Someone had been reading me from the inside without my knowing.

I didn't feel afraid. Maybe I should have. A girl alone, far from home, and a man who knew things about her she hadn't told him.

But no. What I felt was something very different. It was like when I was little and got lost at the fair in Padrón and then found out my father had been following me the whole time, from a distance, without saying anything, letting me believe I was brave.

## 8 · Ada

The storm lasted all that day and a good part of the following night.

I don't remember the hours well. I remember loose things, like photos from a roll of film someone had developed out of order.

I remember putting the notebooks back in the box, one by one, with a care I'd never had before. As if each one were now a house with someone inside.

I remember lighting the stove with him watching me, very attentive, following every gesture with his eyes. How I opened the little door. How I set the pine cones underneath and the logs on top. How I blew. When I finished, he crouched beside me and watched the fire catch with the same look of wonder as the night before.

"It burns," he said.

"Yes. It burns."

"You made it burn."

He said it as if it were a miracle. I laughed, and he watched me laugh the way he had watched the fire.

---

At midday I heated a can of lentils on the burner, because I didn't have the head to cook anything else. I served them in two soup bowls and cut some bread. I sat across from him.

He held the spoon the way I hold it. Just like me, with his index finger stretched along the handle, a habit I've had since I was a girl and that my mother tried to break me of for years. I wondered if he had learned it by watching me just then or if I had drawn him that way at some point without realizing.

He tasted the first spoonful and went still. His eyes very wide.

"Is it bad?" I asked him.

"No." He took another spoonful. "It's that I didn't know things had a taste."

I had to look away. I had a knot in my throat over a can of lentils.

He liked the bread even more. He tore it with his hands, into small pieces, and ate them with his eyes closed. I gave him one of the tangerines I'd bought in the village and he took five minutes to peel it, removing every white thread with infinite patience, and when he put the first segment in his mouth and the juice burst inside, he laughed.

It was the first time I'd heard him laugh.

It was a low sound, surprised, a little hoarse. It went straight to my stomach.

I had never drawn his laugh. You can't draw a laugh. But if I could have, it would have sounded like that.

After lunch he went over to the big window, the one that faces the sea, and stood there for a long time.

I had opened the shutter a little to let the light in. Outside, the storm was still going, but you could look at it now. The sky was a dirty gray, low, racing east at full speed. And beneath it, the sea. Still white, enormous, breaking against the cliff in explosions of spray that rose and rose and came apart in the air.

He rested the palm of his hand on the glass.

"I want to touch it," he said.

"The sea? Don't even think about it. It would drag you off in two seconds."

"But it's the sea." He turned to look at me, and for the first time I saw something like impatience in his face, like longing, a desire of his own. "It's the same one as the rock. It is the same one, isn't it?"

I went over and stood beside him. So close that our arms brushed through the wool. Neither of us moved away.

"It's the same one," I told him. "When the storm passes, I'll take you to the cove. I'll teach you to swim."

"Is it cold?"

"Very. Thirteen degrees."

"And you like it?"

"I love it."

He nodded, very serious, as if he had just made an important decision.

"Then I'll like it."

We stood watching the waves, shoulder to shoulder, without talking. Outside, a gull was fighting the wind, motionless in the air, beating its wings without gaining a meter. He followed it with his eyes until the wind tore it loose and carried it off.

I wasn't watching the gull. I was watching him watch it.

I thought it had been a long time since I'd shared a window with anyone.

---

In the afternoon the internet came back for a while, in fits and starts.

I turned on the generator and the monitors, more out of habit than anything. I needed to do something normal. Something that made sense, that had numbers.

The center of the low was over land now, weakening. The worst was over. I sent a short report, without thinking much. No one was going to read it anyway.

He stood behind me. At a prudent distance at first. Then a little closer. So close that I felt the warmth of his body on my back, through the sweater, and smelled his smell. He smelled of salt, of wet wool. And of something like paper.

I had to concentrate very hard on the screen.

"What's that?" he asked. His voice reached me very close to my ear.

"The storm. Seen from above."

"It's a spiral."

"Yes."

"It's beautiful."

I turned to look at him. He said it without irony, with the same simplicity he said everything. His face was lit by the screen, and the low was reflected in his eyes, spinning.

"I think so too," I admitted. "Don't tell anyone."

"Who would I tell?"

I laughed. He didn't. He had asked it seriously.

I opened the iterations. The fifty-one versions of the model, each with its own colored line, all tangled over the map like strands of colored yarn a cat had been unraveling.

"And this?"

"They're versions of the same future." I tried to explain it without technical words. "You give the computer today's data and ask it to calculate tomorrow's weather. But the data is never perfect. There's always a small error. So you run it fifty-one times, changing the data a tiny bit each time. And each time a different future comes out."

"Fifty-one futures."

"Fifty-one iterations. That's what I call them. Everyone else says ensemble members, which sounds like an orchestra. You look at them all together and see which ones are alike and which ones drift apart. That's how you know how much you can trust it."

He stared at the lines for a long time. His brow a little furrowed. With that concentration of his that I was beginning to know.

"And which one is the real one?"

"None of them," I said. "The real one is the one that happens. The others are just possibilities."

He said nothing more. But I saw he was thinking. And I knew, without his telling me, what he was thinking about.

I was thinking about it too.

---

That night I didn't lock my room.

It wasn't that I had decided to trust him. It was that I had understood something. If he was what I had drawn, he could only be what I had drawn. And never, not once in twenty-three years, had I drawn him hurting anyone.

I had drawn him waiting. Looking at the sea. Reading. Sleeping with his head resting on his arm. Smiling, or about to smile.

We sat by the stove, each in our own chair. Outside, the wind was starting to tire. The gusts were longer, farther apart, like the breathing of someone falling asleep. The sea was still roaring, but it wasn't climbing the rocks as high anymore.

Our knees almost touched. Almost.

"I have to call you something," I told him.

He looked at me. The fire painted his face orange and shadow.

"I can't keep saying 'you.' Or 'him.' I'm going crazy." I heard myself say it and laughed, a little. "Crazier."

"What do you want to call me?"

I thought about it.

I thought of real names. Men's names, names from novels, the names of boys I liked in high school. None of them worked. They were all too much. Giving him a real name meant admitting he was real. That he was here. That this was happening.

And I wasn't ready for that. Not yet.

I looked at the dark computer screen, on the other side of the kitchen. The colored lines were still in my head. Fifty-one futures. Fifty-one versions of what could happen.

And one more. The one no one had calculated. The one that wasn't in any model.

"Iter," I said.

"Iter?"

"From iteration." I shrugged, as if it didn't matter. "It's temporary."

He repeated it softly, trying it out in his mouth the way he had tried the lentils.

"Iter."

And then he smiled.

Not all the way. Just the corner. Lifted a little. As if he were about to smile and didn't dare yet.

The smile I had drawn two nights before.

I looked at it. I thought I could spend my whole life looking at that smile. And it frightened me so much to have thought it that I got up to put wood on the fire, even though it didn't need any.

When I sat down again, he was looking at the notebook, closed on the table.

"Will you draw me tomorrow?" he asked.

He said it without anxiety.

"I don't know," I said, and it was true. "It scares me."

"Why?"

Because I don't know what I do to you when I draw you, I thought. Because I don't know if I change you. Because I don't know if I erase you a little every time I use the eraser. Because it scares me to have so much power over someone.

I didn't tell him. I just shrugged.

He looked at the fire for a while. Then he spoke quietly, without looking at me.

"When you don't draw me, I wait." He paused. "I don't mind waiting. It's what I do best."

I felt something break inside me. Something small and old, like a seam that had been pulled tight for years.

Twenty-three years in the margins. Waiting.

"You don't have to wait anymore," I told him.

And I don't know if I was promising it to him or to myself.

---

The day dawned clear.

That's what happens after the big lows. The air is left clean, cold, transparent as freshly washed glass. And the sea, still enormous, turns a blue so intense it doesn't look real.

I woke up on the sofa. I didn't remember falling asleep.

The plaid blanket was on top of me, tucked in snugly under my feet, the way my grandmother used to tuck it in when I was a girl. I hadn't covered myself like that. I lay very still, my cheek against the cushion, and felt a warmth rise from my chest up to my face.

He had covered me.

I imagined it. I couldn't help it.

Me asleep on the sofa, my mouth a little open, probably, and my hair a rat's nest. Him getting up from his chair in the dark, the stove almost out. Coming closer on tiptoe so as not to wake me. Picking the blanket up off the floor, where it must have fallen. Spreading it over me. Tucking it under my feet with those big hands I had drawn so many times and that now, for real, had been so close.

I wondered how long he had stayed looking at me. If he had looked at me the way I looked at him on paper. If he had thought I was pretty, or strange, or simply someone.

I covered my face with the blanket. It smelled of wool, of wood smoke and, very faintly, of rain. Of him.

I stayed like that for a whole minute, breathing in that smell, my heart doing things I hadn't let it do in a long time.

Then I uncovered myself, sat up and made myself look like a normal person.

Iter was by the window. He had opened the shutter and was looking out. The morning light fell full on his face and lit his dark hair with chestnut glints I had never drawn. That I didn't know he had.

My phone buzzed on the table. Once. Again. Again. The signal was back and all the messages were coming in, one after another, like birds returning to the tree after the storm.

Lucía, asking if I was alive. Marcos, with a lighthouse emoji. Three voice messages from my grandmother.

And one more. From the number with no name.

*Ada. Sorry about the other day, that was no way to write to you after so long. I know you don't want anything to do with me and I understand. I saw the storm on the news. I just wanted to know you're okay. I won't bother you again.*

I read it three times.

It was him. The one from the first months. The one who remembered I don't take sugar and waited for me with an umbrella outside the metro. For a second I was glad he remembered me. For the next second I hated myself for being glad.

I didn't answer. I didn't delete it either.

I listened to my grandmother's first voice message with the phone pressed to my ear. *Nena, are you all right? Say something, I can't sleep. Miña rula, say something.*

I answered her with a text. My fingers were shaking a little.

*I'm fine, Abuela. It was long, but I'm fine. Alone and calm.*

I looked at the word "alone" for a long time before sending it.

Iter turned from the window. He looked at me, with the sun behind him, and said good morning in that hoarse voice I was already starting to wait for.

And I decided, right then, that I wasn't going to tell anyone. Not my grandmother. Not Lucía. Not the therapist. No one.

Not for his sake.

For mine. Because if I told someone and that someone looked at me the way you look at a crazy woman, I would start to believe it.

And I didn't want him to disappear.

I hit send.

### Maruxa · Stuttgart, March 1967

It was a year since I'd arrived in Germany, on a sunny Sunday.

The first real sun since October. The Germans came out into the streets like lizards, their faces turned up to the sky and their eyes closed, and the banks of the Neckar filled with families, with bicycles, with children holding balloons. The almond trees in the park had bloomed in a single night, and they were white and pink like brides.

Klaus took me by streetcar to the end of the line, to some hills covered in vineyards I hadn't known existed in Stuttgart. We climbed on foot along a stone path between the vines, still bare. From the top you could see the whole city, the river, the factory chimneys, ours among them, tiny, like a toy.

We sat on a low stone wall warm from the sun. He took bread out of his knapsack, cheese, two apples and a bottle of the local white wine, from those vineyards, that was sour and cold and made me cough.

I was wearing his blue scarf even though it wasn't cold.

We had come a long way with the notebook. We could talk about simple things now, haltingly, mixing languages, with gestures. He knew I had a little sister and a father with a boat. I knew he had been born in a village in the Black Forest, that his mother made the best cherry cake in the world and that his father died in the war when he was two, and that was why he didn't remember his face, only a photograph.

He told me that looking at the city, not looking at me.

---

Then he turned to me, very serious, and asked me something I didn't understand.

He repeated it. I didn't understand that either.

He took out the oilcloth notebook and wrote it, in Spanish, with his usual spelling.

*Wat do dey kol you at hom?*

I sat looking at the question.

At the factory I was María. On my card I was María. For Klaus, in the notebook, in the cafés, by the river, I had always been María, *Fräulein María*, and then just María, with that r that came from the back of his throat.

And he, after a year, wanted to know the other one. The real one. The one my mother used, calling me from the door to come eat. The one my father used when he came back from the sea. The one no one in all of Germany knew.

I don't know how he knew. How he knew there was another one.

*Maruxa.*

### Maruxa · Lake Constance, July 1967

Klaus had it all written down in the oilcloth notebook.

### Maruxa · Stuttgart, November 1967

The Galician Center in Stuttgart was a basement that smelled of octopus, of black tobacco and of bootleg orujo. On Saturday nights it filled up with men from Ourense and Lugo playing cards, women dancing pasodobles with each other, and children running between the tables.

He looked at me. He drew. He looked at me.

It took him half an hour. Outside, night fell. I turned on my phone's flashlight so he could see, and held it over the paper with a trembling hand.

When he finished, he turned the notebook toward me.

It was me.

Badly drawn. With a nose too big and one eye higher than the other. With hair like a storm cloud.

And laughing.

Laughing with my mouth open, my eyes closed, my head thrown back. A huge laugh, overflowing, the kind that doesn't fit on your face. The one from the cove, the day he sneezed. The one from Lucía's green face mask.

I had never seen myself like that.

In thirty-two years, in every photo, in every mirror, I had never seen myself like that. I didn't even know my face could do that.

"This is how I see you," he said.

I told him there, on the floor of the gallery, with the light from the phone between us.

I told him yes. That I had told the jumper-cables man. That there was someone in Madrid who wanted to see me, and that it scared me, and that I didn't know why. And that with him, with Iter, it cost me everything.

He listened without interrupting. When I finished, he was quiet for a while.

"And what scares you?" he asked. "Do you know?"

"Will you tell me someday?"

"Someday."

He nodded. He didn't ask for more.

And I knew, in that moment, that I had just made him another promise. And that this one was the hardest of the three.

We didn't turn on the light.

I don't know who kissed whom in the hallway. Both at once, I suppose. I suppose we were both late.

He pulled the other man's sweater over my head and let it drop to the floor, and he didn't look at it. I took off his salt-soaked T-shirt, still damp, and ran my hands over his cold chest, his shoulders, his back, until I found the mole under his left shoulder. That one was still there.

It wasn't mine. It never had been.

"I'm already making one," he said, very low, against my neck.

And he didn't tell me where.

### Maruxa · Costa da Morte, Today

## 24 · Iter

## 25 · Ada

The twelfth of November dawned calm. Too calm.

I saw it in the models at seven, with my first coffee. A new low, smaller than October's but faster, coming in from the northwest that same night. Gusts of a hundred. Groundswell. The fifty-one iterations agreeing, for once, on the time: ten at night.

And she hung up.

For three years I said it every day. It's taken me a year to stop saying it in my sleep. I'm not going to write it here.

I didn't hear it. Nobody heard it.

Then he turned to me and smiled.

I knew that smile. It was the Madrid one. The one from when something went well for him at work and he'd come home and tell me about it standing up, without taking off his coat.

A folder.

A blue cardboard folder, old, with broken corners and a handwritten label on the cover, in my ten-year-old handwriting.

*Ada's Stories.*

My heart stopped.

He didn't say anything. He held it out to me.

It came wrapped in a pillowcase. One of ours, the gray-striped ones, ironed. The folder was clean. Someone had wiped every page with a cloth. Someone had taped the two broken corners from the inside, carefully, so it wouldn't show.

I'd thrown it in the trash last winter, one night, at three in the morning, without really knowing why. And he had taken it out. He'd cleaned it page by page. He'd kept it a whole year in a closet in Madrid.

Like a hostage, I thought.

This was something I had to do.

"Come back with me, Ada."

Like that. With the first *a* drawn out and the second one falling. The way you talk to a little girl who's stained the tablecloth again. The back of my neck prickled. It wasn't the name. It was the way.

"No," I said.

He raised his eyebrows.

"Aaada..."

"No."

Louder. Loud enough for the old men on the bench to hear. The woman at the fish market stopped weighing sardines. A kid stopped with his backpack half on.

"I'm not coming back. I don't want a coffee. I don't want your things." I kicked the cardboard box. Gently. Just enough to slide it toward his feet. "This I'm keeping." I held up the folder. "It's mine. It was always mine."

And as I said it I knew I was also taking from him the only thing he'd done right. I kept it anyway.

His smile went out.

Little by little. The way the sun goes out behind a cirrus. And underneath I saw, for a second, a face the whole village saw too. That my grandmother saw. That the jumper-cables man saw from his boat, because he stood up.

Then the smile came back. Thinner.

He picked up the box. He put on his navy wool coat and buttoned it all the way up, button by button, unhurried, looking at me.

The way he always waits.

---

I'd prepared a speech. I'd rehearsed it at four in the morning in front of the stove. The part about iterations, the part about the fifty-one futures, the part about how Iter was a word for something that repeats and he didn't repeat.

None of it came out.

"There's a mathematical figure," I said. "A fractal. It's called the Julia set. It depends on a number. You change that number a tiny bit, one little digit, and you get a completely different figure. Like the weather models. You can spend your whole life drawing it and it always surprises you."

He was listening with his brow slightly furrowed. The way he listens to the clouds.

"It's the same with you," I said. "Change one little thing and you're someone else. I've spent a month trying to draw you and you surprise me every morning."

I swallowed.

"Julian."

He went very still.

And then I said what I hadn't rehearsed. What came out of a place I didn't know I had, the same place *you're not real* came out of, but from the other side.

"I'm not putting it on you. I'm giving it to you. The names you put on things are so they won't go away. This one is yours, so you can take it with you wherever you want to go."

He didn't say anything.

His eyes filled.

I had never seen him cry. I had never drawn him tears. Two fell, one from each eye, slowly, down into his beard.

"Julian," he repeated.

Trying it. The way he tried the lentils, the coffee, the tangerine, the word *Iter*. But this time he didn't try it in his mouth. He tried it in his chest. I saw it.

"Julian."

He put his hand on his chest. In the center. Where he once told me the thing I couldn't draw was.

"Then I'm staying," he said.

"You don't have to..."

"I know." He took my hand. "That's why I'm staying."

---

At eleven there was a knock at the door.

Not three knocks. One. And a bark.

I opened it.

The jumper-cables man, in the orange rain jacket, dry now, with a plastic bag in his hand. And Lúa beside him, sitting, very proper, her tongue hanging out.

"Low tide at nine," he said, just like that. "I went down to the rocks in your cove."

He held out the bag.

Inside there were pages. Loose pages, crumpled, soaked, stained with salt and seaweed. Seventeen. I counted them later. Pages from the notebook that the wind had torn out and the sea had left among the stones with the tide.

A cloud. Another cloud. Him at twelve with the wooden sword. Him on the dock in Hamburg. My handwriting on the first page: *This notebook belongs to Ada Koch. If you find it, please give it back to me.*

He had done it. He had found it and given it back to me.

I couldn't speak.

"The man from Madrid spent the night at the Guardia Civil post in Cee," he said, looking at the sea, as if he were giving the weather report. "Breaking and entering. Your grandmother has already gone in to make a statement about the car, and I did the one about the eucalyptus. What happened inside, only you can tell. You need to go to Cee."

"I'll go," I said.

And I said it without clenching my teeth.

---

Julian appeared behind me in the hall. Barefoot. In the fisherman's sweater.

Lúa stood up.

I tensed. I waited for the growl. The raised hackles. The teeth.

Lúa looked at him. She stretched out her neck. She sniffed his feet, his knees, the hand he held out to her slowly, palm up. The one with the mark.

And she flopped onto the floor belly up, legs in the air, at his feet, her tail thumping against the tiles.

The jumper-cables man looked at his dog. Then he looked at Julian. For a long time.

I don't know what he understood. He didn't tell me. I didn't ask him.

Julian crouched down and scratched Lúa's belly. Then he stood up, looked the man in the eye, at the same height, and held out his hand again. Like at the door, three days before.

But this time he said something more.

"My name is Julian."

It was the first time he had said it out loud to anyone but me.

The jumper-cables man shook his hand.

"Thank you for pulling me out of the sea," Julian said. "What's your name?"

And the air went out of me.

Because I had gone a month without asking it. Because he had pulled me up from the bottom of the sea, had carried me in his arms up a cliff, had dressed me in his dead father's sweater, had listened to me tell him what I hadn't told anyone, had given me a carpenter's pencil so I could bring another man back to life. And I hadn't asked him. Not once.

Because asking would have been starting to want to know.

"Brais," he said.

Just that. Nothing more. Looking at Julian, not at me.

Brais.

I hadn't been the one to ask. Now I knew anyway.

And I realized, with a small chill at the back of my neck, that I didn't know what I was going to do with that.

---

He went off down the road with Lúa, on foot, without looking back.

Julian and I stayed in the doorway, watching him go.

"He's a good person," Julian said.

"Yes."

"And he's free."

He didn't say it the way he had on the gallery, with that plain statement of fact that froze my blood. He said it differently. Like someone saying that he is too.

He took my hand. The one with the mark, in mine.

"Wednesday," he said.

"What's happening Wednesday?"

"Your grandmother's making empanada." He looked at me, very serious. "For three."

---

After lunch I sat in the car, in front of the lighthouse, with the keys in my hand. And I didn't start it.

I unlocked my phone. I went to blocked contacts. He was there. No name. Nine digits.

I thought about the blue coat buttoned button by button on the dock. It was just like one his father used to have. His father, who used to leave. He didn't die: he left. He'd take the car one Tuesday and come back a month later, or three, with presents and an explanation that was never the same. He told me about it only once, in Madrid, in the small hours, as if he were confessing to a crime. That as a boy he used to memorize the schedules of everything, the trains, the meals, his mother's shifts, so that nothing would ever catch him by surprise again. That he bought the coat with his first paycheck, because it was the one his father was wearing the last time he came back.

Back then I thought that was tenderness. Later I thought it was something else. In the car, with the phone in my hand, I thought it was both. That we'd both lost a father. I started drawing someone who stayed. He learned to make sure nobody could leave.

And I remembered the Perseids. That first August. I'd told him meteorologists don't look at the stars, because stars don't make weather. And he showed up at three in the morning with a thermos of cocoa and a blanket and took me to a vacant lot in Guadalajara to look at them anyway. He counted forty-two. I fell asleep at thirty. He woke me with a kiss on the forehead and told me he'd made forty-two wishes and every one of them was me.

That was true. That happened.

I loved him. For a while I loved him with everything I had.

I unblocked him.

It was that easy. One finger. The conversation opened by itself, with his last message at the very bottom: *See you on the 12th.* And below it, my *No.* Two letters. All of a sudden they seemed like very few.

I started typing.

*I'm sorry. It's all been so strange. I'm really on edge, I don't know what*

I stopped. I read it. It was my old handwriting. The one for apologizing for things I hadn't done, thumb moving fast, before he got angrier.

I deleted it letter by letter.

I typed it again, shorter. *Can we talk?*

And he would answer in less than a minute, because he always answered in less than a minute. He'd say it was fine. That he took it all back. That he'd wait for me in A Coruña. I saw myself climbing the stairs of the apartment on Calle Ibiza with my suitcase, putting in the key, which would still turn the same way, a little stiff at the end. The gray striped sheets. The dinners with his friends, where everyone laughed at his jokes and so did I, a second after the others. My mother would go back to calling me on Sundays. My mother adored him. *A boy like that, Ada. Difficult as you are.*

My mother, who when my father left shut herself up in her sadness as if in a room with no windows and never came out again, because inside, at least, she knew where the walls were.

It would be so easy. Not because I wanted it. Because I knew it by heart. Because there I knew where everything was, me included.

I looked at myself in the rearview mirror.

My face was puffy from not sleeping and my hair was still stiff with salt. And I had eyes I didn't like. The eyes of someone working out how much it would cost her to give up. I looked ugly. Not because of the dark circles. Because of that other thing. I saw my mother. The same tight mouth. The same eyes doing sums.

I held my finger over *send* for a long time.

Then I thought of him crouched in the grass, looking at the palm of his hand as if it belonged to someone else. Three years at my side knowing I drew, and never knowing whom.

That man didn't know who I was. And I had come close to not knowing it again either.

I deleted *Can we talk?* I blocked him again. I put the phone in the glove compartment. I started the car.

On the way, without quite deciding it, I knew what I would answer if anyone asked me what he was to me.

---

In the afternoon I went to Cee to sign.

I went alone. Julian offered to come and I said no. Not because I didn't want him there. Because this was something I had to do myself.

The post smelled of vending-machine coffee and paper. A young officer, with glasses, sat me down in front of an old computer and read aloud to me what they already had: my grandmother, who had seen the gray car going up the hill; Brais, who had found it stuck by the eucalyptus. Then he asked for mine. I told him. The three knocks. The door. A notebook thrown into the sea from the cliff. He typed it all with two fingers.

"Now I need your details," he said. "And those of the accused. Full name."

I opened my mouth. Nothing came out.

I hadn't said it in months. Not out loud, not inside. I had erased it from my contacts, from my photos, from my emails. I had promised myself never to write it. Not even here. I wasn't going to give him even that.

And now a twenty-five-year-old kid was asking me for it with his finger over the key.

I thought of Julian that morning, his hand on his chest, trying on his name the way you try on a new coat. I thought of Brais, saying his without looking at me. Names are given. Names are received.

And some, I realized, are given back.

"Álvaro," I said. "Álvaro Lasheras Gil."

The officer typed it. Slowly. With two fingers. He asked me to spell it and I spelled it for him, letter by letter, my voice steady.

"Relationship to the accused?"

I didn't answer.

Three years. The blue coat. The folder, cleaned page by page. *I miss you, Ada. So much.*

"None," I said.

The officer looked up from the screen.

"None?" He said it slowly, like a warning. "If he's your ex-partner, it's best to put it on record. It changes things."

"None."

He looked at me a second longer. Then he typed it.

That was all.

The ground didn't shake. The lights didn't go out. He didn't appear in the doorway. It was a name. Three words on one line of a form, between the ID number and the address. An ordinary man's name, the kind there are forty of in any office in Madrid.

I had made the monster myself. By keeping quiet.

I signed with the post's ballpoint pen, which was tied to the desk with a little chain.

On the way out, in the parking lot, with the northwest wind in my face, I said it once more. Out loud. To check.

"Álvaro."

Nothing. The wind took it, the way it takes everything here.

Before starting the car I called Lucía.

She picked up on the first ring, as if she'd been holding the phone in her hand for months. Maybe she had.

"Ada? You never call me."

"I'm fine."

"That's what people say when they're not fine."

"Lucía. I said his name to a Guardia Civil officer. Out loud. And he wrote it down on a piece of paper."

Silence on the other end. A long silence, a Madrid silence, with traffic in the background.

"And?"

"And nothing. Nothing happened."

Lucía burst into tears. And then into laughter. And then both, the way she does, with no transition.

"I'm coming at Christmas," she said when she could. "I don't care if you have room. I'll sleep on the sofa, on the stairs, inside the lamp. And you're going to introduce me to the towel guy properly, because on the video call I only saw half his face and that was already too much."

I laughed. In the parking lot of a Guardia Civil post, in the northwest wind, I laughed.

"Okay," I said.

"Okay what?"

"Okay to everything."

I drove back along the coast road with the window down, even though it was cold. I didn't think of him until Corcubión. And only to realize I hadn't thought of him.

---

### Maruxa · Costa da Morte, September 1971

We were married in the harbor church on a Saturday in September, by the same old priest as always, who had to ask the bishop's permission because the groom was a Protestant, and the bishop took three weeks to answer.

My mother came. In a wheelchair, pushed by my sister, in a blue dress with the holy card of the Virgen del Carmen in her handbag. She cried from the first minute.

My father walked me up the aisle on his arm in his funeral suit, with the face of a man who hadn't slept in three nights. When we got there, he gave my hand to Klaus and said to him, in Galician, in front of the whole village:

"Cóidama, alemán."

Look after her for me.

Klaus didn't quite understand it. He nodded anyway, very serious, his eyes shining.

The ring was the silver one with the little blue stone. The one from the Neckar. He took it out of the pocket of his gray coat, where he had carried it for two years, jingling against his keys. His hands were shaking so much it took him three tries to get it on me.

He didn't sing at the wedding. I made him promise he wouldn't sing.

I sang. At the reception, in the harbor tavern, after the pulpo. My mother's alalá. And he sat very still in his chair, glass in hand, listening to me the way he used to listen to me in the factory, at the far end of the floor, with his head tilted a little.

The photographer took a picture of us as we came out of the church. Just one. He told us to look at the camera.

I looked.

He didn't.

That's the photo that hangs above my sideboard. My granddaughter says it's the most beautiful photo in the world, and that she doesn't understand why her grandfather isn't looking at the camera.

I understand.

He'd gone five years unable to stop looking at me. He wasn't going to start that day.

## 30 · Julian

My name is Julian.

I have said it eleven times since this morning. I counted them. To the dog. To the bathroom mirror. To a gull on the gallery railing, which didn't flinch. To Brais. To Ada's grandmother, at the door of her house, with the smell of the empanada coming out from behind her like a welcome.

Abuela looked me up and down. The beard. The shadows under my eyes. The half-moon on my hand.

"Xa era hora," she said.

And she stepped aside to let me in.

---

The kitchen was small and warm. There was an oilcloth tablecloth with lemons on it. Three plates. Three glasses. A radio on very low, with a man talking in Galician about the state of the sea.

I sat where she told me. Ada sat beside me. Her grandmother, across from us.

She cut the empanada with a knife that was older than me. Much older. She gave me the biggest piece.

"Eat," she said. "Estás moi fraco."

I didn't know what *fraco* was. I knew from the way she looked at me. The same way Ada looked at me at the beginning, when she gave me lentils: like something that has to be held down to the earth with food.

I ate.

It was tuna, with pepper and onion, and the pastry fell apart. I burned my tongue. I didn't mind. It was the first time I had eaten something made by someone who wasn't Ada. The first time someone put a plate in front of me without knowing where I came from.

Or knowing it and putting it in front of me anyway.

Abuela didn't ask anything.

That was what impressed me most. Not where I was from. Not what I did. Not how I had met her granddaughter. She ate without hurrying, filled my glass with wine twice, and over coffee, while Ada washed up, she leaned toward me across the oilcloth and said, very low:

"Ti cantas?"

I didn't understand.

"Do you sing?" she repeated, in Spanish.

I looked at Ada. Ada had gone still at the sink, a plate in her hand, her back to us.

"I don't know," I said. "I've never tried."

Abuela nodded. As if that were a good answer. The best one possible.

"Pois proba," she said. "Que non sexa tarde."

---

We walked back to the lighthouse, with the evening coming down.

Ada was quiet. I took her hand. She squeezed it.

"She asked you about singing," she said.

"Yes."

"She asked my grandfather that once. Fifty-five years ago."

"And did he sing?"

She laughed. For the first time in a week. With her head thrown back.

"Terribly. He sang terribly. But he stayed."

---

That night I waited for her to fall asleep.

It didn't take long. She hadn't really slept in days. She fell asleep in the bed upstairs with the red pencil still in her hand and the page of *The Boy Who Stays* face down on the nightstand, with me drawn on the back.

I took the pencil from her. I pulled the blanket up over her. I went downstairs.

The iron trunk was at the top, in the lantern, under the lamp. The same one where Ada had locked the notebook away with a padlock. It didn't have a padlock anymore. Inside there were things belonging to the keepers who lived here before her. A cap. A logbook with its covers swollen from the damp. And two manuals, in old-fashioned Spanish, with pen-and-ink drawings: *Instructions for the Lighting Service* and *On the Auxiliary Generator Set*.

I had read them all the way through. Three times. On the nights she slept and I didn't.

I think even then I knew that one day I would want to do this.

---

The auxiliary generator for the lamp was in the shed out back. Not the small one for the house, the one Ada turns on every time the power goes out: the other one, the big one, the one nobody had started since eighty-seven. Rusted. With a layer of dust and nests.

I cleaned it. I changed the oil with a can that was on the shelf and smelled old but worked. I bled the diesel. I checked the wires one by one, with the flashlight in my mouth, the way the drawing on page forty showed.

I pulled the cord.

Nothing.

Again.

Nothing.

The third time it coughed. It spat black smoke. It choked.

I sat down on the floor of the shed, my hands black with grease, and realized I was smiling.

Because it had failed. Because nobody had drawn me knowing how to start a 1962 generator. Because I was learning it myself, alone, in the dark, with grease up to my elbows and a wet manual. Because if it started, it was going to be mine.

The fourth time it started.

A low sound, steady, that made the sheet metal shake. The bulbs in the shed flickered. They stayed on.

---

I climbed the hundred and twelve steps of the tower.

I counted them. I had climbed them many times with her, by day, to look at the sea. Never at night. Never alone.

At the top, the lantern. The big lens, of glass cut in rings, like a glass onion the size of a man. Ada had taken its cover off the week before, to show it to me. She said it was the most beautiful thing she had ever seen. She said nobody had lit it since 1987, when they automated the one at Fisterra and this one was left without a job.

I closed the circuit. The way page twelve said.

The bulb inside lit up. First orange. Then white. Then so white I had to shut my eyes.

And the lens began to turn.

Slowly. With a creak of old gears that grew smoother. And the light came out through the glass rings as a beam, a long white arm that swept the sea, the rocks, the cove, the road, the village down below with its four lights, the hill, and came back to the sea.

Once every twelve seconds.

I watched it with my mouth open.

It was the first thing I had done in the world that nobody had drawn me doing.

---

I didn't hear her come up.

I noticed because, in the glass of the lantern, I saw something move behind me. I turned around.

It was Ada. Barefoot, with my fisherman's sweater over her pajamas, her hair a mess, out of breath from the hundred and twelve steps.

She came over to the railing. She watched the light turn.

She had spent a month living in a dark lighthouse. She had spent her whole life living in the dark. I know because she told me, in pieces, at night, by the light of the stove. I know what a cumulonimbus cloud is and I know what a woman who has stopped singing is.

"I lit it so you'd see it when you came back," I said. "But you came back sooner."

"Came back from where?"

"I don't know." I shrugged. "From wherever you were."

A sound escaped her. I don't know if it was laughing or crying. Both.

The beam passed over us. It lit up our whole faces for a second. It went away again.

"Thank you," she said.

"It's not for you." I pushed her hair out of her face. "Well, yes. But not only."

"Who else is it for?"

I looked down. At the village. At a small window by the harbor, the only one still lit at that hour, above the net house.

"For anyone who's looking."

---

We sat on the iron floor, back to back. The generator purred below. The sea breathed. The light turned.

"Someday I'll light it myself," she said, all at once.

"I can teach you."

"No." She shook her head against the back of my neck. "I want to learn it on my own. Like you. With the wet manual and black hands. I want it to start for me the fourth time."

I don't know how she knew about the fourth time. I hadn't told her.

I didn't ask. The same way she hadn't asked Brais's name. There are things you don't ask because asking would be starting to want to know them, and others you don't ask because you already know them.

"Okay," I said.

"Promise me you won't leave it on until then."

"Why?"

"Because the next time it's lit it has to be because I've come back. For real. All the way."

I nodded.

We stayed like that a long time. Until it started getting light behind the hill. Until she fell asleep again, against me, with her mouth a little open.

She snored once. Softly.

---

Before switching it off, I got up to look at the sea one last time from up there.

In the glass of the lantern, with the dawn behind it, I could see the reflection of the tower, the railing, Ada asleep on the floor wrapped in my sweater.

And a silhouette standing behind her. Tall. Still. Looking at her.

I froze.

Then I raised my hand. The silhouette raised its hand.

My reflection. Of course. It was my reflection.

I lowered my hand.

The silhouette took one second longer to lower its own.

One second. Nothing. The glass is old, I told myself. The rings bend the light. I had been up all night.

I switched off the circuit. The lens stopped. The light went out.

I picked Ada up in my arms and carried her down the hundred and twelve steps, one by one, without waking her.

I didn't look at the glass again.

My name is Julian. That is the twelfth time I have said it. Today I said it only to myself.

---

### Maruxa · Costa da Morte, Today

Non durmo ben desde que morreu Klaus. Seis anos.

For fifty-three years he woke up before me. At five, the hour of the boats. And he didn't get up: he lay there watching me sleep, like that morning in Stuttgart. Every time I felt him awake, I shut my eyes tighter and pretended to be asleep, so he could go on looking. I never told him. I think he knew, and pretended he didn't, so that I could go on pretending.

That is how we loved each other. Both of us pretending at once.

The first night without him I came home from the hospital to a bed I had made myself that morning, because Klaus used to say an unmade bed is a house that has given up. I woke at five and there was no one watching me. I felt it before I opened my eyes: the air in the room was still in a different way. I lay there a long time pretending to be asleep. Out of habit. Just in case. Then I got up and made coffee. Two cups, without thinking. I drank them both.

I get up at three, at four, and sit at the kitchen window with the light off, looking at the harbor. The boats. The breakwater. The black hill against the black sky. And on top of the hill, where the land ends, the lighthouse.

Dark. Always dark. Since eighty-seven.

Not that night.

That night, at ten past two, I was at the window with a chamomile tea and I saw it light up. First a little yellow light, like a candle. Then white. Then the arm of light that passed over the sea, over the harbor, over my window, over me.

Once every twelve seconds. I counted.

I put my hand to my mouth.

Klaus told me once that, when he was a boy, his mother would light a candle in the window on winter nights so his father would know where the house was when he came home. That it wasn't necessary. That the street had a thousand lights. But that the one in the window was the only one that was lit for him.

I don't know who lit the lighthouse. I know who lives there.

And I know, because I saw him today in my kitchen eating the empanada with his eyes closed, that the young man with the beard is not of this world. Nor does he need to be. Klaus wasn't either. He came with a dictionary and a gray coat to a village that didn't want him, and he stayed fifty-three years.

I stayed at the window until it got light and the lighthouse went dark.

Then I washed the cup. I got dressed. I went down to the harbor like every morning.

And on the breakwater, facing the sea, with no one to hear me, after six years, I sang.
