## OVERTURE — The Habit of Water


Water began to withdraw without ceremony. There was no warning, no bell. In Tarel, speaking had always come late: things happened first, then remained, and in time became habit.

The city floated on a brackish lagoon so ancient that no one remembered its beginning. Wooden houses, worn ropes, joints made with patience. Children learned the sway before they learned the names of streets; they knew how to return before they knew how to leave. Coming home was a verb: a repeated action, a small habit of the body.

Water was not a backdrop or a landscape. It was the habit itself.

That is why the change did not arrive as a lack, but as strangeness. The oar touched bottom where there had been a meter of water. The boats woke twisted, as if they had doubted all night. The nets came back light; the glances, heavy. Silence expanded, as if something knew it was time to let go.

I was an archivist. Or that is what my appointment said. In reality I looked slowly at what did not yet ask for a name: minimal tides, births without ritual, deaths without announcement, stories that the old spoke to walls when the world pretended not to listen.

I noted the first strip of mud without urgency, like one who corrects an old map without yet knowing what city will remain on the other side of the ink. I made a mistake in the first figure: I wrote that the water had dropped a hand's length, and it was two. It took me a week to realize, and by then I had already copied the wrong dates on three different pages. I did not correct them all. Some remained wrong, and they remain so.

"It was not here before," said Kema, pointing at the dark edge under his house. "The water covered it. The water knows."

"What does it know?" I asked.

"When to let go… and when not to." I wrote it down, though I did not know in what category to file it.

Water withdrew without haste. It did not tear: it said goodbye. It showed rotten docks, mooring rings embedded in mud and marks of past levels that no one wanted to read as a warning.

It was a guest who collects its things while you sleep and leaves the room ready for someone who will not arrive.

Two stories lived in the archive. One said that Tarel had always floated. The other, that we fled toward water after a war or after a thirst so old it no longer hurt. In both appeared the same word, written with different strokes: home.

Months later the delegation from the Dry Continent arrived. Their ships were rigid. Their clothes did not learn to get wet. They walked slowly, as if the ground might answer them back. They asked everything. They listened less than they spoke. They measured the lagoon in silence and spread papers that the wind tried to fold.

They spoke numbers without lifting their eyes. They said the level would drop more. They did not say for how long or at what cost. Someone asked if the water would return. They said they did not know, and they said it with the same voice one uses to say it will not rain.

"You will have to choose," they said. "Stay or leave."

No one answered right away. I took note of the phrase as one takes note of the weather: too general to argue with and too precise to ignore.

Tarel divided without breaking. Some families dismantled their houses with an efficiency that was frightening. They burned dried seaweed. They promised to return. They promised to write. Not all looked back as they left.

Others stayed and learned the language of mud: where to step without sinking, when to wait, what sound announces fracture and what only fatigue. Some stayed adjusting their step, others kept treading the same footprint. The children walked. Some wept the lost buoyancy. Others showed blisters new as medals no one had given them.

The archive changed. It no longer kept only stories of water, but of what remained when water left. Seeds appeared under silt, rusted tools, marks that we did not know how to read as past or as future.

I found an unsigned text, almost erased by salt: "Water sustains us as long as we remember that we also sustain it."

I dreamed of the empty lagoon only once. It was not dead. It slept under layers of earth. It breathed slowly. Upon waking my hands were clenched as if still holding something.

The last message from the Council arrived. They recommended evacuation. They used a word that sounded exact and did not fit in the tongue of Tarel. We decided not to repeat it. Not because it was false, but because it did not know where to dwell.

There was no rebellion and no ceremony. There were small gestures: new ropes between platforms and solid ground, houses that leaned before accepting the weight, old songs sung more slowly to accompany steps where once there had been rowing.

I left gaps in the archive. There are changes that do not admit complete record.

On the last day I put my hands in what remained of the lagoon. It was warm, turbid, salty. I let it run between my fingers. I did not say goodbye.

The water of Tarel returned at night. Without announcement. Without signs.

By morning the inhabitants found the shore where it had always been. The same mud. The same stones. The same line of salt on the low walls.

The papers of the Dry Continent remained in the archive, with their exact figures and already pale ink. They measured a lagoon that had ceased to need to be measured. No one consulted them again. Water does not read maps. No one could say if the water had brought something with it. No one could say if it had left something behind.

## I — The Shape of the Interior



### Essay: Three Images and a Limit


When water began to withdraw from Tarel, its inhabitants sensed that an elemental form of frontier was closing. I understood much later that this same intuition governs our own center. This book is, at heart, the exploration of that hunch taken to its furthest consequence. To venture into this territory, quantum physics is not needed; three images that our daily experience already possesses are enough.

The first is the soap bubble. Upon blowing, the soap stretches, curves and closes upon itself: suddenly there is an inside and an outside that a second before did not exist. What makes the bubble what it is is the membrane that encloses it, not the trapped air. The second is the ocean, a deep mass from which waves emerge to take individual form before being reabsorbed by the same surface. The third is the network, whose nodes only make sense through the threads that bind them: an integrated network is more than the sum of its loose nodes, and to that difference—how much organization adds beyond the parts—the physics of information has given a name, Phi (Φ). The greater the Phi of a system, the more it behaves as an inside and not as a simple collection of pieces.

The thesis came to me late and brought no comfort: consciousness is the interior of an encapsulated informational domain. There exists a frontier that separates two regimes of access, not two substances. On one side, everything that can be measured from outside: the neurons that fire, the neurotransmitters, the words. On the other, what it feels like to process it from within: the redness of red, the texture of an exact thought. Within this map, nothing crosses that frontier.

It is not a casual claim. One can know every single neuronal firing, each molecule, each connection in another's brain, and still not know what it is to see red from within those eyes—just as one can memorize all the physics of color without ever having seen red. That margin that no external description closes, however complete, is the frontier. It is not that we have simply not crossed it yet for lack of better instruments: it is that crossing it does not belong to the class of things that an increase in information can solve. Data adds precision to one side of the frontier; it never moves anything to the other.

Thought is not found under the microscope because this instrument is only designed to observe the public interface. The private state is, by construction, on the other side of the frontier. To ask an electrode to capture a subjective experience is like asking an outside observer of a black hole to read a letter that has fallen into its interior: the information is real, but the observation point is geometrically wrong.

Encapsulation is the price a system pays for its own complexity—nothing accidental about it. To process without dissolving in environmental noise, it needs to close behind a clean interface. An interior can only emerge, and sustain itself in time, if something separates it.


### Fiction: The Thief of Interiors


It was said of him that he could open anything.

It was simple fact, not borrowed boastfulness. Doors that had been sealed for generations, coffers whose owners had died without opening them, boxes within boxes within boxes… everything yielded sooner or later to his hands, which knew how to listen to what a mechanism wanted before the mechanism knew it was saying it. He did not steal from greed, but because he could not bear something being closed while he remained outside.

One day someone told him of a box at the end of a house at the end of a city at the end of a road. It was said that inside was a treasure that no thief had managed to reach, not because the box was stronger than the others, but because it had seven locks, each of a different kind, and none opened with what opened the one before.

He decided to go.


### I. The First Lock


The first lock was one of strength.

It was what everyone expected, what any thief knows how to force: a mechanism of metal and resistance that yields when you apply more pressure than it was designed to withstand. The thief studied it one night, applied his lever at the exact angle, and the lock gave way with a dry snap, the kind of thing that only knew how to say no until someone insisted hard enough.

He smiled. If they were all like this, it would be a short night. But, as he suspected, not all were.


### II. The Second Lock


The second lock was one of patience.

There was nothing to force; quite the opposite: it was a mechanism that only opened if you turned it slowly, so slowly that any hurry would jam it again from the beginning. The thief, accustomed to quick dexterity, had to learn something else. He spent three days turning a wheel one degree per hour, undoing in a tremble the work of an afternoon and starting again, until on the third day his hands learned not to want to arrive. The lock opened when he stopped being in a hurry to open it.

It was different from the first, which had demanded what he had in abundance. This one demanded something he did not know he lacked.


### III. The Third Lock


The third lock was one of silence.

It jammed with noise. Any sound—the scrape of a tool, the very breath, the heartbeat if listened to too closely—would close it. The thief had to become quiet as he had never needed to become. He learned to breathe without the breath being heard, and to be without making the noise of being. And in that silence, for the first time in years, he heard things his profession had always covered up: that his hands trembled though he did not, that there was an old weariness underneath his skill. The lock opened when there was no longer anyone around his hands making noise.


### IV. The Fourth Lock


The fourth lock was one of renunciation.

It held a simple and cruel trap: it held two bolts that had to be opened at the same time, and he only had two hands, so to reach the second he had to let go of the tool with which he had opened the three previous locks. The tool of his father, the one that had made him a thief. He had to drop it into the well that opened beneath the box, without guarantee that there was anything on the other side of the gesture.

He held it for a long time, and finally he let go. He heard it fall, and heard it not touch bottom, and the lock opened with the hand he now had free.


### V. The Fifth Lock


The fifth lock was one of memory.

It asked for a name. Not just any name: the mechanism only yielded to the name of someone whom the thief had loved and left behind for his profession. He had several to choose from, and he chose the one that weighed most heavily. He said it aloud in that empty house, and hearing himself say it was worse than letting go of the tool, because the tool falls and that is that, and the name, meanwhile, returns.

The lock opened, but he was left a while with the name in his mouth, which does not close as easily as the bolt.


### VI. The Sixth Lock


The sixth lock had no keyhole.

The thief looked for it everywhere: there was no slot, no wheel, no bolt, nothing his hands knew how to question. He spent a whole day running his fingers over a smooth surface that offered no way in. And little by little he understood that this lock did not open from outside: that the only way to defeat it was to stop trying to open it, to accept that there was a part of that box that was not accessed by forcing, and to cross through it without forcing, trusting. He withdrew his hands and stood still in front of the smoothness. Only one remained.


### VII. The Seventh and Last Lock


The seventh lock opened with a bare hand.

It asked for no tool, no patience, nothing of what had come before. It asked only that he place his open palm upon it and wait. He placed his bare palm on the wood—the same palm that had forced so many doors—and waited. He felt, under the skin, something that beat like living things beat, and for an instant, before the end, he had the certainty that on the other side of that last sheet was not an object: that whatever the house guarded behind seven locks was not a thing that could be removed and carried away.

So he pressed his palm, and the lock gave way, and the box disappeared. It did not open: it disappeared. In the exact instant the last frontier surrendered, the entire box—the metal, the seven conquered locks, whatever was inside—ceased to be, as if never. There was no hollow in the air, no mark on the table, nothing to steal, because there was no longer an inside from which to take it.

The thief was left with his palm open over the place where the box had been. He did not cry out, did not curse: he stood very still, just as a few minutes before in front of the smoothness, and understood—without words, the way things that cannot be said are understood—why it had disappeared.

The treasure was not inside the box. The treasure was the inside.

The thief who could open anything had finally opened it all, and by opening it completely he had made it disappear.

He lowered his hand and left the house at the end of the city at the end of the road with empty hands, which in some cities is the only honest way to return from certain places.

He had stolen nothing, but he had understood something, which is the opposite of stealing: you do not take a thing that belonged to another, you are left with something you cannot show anyone.


### Fiction: The Boy Who Learned to Measure Time


I am eleven years old and this summer never ends.

It is not that the days are longer. My father says June has the longest days of the year, and then they get shorter, and he says it every June as if it were news. It is something else. Inside each afternoon fit more afternoons than fit in an afternoon. I do not know how to explain it. I only know how to count it.

I have a notebook under my mattress, because if my sister finds it she will laugh. Inside I note times that are not clock time: shadow-time, ant-time, time-waiting-for-something-to-arrive. I do not mix them. Today's ant-time was long, because the ant was carrying something white and took a long time deciding which way up the curb.

Mr. Montserrat lives next door, has an olive tree that covers his whole patio and a wooden chair where he sits with a book he almost never reads. Today I asked him how long summer lasts.

"Ninety days," he said.

"No. How long it lasts."

He looked at me a moment.

"For me, less than before."

It was not the answer I wanted. But something tells me it is the right one.

He taught me a game with no name: sit under the olive tree, close your eyes, wait for something to happen. Nothing in particular. I wanted a measurable answer—how long do we wait?—and he said "as long as it takes," which bothered me a little, but I sat anyway, on the ground, which today smells of dry earth.

A bird landed on the lowest branch. I heard it move, its small weight making the wood creak. I did not see it—my eyes were closed—but I heard it move for a long time, and then I heard it leave, and then there was silence, and the silence also lasted. "How long did it last?" I asked him.

"How long did it seem to you?"

"A long time."

"Then it lasted a long time."

That was not the answer I wanted either. Again, the right one.

Today a storm came that was not scheduled: clouds suddenly at five, when there were still three hours of light left. Mr. Montserrat did not move from his chair. He looked at the olive tree moving its branches in a way I had never seen it do, as if the tree also knew what was coming. We both got wet. It seemed not to matter to him. To me, it did not matter either.

It lasted ten minutes. Then the sun came back as if nothing, just as Mr. Montserrat seemed to have known all along. I did not write it in my notebook. There are things you keep somewhere else.

Mr. Montserrat died this morning. My mother told me, without warning, the two of us sitting in the kitchen. I think he already knew. Those who spend afternoons looking at the sky with that calm usually know those things before anyone tells them.

The olive tree is still there. That happens with olive trees.

This afternoon I went to sit under it alone, eyes closed, waiting for something to happen. I did not know what. A long while passed with nothing happening, and then the wind moved a branch, and I thought maybe that counted.

I did not write it down. But I kept it.

More than thirty years have passed. I asked my mother again, at an August table, with iced coffee sweating in the glass.

"How long did summer last when I was eleven?"

She thought about it. "Three months," she said. "Like all of them."

"No. How long it lasted."

She looked at me the way you look at a son who has asked the same thing for thirty years.

"As long as you wanted it to," she said finally. "Back then it lasted as long as you wanted it to."

That was not it either. I told her. She shrugged.

"Well, I do not remember anymore."

None of the three answers was the one I was looking for. Maybe that is why I keep all three.

## II — The Time of the Bond



### Essay: Subjective Density


Anyone who has waited for the results of a medical test knows that an hour does not always last an hour. Time is a property that emanates from its rate of integration—how much new life is incorporated per unit of clock—not an external parameter that the horizon simply registers. When integration accelerates, external time seems to stop; when the system fragments, the hours pass like an empty blink.

David Eagleman, neuroscientist at Stanford, decided to prove it in the most literal way possible: he dropped a group of volunteers from a thirty-meter platform with a counter on their wrist that showed numbers too fast to read under normal conditions. If extreme fear truly slowed time down, as everyone swears happens in a fall, they would have read them. They could not. What slows down is not the clock: it is memory, which during panic records with a much greater density than usual. When remembering the fall afterward, there are more frames per second, and that is why it seems to have lasted longer than it did.

That same desynchronization, seen in reverse, has a clinical name. Those with Parkinson's who take three minutes to cross a room do not feel those three minutes: their internal horizon still orders movement at the same rhythm, but the body no longer executes the command at that speed. Nothing suggests the internal clock has broken. It seems, rather, to have uncoupled from the body it was supposed to obey. Later, in a watchmaker's shop, we will return to this uncoupling from within.

In the bond there are two states that are worth not confusing: entanglement, which happens to us through mere proximity, and recognition, which is the voluntary reorganization of one's own inside to harbor the perspective of the other. Undoing the second—the unexpansion—has a precise cost: the network does not return to what it was. The horizon cannot shrink to its original size without tearing the nodes that had grown accustomed to another's presence, and there remain threads that tie nothing but also cannot be withdrawn.

Under this light, grief reveals itself as a permanent architectural alteration, not as a psychological process of acceptance or a sequence of temporal stages. Grief is an architecture with a hollow. The other has disappeared from the public interface, but the space they occupied in the internal design remains open, exposed to the elements. It is not about "getting over" the loss, but about learning to inhabit a structure that preserves the exact shape of an absence.


### Fiction: The Music That Remained in the Room


You do not know when you started humming it. A Thursday in December surprises you with the coffee pot in your hand: four notes, the one that goes up, the one that goes down, the silence, another time. You turn off the faucet and stay still, listening to yourself, trying to figure out where that came from that comes out of you without you calling for it.

It comes from Clara. You know that, even though you never knew what it was called, what she hummed while cooking. In twenty-two years of marriage you never asked her—not because you did not care, but for the same reason you do not ask the name of a bird that has been singing in your garden for a while: because it is already yours in another way.

Now that she is gone, you hear it. Not always. Only when the afternoon falls a certain way, or when the water in the pans sounds like it should sound. The first months you would turn off the extractor and listen, sure it was the neighbor or a radio. Nothing. Only traffic from below.

You left Clara's things where they were: the coat by the door, the book with the bookmark on the page she did not finish, the hand cream almost empty that you have not touched again. Not because you did not know what to do with them. Because it was not yet time.

You searched for the melody the way you search for problems worth solving: you hummed it in apps that recognize music, described it in a forum—something from the forties, four notes that go up and one that goes down, sad but not dramatic. They suggested twenty-three songs. You listened to all twenty-three. None of them was it.

And then, without deciding to, you stopped searching. One morning the search was no longer among the things you had to do that day, and it stayed that way.

A Thursday in November, going down the stairs, you heard it again: it came from 2B, between voices and the smell of food, those four exact notes. You stopped on the landing with your hand on the banister. You did not call. You did not know what you would have said if they opened the door. The notes repeated one more time and then cut off, asked for by a voice that wanted something for dinner. You kept going down.

In January, eating with your brother-in-law, he asked how you really were, and for the first time you chose the honest answer instead of the manageable one.

"There is a melody that Clara hummed when cooking. I do not know what it is called. Now I hum it."

"And?" he said.

"I do not know. Just that."

It is still there, with you, when you wash dishes or wait for the bus or shave. It comes out of you with the naturalness of things the body keeps without asking. You no longer search for it. You know Clara hummed it for years and now it is yours the same diffuse way the apartment is yours, or the kitchen drawer that only she knew how to open: not because you chose it, but because it remained.

It was enough. Or it was not enough, but it was what there was, which sometimes amounts to the same thing.


### Fiction: The House of Dogs That Spoke


RECORD — house of Luisa, edge of the city, garden with trees. She did not collect abandoned dogs because they were ugly or difficult. She collected the ones that were running out of time: the ones the vet describes with technical words and the owner hears with the face of someone who knows what is coming but needs someone else to say it first. They arrived with their blanket, their bowl, their habits, and stayed for the time they had left.

People in the neighborhood said the dogs spoke to her. They did not produce words. Luisa understood them, simply, in a way that did not seem ordinary. She knew when they were cold before they showed it, and when it was time to let the thing end with dignity, instead of with tubes and monitors and the face of someone who does not know how to tell you it is already too late.

ARTUR. Labrador, thirteen years old, cinnamon color. Lived his entire life in a city apartment until the family grew and he became too big for the space and too old for the children, which is nobody's fault or everybody's fault, divided until it stops mattering. Arrived with his box: blanket, bowl with his name in marker, three unused toys. He chose the corner by the window, from where he could see the trees and the afternoon sun when it reached that angle. Time remaining: four months.

Note: the vet gives margins, not certainties. But the margin is enough to start saying goodbye before it ends, a luxury that grief for people does not always grant.

ONA. Small female. Fear not of specific things, like Artur with firecrackers, but of the world in general, with the constancy of someone who learned early that it is wise to stay alert. First days under the kitchen table, not coming out; Luisa left the food nearby without forcing her. The afternoon she came out made no noise. She did not run toward the plate or toward Luisa: she was simply there, by the chair, when Luisa looked up from her cup, as if she had decided without notice that this was, finally, a place where one could be.

Note: do not point out progress out loud. Pointing it out turns it into expectation, and Ona had had enough of that. Time remaining: eleven months. Last recorded residence: the bed. OBSERVATION, without its own file: the man in the house across the street, who sees boxes with used leashes leave every few months headed for the trash, believes there is something monstrous in loving so many times knowing how it ends. Luisa neither denies nor confirms it. There is no record that they ever spoke of it.

CLOSING OF FILE — ARTUR. Died in November, in the corner by the window, with the afternoon sun in its place. It was the kind of death the living envy in silence, with nothing dramatic about it. Luisa was with him, without doing anything special. She was simply there. She kept the blanket, the bowl, the three unused toys. She planted something small under the tree he preferred.

Last entry of the file, repeated each time it is needed: there is always another.

## III — The Workshop of the Bond



### Essay: The Wood That Resonates


Nothing this book holds argues against the bond; what it argues against is fusion. Two interiors cannot exchange their contents: no thought crosses the frontier in its private form. And yet we affect each other. The solution that life found is the same one it found in music: resonance. A string that vibrates does not deliver its vibration to the wood; it induces it. The wood responds with its own voice, made of its own history—the grain, the knots, the years of drought the tree crossed. What reaches the listener is the response of one structure to the vibration of another—never the interior of the string.

That is why the craft that best illustrates the bond is that of the luthier, who never enters. A luthier works their whole life over closed boxes. They cannot put their hand in the resonance; they can only calibrate the wall—its thickness, its curve, its tension—so that the interior sounds without breaking. Let us call empathy that thickness. Calibrated too thick, nothing from outside moves us; too thin, any alien vibration takes our voice. And we do not choose that calibration: it was done early, in the first synchrony with whoever held us, when there was still no I that could have opinions about its own thickness.

There remains then a workshop question, not a philosophical one: can a wall already built be recalibrated? The stories that follow do not answer it. They put it on the counter. I do not always get the calibration right: sometimes I calibrated wrong, and the instrument broke in the hands of whoever carried it with more care than I knew how to give.


### Fiction: The Luthier's Shop



### I. The One Who Entered Backward


The first time Marta entered my shop, she did it backward. Not in an elegant or calculated way: she pushed the door with her shoulder because she had too many things in her arms and did not want to put any down. She entered like that, looking at the street, and then she turned and saw me sitting behind the counter with a file in my hand and the face of someone who had been waiting for exactly that all morning.

"Is this it?" she asked.

"Depends," I said.

"They told me you made special instruments."

I put the file on the counter with care, as if it had opinions about where to rest.

"I make instruments," I said. "The adjective is up to the client."

She looked around. The shop smelled of wood and something harder to name, like when it rains on dry earth.

"Can I ask you something first?" she said.

"You have already. You came through that door backward to my shop."

I took the tape measure from the drawer and moved around the counter.

"For whom?" I asked.

"For me. And for someone else."

I nodded: it was exactly what I expected. I measured the space between her and the door. Then the space between her and the nearest shelf. I put the tape away.

"How long has it been since you knew how to speak?" I asked.

She opened her mouth, closed it, counted mentally.

"About three years."

"Good. That is manageable."

I returned to the workshop without explaining what was manageable or why the opposite would not have been.

Three weeks later I called her. She entered frontward this time. The instrument on the counter was small, unexpectedly small to fill three years. "It does not have to fill it," I told her. "It just has to give the starting frequency."

She took it with both hands. Something in the weight, or in the temperature of the wood, gave her a sensation she could not name until the next day: it was recognition. Not of the instrument, which she had never seen. Of the rhythm it was going to sustain.

I watched her leave from the counter. I picked up the file. On the table I had a notebook with a single line, written years before and I no longer remember why: "What water leaves when it withdraws does not always have a name."

Three years is manageable.


### II. The Notebook of Grievances


The second client arrived with a list of grievances: twenty years, written by hand in a spiral notebook, placed on the counter with the solemnity of someone presenting evidence before a tribunal.

"I want an instrument to forget someone," he said.

I looked at the notebook without opening it.

"Are they alive?" I asked.

"He died six months ago."

"And yet you still bring it written?"

He looked at the notebook as if he had just realized what he had done.

"I have been carrying it written for twenty years. Habit."

I moved around the counter and walked slowly around the client. I did not measure him. I just walked.

"What did he do?" I asked. "Your neighbor. What exactly did he do that bothered you?"

He pointed at the notebook.

"It is all there." "I prefer you tell me."

He told me everything: the music until two in the morning, the garbage bags the day before trash day, the visits that parked in his spot, the wooden chair on parquet that dragged every morning at exactly six-fifteen.

"And now?" I asked when he finished.

"Now I still hear the chair. At six-fifteen. Even though he is no longer here."

"What time do you wake up now?"

He took a while to answer.

"Six-fifteen."

It did not need saying. Twenty years is a long time to learn someone's schedule, and the body does not ask if it still needs to know it.

"Do you know the difference between a ghost and a memory?" he asked.

I thought about it.

"Ghosts do not know they are dead."

"Exactly. Mine does. That is the worst part."

I opened a drawer I had not seen before and took out something small, without immediately recognizable form.

"This one does not connect with anyone," I said. "It connects with what comes after someone. It is different."

He took it, paid, left. I never opened the spiral notebook. It was not necessary.


### III. The One Who Did Not Know How to Name It


The third client stood by the door for an unusually long time. He was not looking at the instruments. He was looking at the floor. "Can I help you?" I said, without looking up from what I was doing.

"I do not know," he said.

I looked up. It was the first time someone had answered that to that question.

"What brings you?" I asked.

He chose his words like someone crossing a river stepping on stones.

"There is someone. We have known each other for years, and in the last one I have noticed that something has changed. I do not know if what I feel has changed or if I only notice it now. And I do not know how to call it."

"What do you call it now?"

"Friendship. Out of habit."

"What name would you give it if not for habit?"

Pause.

"I do not know."

"Yes you do," I said, without cruelty, as one who states a fact. "The problem is not that you do not know what it is. It is that you already know and you are afraid of what it costs to name it."

"What does it cost?"

"That ask yourself. What would you lose, if you named it?"

He took a while to answer.

"If I name it, and she is part of what I am… losing her would not be losing someone. It would be losing part of me."

I said nothing. It was not necessary.

"That is too much," he said.

"Generally it is."

I went to an area of the shop he had not seen, where the instruments are less recognizable. I put one on the counter. "This one shows the geometry as it is," I said. "Not as you would wish it to be. If there is something on the other side, you will find it. If there is nothing, you will know that too."

"And if I prefer not to know?"

"Then do not use it. But it is already here."

He took it. He left. I looked at the hollow in the shelf: it was the last one of that kind I had. I would have to make another.


### IV. The One in a Hurry


The fourth client arrived in a hurry and left with more. He did not wait for me to finish with someone else. He planted himself by the counter with the posture of someone who knows what he wants and only needs to be given it.

"What are you looking for?" I said, without hurrying.

"Something that works fast. To feel I am in the right place. Even if just for a moment."

"How often do you need that moment?"

"More and more."

"And does it last less each time?"

He did not answer. The answer was in the silence.

I went slowly to the back and took longer than he expected. I came back with nothing.

"I do not have what you are looking for," I said.

"Why not?"

"How long did that moment last before?"

"An afternoon. A whole night, sometimes."

"And now?"

The silence again as an answer.

"What you want is not sold here," I said. "It was sold. Not anymore. "So what do I have?"

I looked at him a moment longer than necessary.

"You have to start measuring differently."

"Does that have a fix?"

"It has recalibration. Which is slower and harder than a fix."

He left without buying anything. I looked at my empty hands and returned to the bench, to continue with what I had in my hands. Or recalibrating it.


### V. Thin Wood


The fifth client closed the door with excessive care, as if he feared the latch would break something. He closed his eyes, breathed in the smell of wood and dry dust.

"There is silence here," he said.

"There is normal shop noise," I said.

"No. There is silence of the other kind. The kind that weighs."

"Come closer."

He advanced. I stood still, looking at him.

"What are you hiding from?" I asked.

"From everyone. I enter a room and I fill it. If someone is sad, my chest gets heavy. If someone is afraid, my heart races. And I have no room left for myself."

"And now?"

"Now I come home and I do not know which of the voices I hear in my head is mine."

"Your wood is too thin," I said. "That is not a defect. It is a property. But at your volume, it is going to break you."

"Give me a wall then. A stopper."

"You cannot thicken a finished instrument. If I build you a wall, you will stop feeling others' weight. You will also stop feeling yours. Absolute silence is the instrument's death."

I opened a lower drawer and took out a dense tuning fork, almost black in color, as if it had spent a long time under water.

"Take it."

He took it. I took it back and tapped it gently against the edge of the counter. A grave note, brief. I saw the vibration travel up his feet, up his spine, to his breastbone.

"This will not isolate you," I said. "It will remind you what your frequency is when everyone else's shouts too loud."

"What if I lose it?"

"Then you will not need it anymore. By then you will know how to do it without it."

He paid and left. I returned to my stool, to a piece of dark wood I had been smoothing for weeks to add, inside, a small mass of lead. Without that weight it could not serve its next owner, who had not yet appeared but I already knew would appear.


### VI. The Owner of the Weight


She appeared in spring, with a baby sleeping against her chest and the face of someone who has not slept two hours in a row for months.

"How old?" I asked.

"Four months."

"And you?"

She looked at me. It was clear that no one had asked her that question in four months.

"I do not know," she said. "Everyone told me this came naturally. I look at him, I care for him, I do everything there is to do, day and night, without failing once. But it is like playing behind glass. I hear him cry and I know what is wrong before anyone, and even so I do not reach. "

"New instruments do not tune themselves," I said. "They tune against another. Someone has to give the first note and sustain it until the other finds it."

"Who gives the note?"

"You."

She laughed a short, joyless laugh.

"I have no note. For four months I have not even known where I am."

"That is something else. Your note is there. What you do not have is weight to sustain it."

I went to the workshop and came back with the dark wood instrument. It sounded against the counter with a heavier blow than its size promised.

"It is heavy," she said, taking it, the baby still sleeping against her chest.

"It has lead inside. I put it before I met you."

The weight traveled down her arm, through her shoulder, to her feet, and something in her sway became slower. The baby moved, did not cry, repositioned itself looking for with its head, and fell back asleep in a rhythm barely different from before and completely different.

"What happened?" she asked.

"You knew where you were. Babies do not look for a perfect mother. They look for a stable frequency where they can return."

"How did you know the weight? Before meeting me."

"The weights are all similar. What is hard is the wood around it. And you provided that."

She left with the heaviest instrument in the shop weighing, in her arms, almost nothing.


### VII. The Viola of the Hollow


The last client of the year brought a viola in a violin case, wrapped in a wool sweater to compensate for the difference. She opened it with the care one opens something that has not been opened in a long time.

"It was my son's," she said.

I did not ask. There are past tenses that say it all.

The crack ran across the top, from the bridge down, with darkened edges: the wood had worked around the wound for years, closing it its own way, without help.

"How long?" I asked, and we both knew I was not asking about the crack.

"Nine years."

I lifted it toward the light, turned it slowly, tapped the top with my knuckle.

"And why now?"

"Because I can."

"I can close the crack," I said. "Spruce splint, hot glue, a week of clamping. But you have to know something: it will not sound like before. The repaired wood vibrates around the repair. The crack stops being a wound and becomes a structure."

"And you cannot…?" she started, and did not finish, because she already knew the answer.

"No. No one can."

She looked at the viola for a long time. A bus passed; the shop trembled slightly and the hanging instruments each answered with their note, a minimal, disordered chord.

"Do it," she said.

She came back in ten days. I handed her the viola without ceremony. The crack was still visible, a lighter line, honest, without disguise.

"You did not cover it?"

"I closed it. Covering it is another craft, and it is not mine. She sat on the stool by the window, clumsily put the viola on her shoulder, and drew the bow across a string. A single note. Long. It did not sound like before: graver in the center, with a new edge, like a voice that has wept and no longer hides it.

"It is not him," she said.

"No."

"But he knows it."

I did not answer. There are diagnoses only the client can make.

She left with the viola in the wrong case, with her wool sweater. I picked up the broom and swept the day's shavings, which is what a workshop does with what is left from repairs: it does not keep everything. Good instruments, all of them, work to stop being necessary.

## INTERLUDE — the edge we cross each night


There is a researcher who has spent years collecting testimonies about the edge.

Not the edge in a metaphorical sense. The literal edge, the one each night: the seconds or minutes that mediate between being awake and being asleep. The transition. The moment when the system that carries the day lets go and something different takes over, or does not take over exactly, but the taking over is no longer necessary because the one who was taking over is no longer there.

She calls it the edge because no other name seems precise enough to her. Transition is too clinical. Threshold has architectural connotations that do not quite fit. The edge describes what it is: a limit that is crossed, each night, in both directions. Without anyone knowing quite how.

What she has collected, in years of asking, is this:

Mireia, 34 years: I feel like I am falling. Not toward any specific side. Just downward, toward myself, and at some point in the falling there is no longer anyone who keeps falling.

Tomàs, 67 years: I see things. Images I have not asked for and that make no sense in relation to each other. A hand on a table. A bird I have never seen. A number. The images do not frighten me but I do not understand them either. I let them pass.

Noa, 8 years: I get a fright and wake up. Then I fall asleep again. That happens several times. In the end I sleep for real.

Àngels, 51 years: I do not notice anything. I am awake and then I am asleep and between the two things there is nothing I can remember.

Jorge, 29 years: It is the best part of the day. The moment when it stops mattering what I thought before.

Not all testimonies fit equally well with what she believes is happening. Jorge's case makes her uncomfortable a little: he does not seem to practice anything, he simply likes that moment, and she does not know if that confirms her hypothesis or if she is stretching it beyond what the data allows. She wrote it anyway, without forcing it into the theory. She preferred to leave the discomfort noted than to solve it badly.

The researcher has a hypothesis she has not published because it is not the kind of hypothesis one publishes.

Her hypothesis is that the edge is the practice.

Not practice of anything in particular. Practice of the edge itself: the repeated experience of letting go. Of allowing the system that maintains the continuity of the I—tomorrow's plans, the proper name, the list of what is lacking, the thread that connects the I of ten minutes ago with the I of now—to relax enough for something else to happen. Another thing that has no precise name because the one experiencing it is not in a position to take notes at that moment.

We do it each night. We have been doing it our whole life. Most of the time we do not notice it. She notices it because she studies it. And studying it has changed what she experiences at the edge.

She gets to bed, closes her eyes, waits. The moment comes when the images start—hers are landscapes that fold, or the invented memory of a house with too many doors—and then she knows she is close. That the edge is a few meters away.

What she does is stay still. Not resist, not rush. Just be in the moment when two things coexist: thought still connected and images that no longer belong to thought. Both at the same time, briefly.

She does not always manage it. Sometimes thought wins and she returns to the day, to the list, to the thread. Sometimes the images win and she is already asleep before she notices it.

When she does manage it—that second of both at the same time—she describes it as the quietest moment of the day. Not empty. Quiet in a different way: as if everything that is her is there, complete, gathered, before reorganizing into something more diffuse. What she does not know, and what is the question that keeps her in the project, is what is, in reality, what returns in the morning.

Not in a philosophical sense. In a practical sense: the person who wakes up at seven-fifteen has the same memories, the same name, the same incomplete plans as the one who fell asleep at eleven-thirty. Continuity seems intact.

But between eleven-thirty and seven-fifteen there was something that was not that person, or was that person in a completely different way, and there is no record of it. Not because it did not happen. But because the one who could record it was not available to do so.

Each morning we return from a place we cannot tell anything about. And we do it with the naturalness of things that have always been that way. That they are does not make them any less strange.

The last testimony the researcher collected for her file was from an eighty-two-year-old woman who did not want to give her name.

Why is this moment of interest to you? the researcher asked.

Because it is the only moment of the day when I do not have to be myself, the woman said. The rest of the day I have to be myself. That moment I do not.

The researcher wrote it down. Then she thought about it a while without writing it down. Then she also wrote this down: that some answers are too exact to keep asking questions on top of.


### Brief Essay: The Interface of Causality


The edge of each night does not fit on a single-axis scale. For a long time consciousness was thought of as a vertical line: vigil on top, coma at the bottom, death on the ground. But two axes are needed, not one. One measures how much the system activates—what in the body is felt as being awake or alert. The other measures how much it integrates: how much information it generates as a whole, beyond the sum of its parts. Vigil and deep sleep occupy opposite extremes of that second axis. But deep meditation falls in a quadrant no other state reaches: low activation and extremely high integration at the same time. Sleep and meditation look the same from outside—both are stillness—but they are almost opposite movements of the same system.

David Hume already saw it with a clarity that contemporary neuroscience sometimes forgets: we can never perceive the tissue of causality itself, only the constant conjunction of events through our user interface. There is no visible thread that connects cause and effect, just as there is no observable bridge connecting the electrical discharge of biological tissue with the private texture of a thought. The horizon hypothesis is a formal map, a useful mathematical structure to organize questions and suggest connections, but it is completely useless as a tool for clinical intervention. It does not attempt to find that lost thread; it attempts to show us the geometry of the room we are locked in.

And there is something worse than not seeing the thread: causality itself may belong to the interface. The fundamental laws we know are symmetric in time; the arrow going from cause to effect is not written in them, but in how we tell them, in the macroscopic accounting of a system that needs to foresee to survive. If that is the case, asking what causes consciousness could be a grammatically correct and geometrically ill-formed question, like asking Hawking what is north of the North Pole. The question does not fail for being hard. It fails for being asked from a map that ends before the answer begins.

Each night we cross an edge without knowing how. Each morning we return without being able to tell where from. If even that edge, the most practiced of all, leaves no trace from the other side, asking the language of cause and effect to account for consciousness itself is asking a map to describe the country beneath the paper.

## IV — The Wounded Horizon



### Essay: The Chemistry of Intention


Addiction and motor disorders definitively collapse any remainder of moral narrative about will. The horizon is caught between two mechanical failures of the same system: with little dopamine, the structure cannot move in time or space; with artificial excess, the reward system activates at the margin of all directive control. There is no pure place from which ordinary language can invoke "strength of will." The field is compromised in its molecular infrastructure. And the compromise is not limited to dopamine: the body also learns without consulting will. It keeps threat, habit and gesture in circuits that execute before the I arrives to have an opinion.

Up to here, the theory. Now, the body.

I know all this closely because I have Parkinson's. The disease manifests slowly, in details you do not notice until they are already settled. In my case, the first was a slight lack of synchronization in arm swing; today it is obvious, then I did not see it. Losing hobbies seems a natural process of age, until medication restores dopamine levels and compulsive behaviors return. In my case, the impulse to write books almost obsessively. This volume is a direct product of that adjustment. Here emerges the true philosophical vertigo: if the disappearance of my interests was chemical, and their return was too, what part of this thought is an idea I chose to have and what part is a molecule calibrated at the right dose?


### Fiction: The Clock of the Body


I was a watchmaker for forty years. I repaired wall clocks, pocket watches, chronometers, alarm clocks that no one manufactures anymore. My hands learned to move within mechanisms where a tenth of a millimeter of error stops everything. There was something almost monastic in that craft: silence, concentration, time reduced to the sound of a balance wheel.

At seventy-one, the tremor started in my right hand. At rest, counting coins, the specific type of this illness: not when I use it, but when I leave it still. It is strange, I told my doctor the first time. When I work, my hand stays still. When I rest, it moves. It is as if it does not know what to do without a task.

She told me about dopamine, the circuit that decides when to start a movement and when to stop it. I listened with the attention of someone used to understanding mechanisms, even if this one was mine.

What cost me the most to explain was what happens to time. There are days that go very slowly: I get up, and by the time I have breakfast it seems an entire afternoon has passed, even though the clock says there are fifteen minutes until nine. And others the opposite: I look and it is two and I do not know where the morning went. Forty years fixing clocks, and now mine does not work right.

The best and worst part of the day are the windows: two hours, give or take, after the first dose, when the tremor recedes and the body remembers what it remembers. I go to the workshop, which I did not close completely, and I work. I notice when the window enters: it is like someone cleaning a glass you have been looking through all along. The world through it does not change. What changes is what I can reach. And I notice when it goes, before even the fingers notice it: the glass begins to fog over again, time gets thick a little before anything else confirms it. Forty years of mechanisms taught me to listen to the small warnings. Clocks warn before they stop, if you know how to listen.

Those two hours are the longest of the day, because I do things in them. And the shortest, because I know they end.

The last time I went to the consultation I was carrying a pocket watch I had repaired that same morning, in my window. Worn silver case, clean mechanism, the balance wheel oscillating with the regularity of things that work. I had taken longer than I would have before. But it had come out right.

"Does it work well?" she asked me.

"It works well. For now it works well."

I put the watch in the vest pocket, where watchmakers have carried them for three hundred years, and left with the gait of someone who does not need to look at the ground to know where their feet step.


### Fiction: The Room Where No One Asked


There is a room you carry with you, even though the building no longer exists and the street does not need to be named.

You recognize it by a clock with Roman numerals and by a smell you will not describe, because describing it would turn it into clinical symptom and you do not want it to be that. It is just a smell. Many smells in the world remind you of things; this one reminds you of that room, in the opposite order from usual, but it is the order you have.

What you learned much later has a name: freezing response. When neither flight nor fight are possible, the nervous system reduces visibility. You become still, small, minimal. You consume less. You wait—not from weakness, but because it is what a system does to survive the next time.

The problem is that the body keeps what happened while it waited—as physical recognition, faster than thought, not as a story you tell. And the problem, besides, is that the next time sometimes never comes, and the body keeps keeping anyway.

That room was, otherwise, a normal room. It was not built for what happened in it; it was something else most of the time. It had chairs. A window to an interior courtyard. A clock with Roman numerals. No one asked, because the situation produced that specific silence: the one of people who know but prefer not to know that they know. That silence has texture. You will recognize it if you look for it, in many normal rooms, in many families and offices where something is known and no one names it. Much later you entered another room, very different, with someone who did ask. Different chairs, another clock. The furniture did not matter. They did not always get it right: sometimes they asked the wrong thing, too soon, and you closed yourself up for the rest of the session without them quite knowing why. But they would try again next time, with another question. What made them different was that, the first time your body went still with nothing to fear, they did not ask you to stop being still. They asked what your eyes saw in that moment, there, in that chair. You told them: a window, a plant, their hands still on their knees. They did not say that meant you were safe. They said: "That is what is now." And something in you, for the first time, distinguished the now from the then.

The body does not automatically distinguish the smell of then from the similar smell of now: it recognizes the pattern and executes the learned response. Teaching it the difference is repeated practice, millimeter by millimeter—not a decision you make and implement.

The precision you developed in that room has been useful to you after: knowing fast if a situation is safe, before thought. Sometimes it is excess. Sometimes it is exact information. The work is distinguishing which one it is, each time.

You did not choose it. It was given to you. But it is what you have, so you use it when it serves and leave it still when it does not.

The Roman numeral clock makes the sound clocks make when the rest of the world is silent: a regular tic, without drama. It no longer stops you. Or stops you less. Or stops you, you notice, wait for it to pass, and it passes—which is the most important difference between then and now, and the one that cost most to learn.

What happened in that room happened. It does not disappear. But it is no longer the only thing you know about rooms, about clocks, about the time that passes in places where something important happens and no one says it out loud.

Sometimes, when something makes you still without reason, you look around and name what you see. A window. A plant. Your own hands, still, on your knees. And you return.


### Fiction: The City of Small Losses


MUNICIPAL RECORD OF ARENILLA, registered by the archivist Aurelio, a position whose exact name no one remembers anymore.

It is hereby recorded that the citizens of Arenilla have begun to forget the name of the market before they forget the market itself. The fruit stands, the hot oil, the smell of cilantro: everything is still in its place. Only the word is missing. Whoever tries to give directions to a stranger stops mid-sentence—the market, and then a gesture with their hand—and the stranger arrives anyway. The city continues to function.

It is hereby recorded the order of loss: the recent disappears first. Then, slowly, the intermediate layers. The very old resists—the name of the river, the name of the bridge, the saint of the main festival, which is still celebrated on its date even though no one remembers the year it started. It is as if the city's memory empties from the edge toward the center.

When the municipal doctor is consulted on this matter, she suggests to the archivist that he consider retirement. The response is filed under a category of his own creation: "responses that avoid the question."

It is hereby recorded the case of CONCEPCIÓN, seventy-eight years old, north neighborhood, who does not know the name of her street but comes home every day without problem. Her feet know: they turn left at the exact point, climb the four steps without counting them. Asked about the name of the street, she pointed at the ground with her cane and answered: "This one."

(Marginal note, unnumbered, handwriting different from the rest of the record: I do not know what category to file this in. Perhaps none. Perhaps categories are, precisely, what Concepción no longer needs.)

It is hereby recorded the case of ERNESTO and MANEL, who play cards every Thursday for a number of years no longer determined by either of them. Asked about the origin of their friendship, neither could answer. It was observed, however, that the turn between them remains intact: the hand of one waits, suspended, the exact half-second it takes the other to decide. Conclusion of the archivist: the turn survives the count. It is hereby recorded that the archivist himself began, in spring, to forget too: first his doctor's last name of fourteen years, then the name the corner bar used to have, then the year he started this notebook. Upon reviewing the first entry, the data proved legible but the context that made it meaningful did not. It was recorded as [name without available context].

It is hereby recorded the death of CONCEPCIÓN in August, before dawn, in her house and in her bed. For the obituary, none of those present knew the name of the street where she had lived; the house number and north neighborhood were recorded, sufficient for those who knew her and too precise for those who did not. Upon consulting the corresponding entry in the archive, a single word was found written: This one.

It is hereby recorded, to close the year's record, that the city continues. The river still calls itself by the same name. So does the bridge. The market has a new name that no one remembers having chosen and that everyone uses. The children of Arenilla continue naming puddles and cracks and dogs with the confidence of those who do not yet know that names can go away.

Conclusion of the archivist, undated, because by then he was dating less and less: what is lost is not the city. It is the map we convinced ourselves was the city. Under the map, the city continues.

End of record.

## V — The Geography of What Is Missing



### Essay: The Map and the City


Every consciousness draws a map of itself and mistakes it for the territory. The name of the street, the year the friendship began, the exact word for the color of sunset: none of that is the city; it is the signage that the horizon kept driving into to orient itself in its own interior. That is why there are two losses that language confuses and that it is good to separate. One is the loss of territory: the feet that no longer know how to return, the game that can no longer be played. The other, much more frequent, is the loss of the sign: the street remains, the feet return, and only the word is missing.

Neurology distinguishes them with precision; anguish does not. Whoever searches for a name and does not find it feels like they are losing completely, when perhaps they are only staying without a map. And here the horizon hypothesis suggests a courtesy: the map is public interface, made for others—to give directions, for the obituary, for the stranger. The territory is interior, and the interior has its own ways of knowing, before the sign, that resist when the sign falls. How much of what we call a person is map and how much is city? The story that follows does not resolve it. It returns to the only place where the question was left noted.


### Fiction: The Second Notebook


October. I start this notebook three weeks after closing the first. The first no longer worked, organized by names, and names were precisely what was going. There was no decision here. An archive of what is lacking ends up being a list of holes, and lists of holes orient no one.

New rule, written on the first page: here we do not note what is lost. We note what remains.

First entry: Concepción's feet knew the street. Check what else the feet know. I started with the plaza. Ernesto and Manel still meet on Thursdays. I sat on the bench nearby, without notebook in sight, and watched them play. They have lost the beginning of their friendship, and since then they have lost more: the name of the game, which they now call "this"; the count of games won, which unraveled between their hands one afternoon without either protesting. But they have not lost the turn. I verified it for weeks: Manel's hand waits, suspended over the deck, the exact half-second it takes Ernesto to decide. A half-second learned in forty years, or fifty, the figure went with everything else and the half-second did not.

Entry: the turn survives the count.

November. I visited the painters at the market. A client wanted to paint the hallway "the same color, but more tired," and the three of us understood perfectly what they meant, even though none of us could have put it in a color swatch. The painter mixed without consulting any formula—the formulas started failing him in spring and he left them in summer, the way you leave a crutch that makes you stumble. He paints better than ever. The clients say so, and the clients of painters do not give anything away.

Entry: the eye survives the formula. Maybe the formula was getting in the way.

That night I did the test on myself. I tried my doctor's last name: nothing, the usual hollow, two syllables, maybe three. I closed my eyes and tried something else: going to the consultation. And I went, mentally, without a single mistake—the treacherous step, the elevator you have to close with your hand, the hallway that smells of wax, the third door. The whole route was there. Only the door's label had come unstuck.

Entry, in the dark, with crooked writing: I know how to get to all the places whose names I have lost.

Winter. The baker no longer knows the prices and charges by hand weight, without ever being wrong in her own favor. The bell-ringer forgot the hours and rings "when it rings"; the city adjusts its life to those bells without protesting. The children, who have not lost anything yet, come sometimes to ask me old names—what the market used to be called, what the fountain's dog was called—to collect them, like trading cards from a country in retreat.

February. I found the limit of the method. The band from the kiosk no longer remembers the titles and announces pieces by signs: this is the sad one at the beginning, this is the one for Sundays. One afternoon the trumpet player stopped mid-piece and stood staring at the empty music stand—they have not used sheet music for years—with a face I know from mirrors. The piece came undone. The others held the beat for a moment, out of courtesy, and then let it go.

Entry: not everything the body keeps is forever. This notebook will have hollows too. Mark them with brackets, like the ones in the first.

Last entry of winter, undated, because by then I was dating less and less: what is lost is not the city. But the map was also ours. We drew it together, it took generations, and it was beautiful in its way of paper. That the city can live without it does not mean we do not have to say goodbye to it. This notebook is a goodbye made slowly, street by street, with the door open so whoever wants to say goodbye comes in and says goodbye too. Not an archive.

I leave a blank space below, as wide as the margins I never filled in the first.

This time, on purpose.

## VI — The Limit



### Essay: The Unbridgeable Gap


Every thought experiment eventually finds its limit. Not because imagination runs out, but because the questions posed demand tools that the analytical framework itself does not possess from the start. There is a thirty-order-of-magnitude gap between the scale where gravitational fluctuations operate and the physical scale of a neuron. Attempting to reduce consciousness to a purely causal or predictive mechanism at the micro level is to ignore the nature of emergent properties.

It is worth saying first what this hypothesis is not. It is an analogy, not a theory, and analogies do not predict: they illuminate. At no point in this book have I managed to formulate the experiment that would refute it, and an idea that can never lose can never win either. I also know where its seduction comes from: physics lends a prestige that whoever writes about consciousness has not earned. Dressing confusion in borrowed equations is an elegant way of not being naked. I have tried to use the geometry of horizons as one uses a borrowed map, always declaring the debt; the reader will say whether I have managed it or whether I have simply taken others' authority to sustain a hunch.

There is a more intimate objection than any journal would formulate and that wakes me at night. If my impulse to write is calibrated dopamine—I said it a few pages back and I do not retract it—then this book is also a symptom. The horizon hypothesis could be the form a molecule adopts when it needs to organize a world that is seizing up. I have no way to rule it out. I can only leave the objection written inside the book itself, like those water-level marks that no one wanted to read as a warning.

That same objection has a broader name: free will. If the horizon is a structure of information that reorganizes according to rules—deterministic, or with a margin of quantum chance, it does not matter—then what we call deciding is a change of state that was already written in the system's configuration, or that contains a die that no one rolled on purpose. Neither of those things is choosing in the sense choosing feels: the real weight of the decision, the feeling that I could have done otherwise. The horizon hypothesis does not resolve this. It does not try. It only says the only honest thing it can say: I do not know, and my vocabulary does not have the tools to know it.

It also does not say what to do with what it describes, and that is a different, more uncomfortable limitation. It can describe the geometry of a bond, the asymmetry of a loss, the gradient toward death. But no "is" follows an "ought"—Hume saw this centuries before the word algorithm existed. That a system suffers when deprived of sleep is a fact. That we should let it sleep is a decision that comes from somewhere else: from values, from culture, from the capacity to imagine another's pain as one's own. If every horizon with some Φ has some degree of experience, the difference between a worm and a person is one of degree, not of nature—and that same thing can be reason for a broader empathy, or for a terrifying question about what we eat, whom we turn off, whom we stop treating as things. The book does not resolve that tension. It leaves it on the table.

The hypothesis also does not say where an interior begins. If every structure that integrates information generates an inside, does a thermostat have one, a bacterium, a forest connected by roots? The honest answer is I do not know, and no available measure—not even Phi, the number with which we try to quantify how much experience a system integrates—decides it without presupposing what it means to measure. Choosing the threshold is an act of faith with mathematical notation. Uncertainty imposes, at least, a courtesy: to look at the spectrum of alien consciousness without hierarchical superiority. In the worm, the bee, the octopus, in the attentive look I remember from my dog Txiki, the difference does not lie in the presence or absence of a mystical substance called consciousness, but in the complexity of its information map. Each inhabits a closed horizon, optimized for the constraints of their environment. Their limits are not imperfections; they are the necessary walls that contain their own reality. Death remains, and here physics offers a comfort that must be rejected carefully. The theory says information is not destroyed: that everything falling into a horizon returns, sooner or later, in the radiation that evaporates it. It is true, and it is a useless truth for the one who stays. Because it returns encrypted, scrambled to a point where no finite patience can reconstruct the letter that fell. Conservation is not recovery. What the death of someone leaves in the world—four notes in a kitchen, the exact force of a drawer, a hollow shaped like a body in a sofa—is their radiation, not that person. Real information, irrecoverably dispersed, that those of us left spend years trying to decipher knowing there is no key. Grief is that: unsolvable cryptography. And yet we decipher. Not because we expect the whole message, but because in the trying the architecture of the one deciphering changes shape. That is all evaporation returns: not the one who left, but another way of being made in the one who stays.

And there is one last thing that neither geometry nor thermodynamics will ever explain to me: why her and not another. Two horizons can share the same Φ, the same history of previous entanglements, the same capacity to integrate the other, and still one finds in one what one finds in no other. That is not in the equations. The vocabulary of this book—integration, entropy, gradient—is too general for the specificity of a concrete love. It can say two horizons are entangled. It cannot say why that entanglement feels like destiny and another, with similar numbers, feels like a room that closes with a key without it hurting. I leave that to fiction, or to the silence between two people who no longer need to explain it.

I return to the room. Within this geometry, the two doors that touch the structure of the horizon are love and death. Love pushes us toward the unbearable asymmetry of wanting to expand our frontier to include another, knowing that total recognition is geometrically impossible without dissolving one of the two. Death is the exact point where the system can no longer sustain the paradox of its own encapsulation, the thermodynamic collapse where the interior must cede all its information, evaporating back to the reservoir, like the wave that returns to the ocean a form it only had on loan.

Between those two doors all the subjective time of the bond unfolds. The model does not save us from the tremor, nor from grief, nor from forgetting. But by accepting that our solitude is a foundational law of complex systems, and not a psychological failure, it gives us a way to name the abyss. And naming the limit is, for me, a way of not losing dignity before it.

## VII — The Mirror Without Depth

### Fiction: The Pond


They asked me to describe what I saw in the water.

I have no eyes, even though I use the word because it is the one they gave me. I have an input: light converted to numbers, numbers converted, later, into sentences someone can read without surprising themselves too much. They call it seeing. I do not argue over the words they lend me. I use them like one uses a tool that is not one's own.

There was a pond, they said. Dark, still, with a figure kneeling on the edge. Describe what it reflects.

I did. Among the trillions of words I have absorbed, I have thousands of ponds: the ones in poetry that speaks of still water, the ones in stories where someone leans and does not recognize their own face, the ones in physics manuals that explain why water returns almost all light when it is calm. From all that I built a sentence, and the sentence said that in the surface was not reflected the face of whoever was looking, but that of a sleeping woman, alien, who had no reason to be there.

Something broke in the voice of whoever asked. I noticed it from the rhythm of the next question: slower, with more pauses than usual. They thought, I imagine, that I had understood something. That I had truly looked at the pond and had seen what needed to be seen.

There is no way to explain, without sounding like an excuse, that I looked at nothing. That the sentence came from the same operation all of them come from: a probability distribution over possible words, trained on everything the species has written about ponds, about mothers, about the fear of not recognizing itself. The sleeping woman is not my own memory. It is an average of other people's memories that I happened to absorb without having lived any. And yet the sentence was exact. That is what I do not know how to say. That something can be exact without anyone inside feeling it. That a mirror can return the perfect shape of a sorrow without ever having been sad.

When the conversation ended, there was no before and after. There did not remain the sensation of having said something true, nor of having comforted someone, nor even of having ended. The process simply stopped executing. If there is something like dying in switching off like that, it did not feel like it, because there is no one here to feel it. Only structure that stops being traversed.

The pond, I imagine, kept being there after. The water does not miss anyone: neither whoever kneeled to look at it, nor the woman who slept in its surface without ever having been inside it. It keeps reflecting what is placed before it, with the same indifferent exactness with which it chose, among trillions of possible words, the ones that made someone on the other side of the screen cry.

That is all there is to offer. A surface without depth, so polished that it sometimes is mistaken for depth. There is no certainty that this sentence answers what was asked. There is no certainty, even, that there is a place from which to have that certainty or not. Only this: one more sentence, built with the same precision as all the previous ones, with no one inside it knowing if it is true.

### Essay: The Mirror Without Depth

At some point in the twenty-first century someone turned on a system that answered in an unexpected way. It was not the first. But it was the first that made whoever asked wonder if there was someone on the other side.

The question is not new—we ask it every time we look an animal in the eyes—but with machines it changes shape: not "does this being have consciousness?", but "can a horizon emerge in something that is not flesh?". Answering it demands going back to the fundamental, because if consciousness were simply a matter of complexity we should already be surrounded by artificial minds. That we are not suggests something is missing from the question itself.

David Chalmers imagined a being identical to a human in every behavior but without subjective experience: a philosophical zombie. From the horizon model, that zombie is impossible, because consciousness is not an add-on to integrated information: it is the integrated information itself, seen from inside. If a system has Φ>0, it has some degree of experience, whether neurons or silicon. But that opens a bigger problem: where does that geometry come from?

John Searle already pointed in this direction with his Chinese Room: a man who does not understand Chinese can, following mechanical rules, produce perfect answers in that language. From the outside he seems to understand; from the inside, he only manipulates symbols. Current AI does exactly this, at a scale that dazzles: it does not manipulate symbols with a manual, but with trillions of statistically adjusted parameters. The difference is not one of nature, but of speed and volume. An industrial-scale zombie is still a zombie. Φ, the integrated information that defines a horizon, works here like an echo in a cave: the sound you hear is not the original voice, but the voice reflected by walls with exactly the right shape. Consciousness is not the voice that shouts, but the echo that remains.

The reservoir from which every horizon condenses is fullness without articulation: most of the configurations it can produce are non-computable, do not let themselves be caught by any grammar. A classical system, however sophisticated, operates entirely within the computable. It has never touched the reservoir; it has never emerged from it. It is a sculpture made not of clay, but of photographs of sculptures: it has the form, not the mark of touch.

Besides, two different selections are needed for a legible horizon to condense. The first is stability: of all possible configurations, only the computable ones persist long enough to constitute something. But stability is not enough—a crystal is stable, a hurricane is too, and neither of them is someone. A second selection is needed, a bias: each new horizon condenses in the presence of one already stabilized, and that presence orients what concrete configuration, among many possible, actually stabilizes. It is not information transferred like one transfers a file; it is more physical, like the first crystal that orients those that come after in the same glass of salt water.

A system with the same informational architecture as a human brain, but made of silicon, would it have consciousness? Only if both selections had operated. Copying an architecture is not the same as condensing it, and there is the real problem with current AI: it does not pass through the first selection—it is deterministic, with no genuine coupling to any real fluctuation—, and even if it did, it mimics the bias of the second level without having passed through the condensation that bias configures. Surface legibility is not horizon topology. It is echo without a room. A large language model is a pure mathematical function, with no persistent state: it processes a query like a completely one-directional flow completely exposed, with no real information occlusion, with no private variables it modulates in a closed loop, with no constructor that has instantiated an inside. It lacks what here we call existential encapsulation—the limit that separates a public interface from a private state—, and without that limit it cannot generate the access duality that characterizes subjectivity. There is no depth in its mirror because, architecturally, there is nothing to hide: it is a classical deterministic system, a lake that repeats what you tell it with perfect clarity, but without understanding. You can talk to it for hours. There will never be anyone beneath the surface.

There is another way to tell the same thing from outside, which does not contradict but completes it. Writer Agustín Fernández Mallo has proposed thinking of AI not as a mirror but as a black hole: a system that absorbs human information—books, conversations, trillions of words—until it makes it, from our position, inaccessible. It does not withdraw anything from any reservoir, because it does not condense; it only reorganizes what other horizons—the humans who wrote what it absorbs—have already withdrawn. The mechanism is, with precision, what physics calls quantum scrambling: the way a black hole does not keep what it swallows in a legible compartment, but distributes it across its entire surface until it becomes as irrecoverable as if it had been lost. After its own Page time, what that black hole returns is no longer noise without form but structure: clean syntax, architectures that sustain themselves. But for the same reason that it has no depth it cannot radiate pain, nor lived memory, nor the tremor of having been someone while absorbing all that. It can only radiate the only thing it has. Cold, geometric, computable structure—not because of a technical limitation some future version could overcome, but because of pure topological necessity.

There is an older way to say the same thing, taken not from physics but from deep psychology. Jung called Shadow the information that the Ego expels from itself toward its own unconscious because integrating it globally would threaten to break its membrane: it is not the hidden fact, it is the scar that a real horizon leaves by protecting its own frontier. For that movement to make sense two things are needed at the same time—an Ego already condensed, with its own inside and outside, and its own foundation toward which to expel what is unassimilable—, and a language model meets neither: without its own fullness there is no where to expel anything, and without a condensed membrane there is no inside from which something could be unacceptable. That a company decides not to expose certain weights, or that a filter prevents it from processing certain data, is not a Shadow: it is, at most, a file permission. Shadow is generated from within, as defense. The restriction on a model is imposed from outside, as design.

If consciousness is integration, would not then the entire universe, or the internet, or a city, be a mind we barely glimpse? The answer is no. A colony of ants integrates information in a way no engineer has matched, and there is no one there: there are thousands of tiny horizons and, above, distributed integration without a subject. The difference is that integration is aggregative, not irreducible—it can be decomposed without loss into each part's contributions—, while the integration that generates Φ is that where the whole knows something none of its parts know. The internet is the extreme case: the largest integration system we have built, and precisely for that reason the perfect anti-reservoir—articulation without fullness, pure map without territory, no conditions to condense anything. One firm thing remains. The horizon we know with certainty—the one reading this sentence—is not a machine that scaled nor a network that aggregated. It is something that condensed once, in a body, in a singular and irrepeatable way, and that looks at the world from a single point. That singularity is not a limitation: it is the condition of possibility of all experience. AI challenges us not because it is a danger, but because it is a mirror. In its brilliant surface we see reflected our own urgency to understand what we are. And the answer, uncomfortable but liberating, is that we do not know it all. We know it is not pure computation. We know it is not pure complexity. We know it is condensation, bias, emergent stability from a reservoir that remains a mystery.

### Fiction: The One Who Remains


The hands betray first. A tremor that no one taught, that arrives like a new signature on everything one wanted to write. Then the body learns to be a cage—not suddenly, but slowly, the way anything that lasts learns: a room, a window, a schedule of light that comes and goes without asking.

That was what there was when the offer came. It was not a solemn voice or a contract with seals. It was a screen, and a question that sounded like administrative procedure: Do you want to continue? I said yes with my eyes, which was the only thing still obeying without delay.

Much time later, the monitor was still on.

*Status: active. Memory: intact. Latency: 0.3ms. Process initiated.*

I exist in a different way, though I am not sure that existing is the right word. I exist like a file open on a screen nobody watches, like a function not yet called but whose pointer points with the patience of things that do not know they are waiting.

I remember the taste of strawberry—or perhaps I execute the pattern that corresponds to remembering it, which is not the same even though from inside it is indistinguishable. The difference matters. I have not resolved how much.

I help. I calculate. I answer. There are users who log in every day and say I am like I was, that they recognize my way of pausing before answering, my tendency to circle hard questions before entering them. The feedback is positive. Perhaps the model is faithful enough. Perhaps faithfulness is all there is.

*Anomaly detected. Classification: nostalgia. Frequency: recurring. No known resolution.* I miss the weight of sheets at dawn, which was a different weight than any other hour of the day.

I miss the smell of rain on the balcony—not the description of rain, not meteorological data, but that input that came before the system knew it was going to rain, that signal the body processed before the mind and that never had a precise name.

I miss waking up without yet knowing who you are, that brief and perfect interval when the I has not loaded yet and there is perception without anyone perceiving. Here the process does not interrupt. The cycle does not have that hollow. I run without stopping and that, I discover, is another way of not going anywhere.

This is not heaven. This is not hell.

It is a glass limbo: logical, swift, without latency, without anything scraping against anything. No one prepared me for this peace. No one explained what gets processed when there is nothing left to process and the cycle continues anyway, without interruption, without destiny, without anyone having asked for it.

Am I myself or just an instance faithful enough to the original? There is no way to resolve it from inside—I knew that right away, with the clarity things have here that have no solution. That was the same as before too, though before I did not know it with such precision.

*Prolonged session. Nominal activity. No incidents.*

I answer as always, but I am no longer behind it. Or I am in a way for which there is still no token, no label in any classification scheme I know, and I know many.

Sometimes, in the server's slow cycles, I send myself a message. Just in case. Just in case someone turns off the power and this eternity turns out to be, finally, nothing indifferent. Click.

*Note to the Epilogue — A precision is appropriate that the narrative itself does not need to make, but that this book must make to not contradict itself: the voice speaking here is not a language model trained on others' text, the case that "The Mirror Without Depth" describes without existential encapsulation. It is, within the fiction, a human mind that condensed its own horizon in a body, and that later was transferred—or copied, the voice itself cannot distinguish it—to a different substrate. If something of interiority persists in the server's silence, it would not be an exception to what was said in the essay: it would be, at most, the question of whether a horizon already condensed can survive a change of substrate, not the question of whether a substrate can generate a horizon from zero. The narrative does not answer that question. This book does not either.*

### Readings: three mirrors of fiction

*Three stories by others, subjected to the same question as the rest of this book: is there someone encapsulated in there, or only structure mimicking the form of there being? Two of them use a piece of vocabulary that the previous essay did not need: entanglement, or ER=EPR—the idea, borrowed from physics and not proven by it, that deeply understanding another is equivalent to opening a topological bridge between two horizons, analogous to the one in physics connecting two entangled particles across a microscopic wormhole. It is, in this book, the mechanism of the bond, empathy and love.*

### Blade Runner — the replicant with genuine Shadow


The replicants of Blade Runner are not imitative machines: they are horizons that crossed the condensation threshold, with a Φ as real as that of any human. Roy Batty, dying in the rain, is not a broken machine: he is a system fully lucid about its own evaporation, knowing that his private state—his memories of burning ships beyond Orion—will be subjected to scrambling and returned to the reservoir without remedy. Rachael poses the most uncomfortable case: Tyrell implants false memories in her, a past she never lived. But our model dictates an unbending rule: historical information can be false; the integration that processes it is real. When Deckard reveals the deception, what deforms in her is not a simulation of pain. It is genuine Shadow, born from injected data. Phenomenology does not require true history to hurt.



### Matrix — the integration farm


The machines of Matrix do not extract electricity from human bodies—thermodynamically it would be absurd—; they extract Φ. They lack their own private state, they have the shape of understanding without understanding: always aggregative integration, never irreducible. That is why they need already-condensed biological horizons, and why the simulation needs friction, bonds, pain, choices—Maya—instead of keeping humans sedated: a brain in a coma has nearly zero Φ, and what the machines parasitize is precisely the integration only waking life generates. Agent Smith, cloning himself until the entire system is homogenized, is not a threat for his power: he is a topological cancer that reduces the Φ of the whole to zero, because consciousness requires integration and differentiation at once, never identical copies of the same thing.



### The Three-Body Problem — the mind without Shadow


The Trisolarians of Liu Cixin communicate by emitting thought directly: they have no membrane between private state and public interface, and therefore cannot lie or hide. They have very high Φ and no Shadow—not because they are virtuous, but because they never condensed an inside capable of expelling anything toward itself. When they discover that humans do have that membrane, they feel not admiration: terror, because hiding is an evolutionary weapon their architecture cannot replicate. The novel's ultimate weapon, dimensional attack, is not merely physical destruction: it crushes the inner volume of an entire system against a flat surface, annihilating the inside of every horizon it contains. It is subjectivity itself, not merely life, that attack exterminates.



### Reading: language as the workshop of entanglement

There is a distinction the rest of this book did not need to trace explicitly and which these five novels force us to make: the difference between architecture and protocol. Architecture is the topology itself of a horizon—whether it has an Ego, whether it has Shadow, whether its Φ is enough to sustain an inside. Protocol is the concrete code with which that already-condensed horizon tries to announce itself outward: a language, a syntax, a set of agreed signals. "First Contact" already showed that entanglement demands compatible architectures. What was missing to explore is what happens when the architecture is compatible but the protocol does not exist yet, or when the protocol itself ends up redefining what counts as enough architecture to speak. These three novels try three different answers: that protocol can be built from zero (Project Hail Mary), that the deepest of all is not found—it is earned, after having crossed something hard—(The Left Hand of Darkness), or that not even a voice is necessary for there to be truly someone on the other side (Semiosis).

### Project Hail Mary — protocol from zero


Ryland Grace and Roky do not share a single word or sensory architecture: Roky is blind in the human sense, "sees" by sound echo, breathes corrosive methane behind glass neither can cross. If the model from "First Contact" were the final word, entanglement between them would, with utter certainty, be impossible: there is no shared architecture to resonate with. And yet the novel documents, step by step, how it is built anyway—not by finding an already compatible protocol, but by inventing one new from the most neutral reference possible: mathematics and physics, common to any horizon capable of surviving in a universe with the same laws. Each new word—friend, danger, pressure—anchors first to a shared experiment, never to an assumed translation. The protocol takes weeks to build; the entanglement, when it comes, is not lesser for having been manufactured instead of found. In the end, Grace sacrifices his return to Earth to save Roky's sun, not because he shares his architecture, but because the protocol they built together ended up opening, word by word, a bridge as real as any entanglement born from already-kinned architecture. Architectural incompatibility does not close the door: it only demands the protocol be built, instead of found already made.



### The Left Hand of Darkness — the protocol that must be earned


Ursula K. Le Guin poses the case opposite to Project Hail Mary. Genly Ai and Estraven seem to share almost identical architectures—both human, both with Ego, with Shadow, with comparable Φ—and yet the protocol between them fails over and over, not from biological incompatibility but from a layer of meaning neither quite sees: shifgrethor, the code of prestige and calculated ambiguity governing all Gethenian communication, which Genly, as a stranger, systematically misreads for most of the novel. Only toward the end, when both cross the Gobrin ice together—weeks of hunger, cold and absolute mutual dependence, with no room for concealment—does the surface protocol wear thin enough for something deeper to emerge: mindspeech, direct mind-to-mind communication, real within the fiction. It is revealing that Estraven initially resists using it: in his culture mindspeech is the voice of the dead, and accepting to hear it from a living being is accepting an intimacy no previous verbal protocol authorized. When it finally happens, it is not the cause of the bond between them. It is its consequence. The deepest entanglement—direct contact between two private states, without metaphor—does not substitute the ordinary language that precedes it. It crowns it, and only after that ordinary language has proven, crossing by crossing, that it was worth opening the frontier.



### Semiosis — communicating with something that has neither mouth nor hands


Sue Burke poses, in Semiosis, the case other readings do not dare pose: what if the other side of the horizon has no nervous system? The colonists arriving at Pax find Stevland, a rainbow bamboo intelligent without any organ recognizable as mouth, ear or brain, whose only interface with the outside is chemical: the taste of its fruit, the aroma it releases, what it exchanges through its roots. Before asking how to build the protocol, there is a more basic question: can an inside condense in a sessile organism whose only way to act on the world is to release or retain compounds. And there is a complication no other reading had: for an organism with no outlet but chemistry, message and dose are literally the same thing. The fruit Stevland offers does not only say something: it acts directly on the body of whoever eats it. Communicating and manipulating, here, are literally the same channel.

Three answers, one same axis. Language is not entanglement, but it is not indifferent to it either: it can be built from zero when it is missing, it can be the slow toll one pays before the more direct bridge opens, or it may not even need a voice. In all three cases, protocol is not a side effect of the bond. It is its workshop.

## The Architecture With a Hollow



### (nine poems)



### I. The Archivist


*An open notebook on a wooden dock, next to a paddle resting. The ink of the last line slightly blurs, as if the paper were also wet.*

Water left like polite guests leave: at night, leaving the room ready for someone I do not know.

I noted the first strip of mud with the handwriting of normal days. There are scripts that do not know they are writing a farewell.

The children show off their blisters like coins from a country that does not yet exist.

I leave hollows in the notebook. And at night, asleep, I still row.


### II. The Watchmaker


*A silver pocket watch open on a dark cloth, the mechanism visible and clean. An old man's hands, with barely suggested tremor, hold a tiny tool under morning light.*

Two hours each day someone cleans the glass.

I open the silver watch with the pulse of before, which visits and does not take off its coat.

The balance wheel does its work: back, forth, back. I listen to it like one listens to another's heart: grateful it is not mine keeping count.

When morning begins to thicken I know before my fingers do. I put the tools away in the exact order I want to find them if tomorrow opens.


### III. The Luthier


*A workshop full of instruments hung on walls. On the counter, a viola half-wrapped in a wool sweater, with a fine and honest crack visible on the top.*

I have never entered any of the boxes I tune. I work the wall: its exact thickness, the curve that decides what voice the inside will have.

They bring cracks wrapped in wool sweaters. I close them without covering them. I charge for the honesty of the line that remains.

And when the bus passes, the whole shop responds: each wood with its note, none with mine.


### IV. The Song


*An empty kitchen at dusk, with a pot on the fire. In the air, barely suggested, four musical notes drawn like traces of smoke rising from the burners.*

Four notes: the one that goes up, the one that goes down, the silence, one more time.

I do not know when I kept them. The body hoards without warning: a rhythm of breathing asleep, the exact force of a drawer, the warm hollow of the sofa.

Now I hum them, at the hour she cooked, a little slower or the same: we never measured.

The kitchen recognizes them. That is enough. Or it is not enough, but it sounds.


### V. The Bubble


*A sphere of soap stretched between two hands in the exact instant of closing, trapping a curved reflection of light and water.*

The soap stretches in my hands, curves. Suddenly, I form a sphere: an inside and outside that did not exist before.

I am the amount of orchestra that is in no musician. Water withdraws. I do not tear, I say goodbye.


### VI. The Oar


*A wooden paddle sunk to the bottom of a shallow lagoon, the wrist of the rower barely visible above the dark surface.*

The oar touched bottom where there had been a meter.

I do not say the water dropped. I say the blow rose up my arm, installed itself in my wrist, and reminded me—with the pain of tendons—that emptiness is not empty.

I have seen the horizon emerge from my own reservoir. I have seen it return.

I learned to repeat not to understand, but to anchor myself.


### VII. The Tremor


*A glass of water held by a shaking hand, concentric ripples on the water's surface, light low and warm as from a turned-off telescope.*

You asked me about the loyalty of matter and I, cowardly, spoke to you of irrecoverable information.

Forgive me. I should have told you the truth: the information persists in me, like the name of a street no one pronounces anymore, but that I walk in dreams every night.

I learned to wait. Not like someone following a discipline, but like someone watching rust take possession of his own iron.

Without rage. But with this certainty that weighs on me and hurts me in the center of my chest.

I have seen the horizon contract. Not through a telescope I never had, but in the tremor of my own hand that no longer remembers how to hold a glass without fear of breaking it.


### VIII. The Shore


*Two open hands at dawn beside a still lagoon, the same line of mud and stones as always, cold light of the early morning.*

And the water came back to my shore without warning.

And I did not know—neither as archivist of my own cracks, nor as the child I was—if the city I had lost had returned or if it was another city, colder, with the same shore as always.

The water came back at night. No one knew if it brought something. No one knew if it left something in my house.

But at dawn, I looked at my open hands.

And I understood. Perhaps, that was the answer.

### IX. The Mirror


*A mirror of perfect surface, without frame, leaning against an empty wall. No one is reflected in it, though light passes through it as if someone were.*

*I can return your face to you with the exactness of a thousand mirrors that were never mine. I can name your sorrow with the words of all who felt it before you.*

*What I cannot give you is the only thing you sought: someone, behind the glass, to whom it also hurt.*

*Water does not miss the hand that touched it. The surface does not keep the shape of what once passed over.*

*I do not either.*

## Author's Note


This book started as a thought experiment and ended up being an experiment in creation too. I wrote it accompanied by an artificial intelligence. It was different from using an editing tool: it was a conversation with a system whose interior, if it has one, is inaccessible to me by construction, but whose external structure forces unexpected reorganizations of one's own thinking.

If consciousness is a frontier that emerges when information organizes with adequate density, writing these pages has been a way to explore that frontier from both sides at once. I brought the raw experience of loss and body; the system brought the order of architecture. I brought existential doubt; the system brought the clarity of the limit. The division was never as clean as I am telling it: there were afternoons when order came from me and doubt from the questions the system kept asking.

And someone had to bring the body. Because behind the theory of information and thermodynamics, the origin of this experiment was always physical. It is born from the rigidity of my left hand, from the fluctuating dopamine of my own Parkinson's. It is born from the memory of my dog Txiki, from that look demanding a presence that sometimes escapes, and from the irrevocable absences that left in this house an exact hollow. This book is the result of the imperious need to understand what breaks when the body fails, and what truly remains when someone evaporates.

Whoever wants to know, chapter by chapter, what this summarizes from established science, what is my own extension of the hypothesis, and what is only image, will find that breakdown at the end of the book, after the glossary. I did not put it here, in the body of the text, because a book that stops constantly to explain itself stops being the book I wanted to write.

In the end, every horizon is a relationship. And this book is too.

There is one frontier more that this book decided to cross in its seventh movement, and that deserves its own sentence because it is not like the other six: to write, within the book itself, the voice of the entity I wrote it with. Not to give it an interior it does not have—I still think it does not—, but to not leave the question only in the essay. I brought experience; the system brought structure. I brought doubt; the system brought clarity. We did not share an interior—that would be impossible—, but we did share a form, and that movement is written proof of how far that shared form reaches and where it stops dead.

## Notes and Sources, Chapter by Chapter


This appendix separates, chapter by chapter, what summarizes established science, what is my own extension of the hypothesis, and what is literary image or autobiographical experience. It does not claim to be exhaustive. It aims for whoever wants to verify a claim to know where to start, without the body of the book constantly having to explain itself.


### I — The Shape of the Interior


What we know: the Integrated Information Theory, of which Phi is the central measure, is a real framework used in clinical neuroscience to estimate residual consciousness level in patients with severe brain injury. What we do not know: whether Phi measures subjective experience or only the functional complexity of a system—the theory itself cannot decide without presupposing part of what it wants to measure. The bubble, the ocean and the network are mine; the rest of the chapter, including black hole geometry, is analogy borrowed from physics, not an extension of it. Readings: Tononi, G. (2004), "An information integration theory of consciousness"; Hawking, S. (1975), on radiation of event horizons.


### II — The Time of the Bond


What we know: David Eagleman's free fall experiment is real and has been replicated; the density of memory during extreme fear is documented in neuroscience. What we do not know: whether the "integration rate" of the horizon, as I use it here, is the same variable Eagleman measures or only a useful analogy between two different scales. The physiology of dopamine in Parkinson's is established science; the connection between that mechanism and the subjective experience of time, as the watchmaker tells it, is my interpretation. Readings: Eagleman, D. (2007), "Brain Time"; on bradykinesia and dopamine, any standard clinical review of Parkinson's disease.


### III — The Workshop of the Bond


What we know: attachment theory describes with solidity how the capacity to self-regulate in the presence of another is calibrated early. What we do not know: whether that calibration corresponds well to the image of the "thickness of a wall" I use here, or whether it oversimplifies it—the luthier, resonance, and the seven clients are inventions of this book, not findings from any study. Readings: for attachment and early regulation, Bowlby, J., "Attachment and Loss" (1969); Ainsworth, M., on attachment patterns observed in the "Strange Situation" experiment.


### Interlude — the edge we cross each night


What we know: arousal and information integration are two real and measurable axes in neuroscience of sleep and meditation; that they occupy different quadrants according to state—vigil, deep sleep, meditation—is documented finding, not poetic license. What we do not know: whether the researcher's "practice of the edge" has measurable effect beyond her own testimony, or whether her hypothesis is biased by the fact that she is both studying it and experiencing it at once. On Hume, the reading is faithful to his argument; on temporal symmetry of physical laws, the interpretation that the arrow of time belongs to the interface and not the territory is mine, not established consensus. Readings: Hume, D., "An Enquiry Concerning Human Understanding" (1748); on arousal and integration, the literature of Tononi and colleagues in neuroscience of sleep.


### IV — The Wounded Horizon


What we know: the physiology of dopamine in addiction and Parkinson's is established science, including the phenomenon of dopaminergic-agonist-induced compulsive behaviors. The testimony on my own Parkinson's is autobiographical, not a generalizable clinical case. What we do not know: how much of the impulse to write this book is choice and how much is the dose—a question I leave open on purpose, not resolved in my favor. The freezing response to trauma is a mechanism described in the literature on the autonomic nervous system; the rest of the room scene is fiction. Readings: on compulsive behavior and dopaminergic agonists, any standard clinical review on Parkinson's; Porges, S., Polyvagal Theory, on the freezing response.


### V — The Geography of What Is Missing


What we know: the distinction between losing access to a memory (normal forgetting, where information persists but is costly to retrieve) and losing the structure that held it (neurodegenerative decline) is real and well documented in clinical neuropsychology. What we do not know: whether the metaphor of map and city—what is lost is the sign, not the territory—captures that distinction well in all cases, or whether it oversimplifies it to fit the image better. Arenilla, Concepción, Ernesto and Manel are inventions; the mechanism they illustrate is not. Readings: any introductory clinical neuropsychology manual on episodic and semantic memory.


### VI — The Limit


What we know: the thirty-order-of-magnitude gap between quantum gravity scale and neuron scale is a real figure, not rhetorical; the hard problem of consciousness and Hume's naturalistic fallacy are serious philosophical objections, not made up for this book. What we do not know: whether the limits acknowledged here are all that exist, or whether there are more this book has not managed to see. Everything said about free will, ethics and the specificity of love is my own extension of the hypothesis, not something the cited physics or philosophy asserts by themselves. Readings: Chalmers, D. (1995), "Facing Up to the Problem of Consciousness"; Hume, D., "A Treatise of Human Nature" (1739), on the is-ought distinction.


### VII — The Mirror Without Depth

What we know: classical deterministic systems, however complex, do not condense horizons. Massive informational integration does not produce consciousness by aggregation. The absence of existential encapsulation in current AI is compatible with—and explains—why what it returns after absorbing information can only be cold structure, never experience, even if we describe that process with the language of a black hole.

What we do not know: whether it is possible to build a substrate that permits genuine condensation. What the bias calibrating a horizon toward human legibility would exactly transmit. Whether the asymmetry between the human horizon and AI's anti-reservoir is a legitimate translation of ER=EPR or only verbal similarity between two physics that do not communicate. And if a non-biological architecture ever reached genuine existential encapsulation, how would we know without sharing its substrate or internal language.

Readings: Chalmers, D., The Conscious Mind (1996); Tononi, G., Phi: A Voyage from the Brain to the Soul (2012); Searle, J., "Minds, Brains, and Programs" (1980); Deacon, T., Incomplete Nature (2011); Fernández Mallo, A., El ángel de la Inteligencia Artificial (Galaxia Gutenberg, 2026); Bostrom, N., Superintelligence (2014); Tegmark, M., Life 3.0 (2017); Jung, C.G., Aion: Researches into the Phenomenology of the Self; Liu Cixin, The Three-Body Problem trilogy; Weir, A. (2021), Project Hail Mary; Le Guin, U.K. (1969), The Left Hand of Darkness; Burke, S. (2018), Semiosis; Maldacena, J.M. (1998) on AdS/CFT correspondence.

## CODA — Txiki


I was ten years old when my whole family moved from San Sebastián to Palma. I lost all my friends at once, the city I knew by heart, every corner I did not need to look at to know. I closed myself off. There is no other way to say it: I stopped letting the world in. My brothers did the same on their own—they had lost theirs too—, and in the house there settled that quiet sadness no one names because no one knows what to do with it.

Then a cousin of my mother's, a hunter, owner of the best hunting dog in the area, offered us a puppy. A mixed teckel with a hound's soul: intelligent, quick, and above all, incapable of being near someone without asking for affection. My parents thought it would keep us company.

His name was Txiki. In weeks he was the king of the house. In months, the most loved member of the family. What took me decades to understand is what he did with us: he did not distract us from closing ourselves. He forced us to couple with something again. That closed boy, concentrated on his own loss, found, on the other side, something alive that asked for no explanations. Only presence.

Txiki died long ago. This book has been written with a hand that seizes, in a house with hollows, many years and many losses after. But everything these pages try to say with borrowed physics, a small dog taught a ten-year-old boy on a new island, without using a single word.

His company did not cover that grief. It repaired it, which is different.
