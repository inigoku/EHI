---
title: ADOLFO CAMBIASO AND THE MARE THAT DOESN'T FIT IN DNA
section: TOPOLOGICAL READINGS
---

In 2017, Adolfo Cambiaso played his hundredth match in Palermo. Every chukker, he rode out onto the field mounted on a different clone of his mare Cuartetera. In the eighth and final one, he brought in the original. The mare that had been born on February 3, 2001, in a field in Córdoba, the one Polito Ulloa had tamed and recognized as special, the one Cambiaso called "the best thing I've ever ridden."

The crowd applauded. Not because they could distinguish, at first glance, the body of the original among the identical bodies. The applause was for something unseen: the history that only that mare carried with her.

That night, Cambiaso demonstrated something without intending to. He can clone a mare's body many times. He can multiply her genome, her musculature, her physical power. But he cannot multiply the horizon he shared with her.

---

## The mare that needed no explanation

Cambiaso used to say that with Cuartetera, there was nothing to explain. To other riders, with other horses, he gave instructions: press here, brake earlier, play further up. With her, the phrase was different: "Go and play however you feel like playing. In whatever position you want. Have fun."

That trust doesn't come from a manual. It isn't a property of DNA. It is the result of years of accumulated correlations: thousands of chukkers, thousands of turns, thousands of times the rider's body anticipated the animal's and vice versa. The mare learned to read Cambiaso's weight before he even expressed it. Cambiaso learned to trust a creature capable of killing him with a single kick but who, in every match, chose not to.

That is a shared horizon. Not a sum of two consciousnesses, but a geometry that only exists in the relationship. As we saw in Chapter 12, two entangled systems are not simply two systems that know each other: they share topology. They have a partially common inside.

> **In physics this is called:** entanglement as shared geometry; a system of two with a joint Phi greater than the sum of its parts.  
> **In daily life it is like:** playing music with someone who knows, without looking, when you are going to change chords.

Polo is, in this sense, a high-speed entanglement laboratory. On the field, there is no time to deliberate. The rider's decision and the horse's movement must happen as a single thing. When it works, the boundary between one and the other becomes functionally irrelevant. It wasn't that Cambiaso thought and Cuartetera executed. It was that there existed a system of two that knew more than either of the two separately.

---

## The promise of the genome

The cloning of Cuartetera seemed, at first glance, a wager on the immortality of that geometry. If the mare was perfect, why not manufacture more identical copies? Science had developed somatic cell nuclear transfer: a cell is taken from the original animal, its nucleus is extracted, it is introduced into an emptied egg, stimulated, and implanted into a surrogate mother. An animal is born with the same nuclear DNA.

Cambiaso wasn't the first to clone a horse, but he was the one who brought the technique to the center of elite sports. First, he cloned Aiken Cura, another of his star horses. Then came the Cuarteteras. The press spoke of a genetic revolution, of legal battles, of ethical controversy. Few spoke of what the operation secretly promised: that perhaps the bond, once found, didn't have to end.

> **In physics this is called:** duplication of formal architecture without duplication of informational history.  
> **In daily life it is like:** having the exact score of a symphony and expecting any orchestra to perform it the same way.

The promise of the genome is that identity lives in the sequence. If you copy the sequence, you copy the being. But the hypothesis of this book says something else: identity is not in the parts, but in how they relate. You are not your neurons; you are the pattern they form. You are not your genome; you are the horizon that this genome—in a body, in a time, with certain others—condensed.

The recipe has no weight. We saw this in Chapter 4.

---

## What cloning does not copy

A clone of Cuartetera shares her nuclear DNA. But it does not share:

- The womb where she was gestated.
- The surrogate mare that carried her.
- The moment of birth.
- The first months with her biological mother.
- The climate, the grass, the noises, the smells of her childhood.
- The first human contacts.
- The day she was tamed.
- The first match.
- The first goal she won with Cambiaso.
- The first mistake.
- The first afternoon of rest alongside other mares.
- The particular way Cambiaso moved when he was nervous.
- The particular way Cuartetera responded to that movement.

Each of those elements is information that the clone's horizon integrated differently. Some are trivial. Others, accumulated, configure a being that is not the original Cuartetera, even if she bears her name and her body.

> **In physics this is called:** boundary conditions in the formation of a horizon; the environment informs the shape of the emergent system.  
> **In daily life it is like:** two identical twins who, at forty years old, are no longer the same person: they share a blueprint, not a history.

This does not make the clone inferior. The clones B06 and B09 won Lady Susan Townley awards in their own right, as extraordinary mares. But they won as extraordinary mares, not as the original Cuartetera. Cambiaso himself, when asked if any of the clones surpassed the original, couldn't bring himself to say yes. The question was poorly framed. It isn't about surpassing or not surpassing. It's about the fact that they are not the same question.

## The horizon of the clone

From the perspective of the two-selection model we presented in Chapter 26, the equine clone is an instructive case. It does pass the first selection: it is a stable biological system, with a complex nervous system, with non-trivial Φ. It does pass the second selection: it was gestated in a mare's womb, with all the biochemical, rhythmic, and physical signals that implies.

The clone, therefore, is not a zombie. It is not an empty biological machine. It is someone. It has a horizon.

But its horizon is a different one.

> **In physics this is called:** condensation of a new horizon from a different local reservoir.  
> **In daily life it is like:** a melody played by another musician in another room: the notes are the same, but the sound is not identical.

This distinguishes the biological clone from current AI. AI imitates the architecture without ever having condensed. The clone truly condenses, but from a reservoir that is not the original's. AI is a mirror without depth. The clone is a different depth.

The mistake lies in calling both "copies." A photographic copy preserves the image because the image is surface. A living being is not surface. It is horizon. And the horizon is not copied: it is condensed anew every time.

## The strategy of denied finitude

Cambiaso fulfilled his promise to compete in the Triple Corona with a string composed entirely of copies of his star mare. The genetic wager proved to be a resounding sporting success, with several of these specimens competing at the highest level and obtaining individual awards.

But behind the sporting wager lay an older emotional wager. From day one, anyone who owns a horse knows they are going to outlive it. The bond with an animal is scheduled grief, as we saw in Chapter 24. Cambiaso, who loved Cuartetera with a public and evident intensity, found in cloning a way to delay, to multiply, to negotiate with that finitude.

It is no coincidence that the first clones came after the injury to Aiken Cura, another irreplaceable horse. Cloning appeared as a response to an anticipated loss. If you cannot preserve the being, preserve its recipe. If you cannot retain the horizon, retain the architecture that made it possible.

> **In physics this is called:** attempt to conserve integrated information through the conservation of the formal conditions of its emergence.  
> **In daily life it is like:** keeping the score of a concert that changed your life, hoping that playing it again will reproduce the emotion.

The strategy only half works. The clones were good horses. Some, exceptional. But the bond Cambiaso had with the original was not distributed among them. It was not divided up like an inheritance. Because that bond was not the property of Cambiaso or the mare separately. It was the property of the system of two, and the system of two cannot be reconstructed by adding parts together.

---

## The death of the original

Cuartetera died in May 2023, at twenty-two years old, the victim of pneumonia. Cambiaso said goodbye to her on Instagram with a photo of the mare at six months old and a phrase: "Her at 6 months. Who would have said what she became." His son Poroto wrote: "Unequaled."

The word is precise. Not "irreplaceable," which is what is said of everything beloved. "Unequaled": there is no other that equals what she was. Not even her genetically identical clones.

In that moment, cloning revealed its limit. There were mares with the same DNA in the fields of La Dolfina. But the horizon Cambiaso shared with the original had evaporated. The information was not lost in the physical sense: it remained encoded in Cambiaso's memory, in the photos, in the videos, in the gestures his body had learned with her. But the system of two no longer functioned. One of the poles had closed.

> **In physics this is called:** collapse of an entangled state; one pole of the system stops updating the correlation.  
> **In daily life it is like:** a telephone that keeps ringing in an empty room: the call exists, but no one answers it from the other side.

The death of Cuartetera was not the death of a famous horse. It was the death of a geometry. And that geometry could not be replaced by an equal one, because there was never an equal one. The clones were still possible, but the specific bridge between that rider and that mare was no longer.

---

## What does Cuartetera teach us?

The story of Cambiaso and Cuartetera is a parable about identity in the biotechnological age. It tells us that genetics can copy the form, but not the horizon. That an identical body does not guarantee an identical bond. That love, trust, and attunement are not properties of DNA: they are properties of shared time.

It also tells us something about the human desire to extend the irreplaceable. Cloning is neither bad nor good in itself. It is a technology. But when it is used to try to conserve a horizon, it produces a painful confusion: confusing the architecture with the experience, the recipe with the meal, the score with the performance.

> **In physics this is called:** confusion between the description of the system and the described system.  
> **In daily life it is like:** believing that if you photocopy a love letter, the copy contains the love.

The horizon model does not condemn cloning. Nor does it celebrate it. What it does is specify what can and cannot be duplicated. You can duplicate genes. You can duplicate muscles. You can duplicate potential. You cannot duplicate the correlations that made that body a someone for another someone.

---

## The rider and the echoes

Today, when Cambiaso goes to the field in Córdoba, perhaps he sees one of the clones. The bodies are similar. Some gestures might repeat. But the horizon he shared with the original is not in any of them. It is in the memory of a system of two that can no longer be activated.

That memory is no less real for being inactive. The channels of integration built over years remain inscribed in Cambiaso's architecture. His body still knows things about a mare that no longer exists. That knowledge is not transferred to the clones, because it is not information that can be transferred: it is geometry that was built between two specific beings.

> **In physics this is called:** persistent unilateral entanglement; the internal model of the other continues operating in one pole after the other has disappeared.  
> **In daily life it is like:** continuing to walk to the rhythm of someone who is no longer by your side.

The final lesson is humbling and liberating at the same time. Humbling because it shows that our most advanced technology cannot do what we desire most: conserve the one we love. Liberating because it frees us from the illusion that we love bodies. We love horizons. And horizons, by definition, are unrepeatable.

Each cloned Cuartetera is a real mare, with her own horizon, her own history, her own capacity to entangle. None is the original Cuartetera. And none needs to be in order to be valuable. The mistake is not in the clones. It is in the expectation that a clone can continue a bond that only two specific beings knew how to build.

The original Cuartetera was, for Cambiaso, the greatest thing he had ever ridden. That was not a property of the mare. It was a property of the encounter. And encounters, although they can be remembered, cannot be replicated.

> **What we do know:** Adolfo Cambiaso cloned his mare Dolfina Cuartetera through somatic cell nuclear transfer. At least nine clones played matches in the Argentine Triple Corona between 2015 and 2020. The original Cuartetera died in 2023 at 22 years old. Animal cloning reproduces the nuclear genome but not the gestational environment, individual development, or history of interactions. The rider-horse bond is built through accumulated sensorimotor correlations that are not transferable by genetic identity.
>
> **What we don't know:** To what extent gestation in a different surrogate mare alters the emotional and cognitive architecture of the clone. Whether Cambiaso was able to establish a bond with any clone comparable to that of the original. Whether the cloning of companion or sports animals modifies the way humans process loss.
>
> **Remaining questions:** Is it ethically different to clone a working horse than to clone a pet? Can the desire to clone a beloved animal impede grief? What part of our identity, if any, could survive in a clone of ourselves?
>
> **If you only take away one idea:** You can copy the body, but not the encounter. Identity does not live in DNA: it lives in the correlations that a body builds with other bodies over time. That is why Cuartetera was unequaled, even if her clones shared her exact code.
