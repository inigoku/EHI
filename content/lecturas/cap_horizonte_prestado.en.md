---
title: THE BORROWED BODY, THE WHOLE WORM, AND THE EDITED CENTURY
subtitle: (A topological reading of All Our Wrong Todays, Slaughterhouse-Five, and The End of Eternity)
section: TOPOLOGICAL READINGS
---

The previous chapter left an uncomfortable question open. When a horizon forks —as happens to Tony Stark in *Avengers: Endgame*— both branches share an identical history right up to the instant of the split, and after that neither can exclusively claim the identity they shared. It is, all things considered, a clean separation: two new horizons, neither with a claim on the other, each free to keep integrating its own history without the other disputing it.

There are worse ways to ruin a trip to the past. Three unrelated stories carry the same question —what happens to the inner horizon when someone negotiates with time— into territory that the wormhole and multiverse physics of the previous chapter cannot cover: what happens when the horizon that arrives doesn't find an empty body waiting for it, but one already occupied by someone else. What happens when a horizon stops integrating its history in order and starts perceiving it whole, all at once, as a geography instead of a sequence. And what happens when someone believes they can edit other people's time without that act costing anything to their own.

---

### 1. All Our Wrong Todays — the grafted horizon

Elan Mastai imagines, in *All Our Wrong Todays* (2017), a third way of ruining a trip to the past, different from the two that close the previous chapter. Tom Barren lives in 2016, but not in ours: in a technological utopia that began in 1965, the day a physicist named Lionel Goettreider got a clean, unlimited energy reactor running. Seventy years later, humanity flies in cars and has cured cancer. Tom's father, obsessed with proving that time can also be traveled, builds a machine and sends his son to witness the founding instant live: the dawn of July 11, 1965, the exact second the reactor first switches on.

Tom arrives, and through a stupid accident —he trips, distracts the physicist at the decisive moment— the reactor never switches on as it should. He climbs back into the machine and returns to an unrecognizable 2016: ours, the one with fossil fuels and cancer with no cure, a world that for him is, literally, the broken version of history.

What matters to this book isn't the physics of the jump —the novel barely pauses on it— but what happens to Tom's horizon upon landing. He doesn't find an empty world waiting for him: he finds a body. In this timeline another version of him exists, John, with an entire life already lived —a mother who died young in the utopia and is still alive here, a brilliant sister instead of a resentful one, a girlfriend Tom never met—. Tom doesn't arrive as the clean fork that happened to Stark, two horizons splitting from a common point. He arrives as a graft: a complete horizon, with seventy years of memories of a history that no longer exists, loaded on top of an interface —a body, a family, an entire biography— that belongs to someone else. The two don't share an origin. Against all logic, they share the same body.

> **In physics this is called:** two sets of incompatible correlations forced to coexist in the same physical substrate, with neither fully yielding to the other.

> **In everyday life it's like:** waking up in someone else's house, with their family, their job, their history, and discovering that your own memory of having lived a different life doesn't erase itself: it simply stops having anyone to tell it to.

It's worth pausing on a detail the novel never underlines, but that says more than any of its twists: Tom learns, little by little, John's gestures. How he takes his coffee. Which side of the bed he sleeps on. The nickname he uses with his sister. These aren't narrative data points: they are the other horizon seeping in, gesture by gesture, into his own, until it stops being clear where imitation ends and substitution begins. No one decides that transition. It simply happens, the same way anyone who spends long enough inside a life they didn't fully choose —a job, a city, an inherited relationship— ends up speaking, without noticing, in the voice of the role they occupy.

The novel doesn't grant Tom the consolation of a new, separate branch, as it did for Stark. It denies him even the consolation of a body of his own. It forces him to ask, chapter after chapter, whether he is Tom living John's life, or whether he has already become, without noticing, John with someone else's memories. The answer the book gives is less a physical solution than a warning: perhaps there is no difference at all between fully inhabiting someone else's horizon and simply being another person.

### 2. Slaughterhouse-Five — the whole worm

Kurt Vonnegut poses, in *Slaughterhouse-Five* (1969), the most extreme case of all those in this chapter: not a horizon that travels through time, but a horizon for which time stops happening in order. Billy Pilgrim, an optometrist by trade and a survivor of the Dresden firebombing —the same one Vonnegut himself survived, a prisoner of war in that city on the night of the attack—, has "come unstuck in time": he jumps without warning or control between his childhood, his wedding, the war, his old age, and his own death —which he already knows about, because he has already visited it—, with none of these jumps being a journey in the sense of the previous chapter's readings. There is no machine. No loop to close, no frontier to yield. Only a horizon that has lost its sequence.

The Tralfamadorians, the alien species that, according to Billy, abducts him and explains his own condition to him, perceive all four dimensions at once: for them a human being is not a series of instants occurring one after another, but a creature as long as a worm, with baby's feet at one end and an old man's feet at the other, whole and present all at once. Death, to them, is not an ending: it is only one bad moment among many others, most of them good, and the moment goes on existing, intact, in its place on the worm, even though the human observer can no longer visit it from any other point in the sequence. Hence the phrase Vonnegut repeats, without irony, every time someone dies in the novel: *so it goes*.

This book has argued, from the start, that time is not an external parameter but a consequence of how a horizon integrates: the integration rate determines subjective density. The Tralfamadorians, and Billy through them, describe something that model hadn't yet had to consider: a horizon that integrated its entire history at once, without sequence, would have no time at all in the sense this book has given the word. It would have, instead, a kind of geography.

> **In physics this is called:** the block universe. Special relativity already treats past, present, and future as equally real, coordinates of a single four-dimensional structure; all the Tralfamadorians add is that someone could, in addition, perceive it whole.

> **In everyday life it's like:** looking at a mountain range from an airplane instead of climbing it on foot: the entire mountain —the base, the summit, every switchback— is there all at once, with no stretch needing to "happen" before another for it to exist.

And if that were true, free will would be nothing more than the price a horizon pays for only being able to look at its own worm one centimeter at a time.

There is a scene Vonnegut tells almost in passing, without underlining it, and that for that very reason lingers longer than it should: Billy, now old, tells his alien abduction story on the radio and no one believes him, and he doesn't argue or insist, he keeps talking with the same calm he'd use to describe the weather. He isn't trying to convince anyone that time is a worm. He lives as though he already knew it, and that quiet certainty —neither euphoric nor terrified— is perhaps the most honest attitude fiction has ever produced toward a horizon that has stopped controlling the order of what happens to it. *So it goes* is not resignation: it is the phrase left to someone who has stopped asking time's permission to hurt.

Billy doesn't choose to accept Tralfamadorian fatalism: he simply discovers he was already living inside it, every time a chapter of the novel jumps without warning from Dresden to his optometry office and back. Vonnegut, who really did survive the bombing that happens to Billy, offers neither comfort nor escape: he offers a way of naming the fact that certain instants, once they've happened, can no longer be undone, and that the only freedom available is deciding in what order, and with what attention, one revisits them.

### 3. The End of Eternity — the edited century

Isaac Asimov imagines, in *The End of Eternity* (1955), the opposite fantasy to Vonnegut's: instead of a horizon that loses control over the order of its own time, an entire organization —Eternity— that believes it has gained control over everyone else's time. Its Technicians, like Andrew Harlan, live outside the centuries, in a corridor they call "Eternity," from which they descend into a given century to introduce a Reality Change: the minimal, calculated intervention —an object moved, a sentence said a second earlier— that suffices to rewrite, without anyone inside the century ever perceiving it, everything that would follow.

Eternity doesn't deny the boundary condition the previous chapter discussed: it exploits it. Every Change violates no physical law; it simply chooses, among the histories physics permitted equally, the one that produces the least suffering. The problem, which the novel takes three hundred pages to reveal, isn't technical. It's that a horizon cannot edit other people's time without ceasing to inhabit its own: the Technicians, shielded from every war, every famine, every catastrophe they themselves prevented before it happened, end up living a version of human history with almost no friction —and, with it, almost no reason to risk anything.

> **In physics this is called:** a system that minimizes its own variance ends up minimizing, along with the risk, the new information it could integrate.

> **In everyday life it's like:** a life with no upsets at all because someone, in the shadows, clears away every obstacle before you notice it —comfortable, probably longer, and without a single story worth telling afterward.

When Harlan finally discovers what centuries would exist had Eternity never been founded, he finds a civilization that did suffer its wars and its nuclear failures, and that for that very reason reached the stars. The safety Eternity was built to offer turns out to be, measured at the scale of an entire species, the same closure that prolonged chronic pain can produce in a single horizon: a system shielded from all novelty stops generating the novelty it needs to truly keep having a future.

In the end, Noÿs —the woman Harlan falls in love with, who turns out to come from a history without Eternity— convinces him to destroy the organization before it can be founded: not to save the future they share, but to give the entire species back the risk Eternity had been stealing from it, century after century, without anyone ever having voted on it.

It's worth lingering a moment longer on the book's final image, because it's the one that truly pays off the whole plot: Harlan, now outside Eternity, looking at a future no one has edited, not knowing whether it will be better or worse than the one he has just destroyed. There is no relief in that image. There is, at most, the same vertigo any horizon feels when it stops having a contingency plan for everything: the terrifying, necessary possibility that things might genuinely go wrong. Asimov doesn't reward Harlan with a happy ending. He simply gives him back the right for his story —and everyone's story after him— to be able to fail.

It is the most uncomfortable paradox this chapter offers: the horizon that believes it has placed itself beyond time's reach, protecting its own and everyone else's from any harm, may end up being solely responsible for that time, with nothing left to fear, also having nothing left to gain.

### 4. Three answers, a single question

Placed side by side, these three fictions —and the four from the previous chapter— stop looking like equivalent alternatives and line up according to the same question we asked then: what do they ask of the boundary condition?

*   **Mastai:** It doesn't ask the boundary condition to go backward or to branch. It asks something stranger: that two complete histories, sharing no origin, coexist indefinitely in the same substrate. There is no clean fork. There is superposition with no resolution.
*   **Vonnegut:** It asks nothing of the universe's entropy —the Dresden firebombing happens exactly once, in the order it happened—. It asks something of the horizon that perceives it: that it give up sequence and accept geography, trading free will for the ability to look at any part of the worm whenever needed.
*   **Asimov:** It asks nothing of a single horizon's physics. It asks something of everyone else's physics: that their boundary condition be editable from outside, by someone who has stopped paying the price of living inside their own.

Seen this way, none of the three asks for the same thing as Avengers or Interstellar in the previous chapter —none rewrites an already-closed past or demands a physical channel with no theoretical grounding—. Instead, they ask for three different ways of ruining not the physics of time, but the relationship between a horizon and its own: sharing it with another without merging, losing the order in which it arrives, or ceasing to be bound by it while imposing it on everyone else.

---

Perhaps the reason these fictions attract us so much isn't the physics they put into play, but what they let slip about the horizon of whoever is watching them. None of us shares a body with another history, as Tom does. None of us perceives our own worm whole, as Billy does. None of us edits other people's time from a corridor outside the centuries, as Harlan does. But all three stories brush, each in its own way, against the same suspicion: that the horizon we are could, under only slightly different circumstances, have been someone else. And that the certainty of not being someone else —of being exactly this body, in this order, subject to the same time that governs everyone else too— is not a fact guaranteed by physics. It is, at most, the only version of the story that has happened to occur so far.

---

> **Note on Chapter 42**
>
> **What we do know:** all three novels are consistent with their own internal rules and ask no better-defined operation of physics than the ones in the previous chapter; what they contribute isn't a new mechanism, but a different angle on the same question —what happens to the horizon, not the universe, when time stops behaving as expected.
>
> **What we don't know:** whether the intuition that "a single continuous self" inhabits each body is a truth about consciousness or just the habit of a horizon that has never had to share a substrate, lose sequence, or find itself outside the time governing it, and that for this reason has never had occasion to test whether that continuity was necessary or merely customary.
>
> **Questions that remain:** if two complete horizons could truly coexist in the same body without merging, at what point would one of the two stop being real? Is the sequence of time a property of the world, or just the way a slowly integrating horizon needs to organize what reaches it? And can anyone truly care for other people's time without, sooner or later, starting to stop inhabiting their own?
>
> **If you keep only one idea:** time travel was never just a physics problem. It is, above all, a problem of who the body that crosses it belongs to, in what order what it integrates arrives, and who pays the price when someone decides that someone else's time belongs to them too.
>
