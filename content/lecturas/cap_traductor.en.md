There is a type of horizon that cultural biography documents again and again without ever quite finding a precise name for it: the genius who cannot find the bank. The composer unable to sign a contract without help. The painter who, at seventy, would not know how to heat soup without burning the pot. It is not incompetence. It is allocation.

> **In physics this is called:** a system that has invested nearly all of its integration capacity in a single register, leaving the rest undeveloped.  
> **In daily life it's like:** a world-class violinist who never learned to drive, because the hours other people spent learning to drive he spent, from the age of five, with the violin under his chin.

---

This book has described the horizon's permeability: how much of another's signal it lets in. This reading takes up something different, and in a way inverse: how much of its own signal a horizon manages to get out without another's help.

Every horizon needs some way of translating itself to the outside world: the part of oneself capable of talking to a clerk, signing what needs signing, remembering to eat. In most horizons that function coexists, more or less integrated, with the rest of the architecture. In some it doesn't. And when it doesn't, someone else usually ends up doing it.

### The mind that steps outside the skull

In 1998, philosophers Andy Clark and David Chalmers proposed a thought experiment that has proven extraordinarily fertile. They imagined Otto, a man with early-onset Alzheimer's who always carries a notebook where he writes down every address and every piece of information his biological memory no longer retains, and Inga, whose memory works normally. Otto's notebook, they argued, plays exactly the same role as Inga's biological memory: it is always available, he trusts it without question, he acts directly on what it says. If that is so, the notebook is not an external aid to Otto's mind. It is part of his mind.

> **In physics this is called:** extension of the cognitive system into reliable, transparent external structures.  
> **In daily life it's like:** the phone contact list you no longer memorize because, in practice, you don't need to: it has become part of how you remember.

Clark and Chalmers were talking about notebooks and calculators. But a notebook has no agenda of its own, no fatigue, no inner life. When the translating function is carried out not by an object but by another complete horizon, the resulting system is not a mind extended by a tool. It is a mind extended by another mind, willing, for years or decades, to subordinate much of its own exercise to that of the first.

### Memory split between two

Daniel Wegner gave this phenomenon, in 1985, a name that has proven productive far beyond social psychology: transactive memory. He observed that long-standing couples do not each store the same information separately; they specialize: one remembers the dates, the other the addresses; one manages the money, the other the relationships. Neither one holds, on their own, the complete system. The complete system is the couple.

Wegner later documented what happens when that system breaks: losing a long-term spouse does not only mean losing companionship. It means losing operative access to capacities that, although they never resided in one's own brain, were an active part of the survivor's everyday cognition.

> **In physics this is called:** a cognitive system distributed across two horizons, neither complete on its own.  
> **In daily life it's like:** a two-partner company where only one of them knows how to talk to the banks: if that partner leaves, the company doesn't lose an employee. It loses an entire function.

### Gala: the economic translator

Few cases are as well documented as the marriage of Salvador Dalí and Gala. They met in the summer of 1929 in Cadaqués: Dalí was twenty-five and lived, according to his biographers, trapped between phobias, obsessive rituals, and an extreme shyness that would have made it nearly impossible for him to sustain the public life his future fame would demand. Gala, who left Paul Éluard and their daughter to stay with him, took on over time a role that went far beyond that of muse: she administered contracts, set prices, negotiated with gallerists, and demanded advance payments with a firmness her own counterparts described as legendary. Dalí came to sign much of his later output as "Gala Salvador Dalí"—an almost literal acknowledgment that his work was no longer separable from her management of it.

> **In physics this is called:** functional delegation of the system's output channel.  
> **In daily life it's like:** a genius capable of generating the purest signal in the world, who still needs an entire second instrument for that signal to reach anywhere at all.

It is worth resisting the temptation to soften this story into tenderness. Gala's own biographers disagree about how much of her role was devotion and how much was a partnership from which she herself benefited: she was, after all, managing an estate she depended on as much as he did. Dalí gave her a castle, Púbol, which—by their own arrangement—he could only enter with her written invitation. The translation to the outside world was not, here, a one-sided sacrifice. It was also a long-term contract between two horizons with different needs.

What is unambiguous is what happened when the system broke. Gala died in June 1982. Dalí stopped creating almost entirely and sank into a silence he would barely emerge from until his own death, seven years later. It is not that he lost his inspiration. It is that he lost half of the system that converted his inner intensity into something the outside world could receive.

### Ángeles de Castro: the domestic translator

Miguel Delibes documented a much more private variant of the same structure, though indirectly: not in a biography, but in a novel that took him nearly two decades to write after living through the event that gave rise to it. In *Señora de rojo sobre fondo gris* (1991), Nicolás, a painter, delivers a long monologue to his daughter—imprisoned for political reasons—while reliving, in temporal jumps, his wife's sudden illness and death. The novel is a thinly veiled account of Delibes's own life: his wife, Ángeles de Castro, died on 22 November 1974, at fifty-one, of a brain tumor, after bearing him seven children. Delibes waited nearly twenty years to publish what he himself described as a tribute to his better half.

Unlike Gala, whose translating work operated in the public, economic register, Ángeles de Castro's—both in Delibes's telling and in what is known of the real relationship—belongs to the domestic register: the one that sustains, organizes, and translates the daily life of someone whose energy is almost entirely committed elsewhere. Literary criticism has described the novel's protagonist as a woman whose mere presence "lightened the weight of living"—a phrase that, read in the vocabulary of this experiment, describes a regulatory function, not merely an affective one.

> **In physics this is called:** translation in the domestic register versus translation in the public register: the same function, a different output interface.  
> **In daily life it's like:** the difference between an agent who negotiates your salary and someone who remembers you had a doctor's appointment today. Both translate you to the world. Only the room changes.

### What the myth leaves out

Both stories tend to be told as love legends. It is worth resisting that reading, at least partly. A structure in which one horizon concentrates nearly all of its energy in one register and delegates the rest to another is not, in itself, either admirable or condemnable: it is an architecture, with specific costs. And the cost is rarely shared equally.

Of Gala we know, thanks partly to her own determination to also manage her reputation, considerably more than we usually know about a domestic translator: she had lovers, kept a castle of her own, left documented evidence of her ambition. Of Ángeles de Castro, almost no record survives independent of what her husband left behind. We know her almost entirely through Nicolás's memory—that is, through Delibes's memory.

> **In physics this is called:** archival asymmetry: who ends up documented and who doesn't depends on who holds the pen.  
> **In daily life it's like:** a household where only one of the two keeps a diary. A hundred years later, the couple's story is whatever that diary says it was.

Sustaining another horizon's translation for decades—negotiating their world, organizing their time, absorbing their social clumsiness—is work, even if the language of romantic love prefers not to call it that.

### When the translator disappears

The structure described in this chapter is an extreme version of the entanglement this book called love, extended from the purely affective register into the executive one. When the translator disappears, what is lost is not only a source of companionship or emotional regulation. It is the part of the system that literally knew how to convert the inner note into something the world could receive.

That explains, with more precision than any tale of genius and muse, why Dalí never painted again after Gala's death, and why Delibes's Nicolás—like Delibes himself, probably—is paralyzed by the most elementary tasks of daily life in the weeks following the loss. It is not weakness of character. It is the structural description of what happens when a horizon suddenly loses the part of its own cognition that resided, by full right and without metaphor, in another person.

> **In physics this is called:** loss of a distributed component of the cognitive system, not merely an affective loss.  
> **In daily life it's like:** a piano that has suddenly lost half its keyboard. The remaining keys still sound fine. But the whole piece can no longer be played.

What this reading describes is not a case apart from grief. It is a lens that magnifies it: when what was lost was not only company but shared functional architecture, grief is not only the absence of someone. It is the loss of part of one's own capacity to be in the world.

> **What we do know:** Gala's management of Dalí's career and contracts, and Dalí's creative silence after her death in 1982, are well documented by his biographers. The death of Ángeles de Castro, Miguel Delibes's wife, on 22 November 1974, and the autobiographical relationship between that loss and *Señora de rojo sobre fondo gris* (1991), are confirmed by Delibes himself and his biographers. Wegner's transactive memory and Clark and Chalmers's extended mind are established frameworks in social psychology and philosophy of mind, respectively.
>
> **What we do not know:** How much of Gala's role was devotion, how much was shared economic strategy, or both at once—her own biographers disagree. How Ángeles de Castro herself would have described the function this chapter attributes to her: we only have her through her husband's memory.
>
> **Remaining questions:** Can such an asymmetric translation be sustained without the translator losing part of their own horizon in the process? Does this structure change when both members of a couple are, each in their own way, narrow-register geniuses?
>
> **If you only take away one idea:** Behind many horizons the world calls brilliant stands another horizon, almost always less visible, that learned to translate them. Losing that translator is not losing companionship. It is losing part of one's own system.
