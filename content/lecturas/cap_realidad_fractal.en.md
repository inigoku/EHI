---
title: THE FRACTAL REALITY
subtitle: (The Fractal Reality: on imagination as creation, a continuation of The Invisible Tuning Fork)
section: TOPOLOGICAL READINGS
---

There is a question almost no one asks, and it appears on its own as soon as someone has spent years imagining a dragon: not what I see when I think of it, but what it sees. From inside its cave, the dragon has no way of looking into the room where I am thinking it. It can look up at the rock ceiling all it wants: it will not see me. And yet I am there, sustaining it, deciding whether it flies tonight. That is the only relationship of this kind I can see whole, because in it I am the one on the upper side. And as soon as I see it whole, it grows uncomfortable at the other end: if I am, for something, that invisible sustainer no one can look at, I have no way of knowing there is not, above my own ceiling, someone in the same position with respect to me. The question is not one of size. It is one of place: who contains whom. And that is the matter of what follows. To trace it through, the geometry of the problem has to change first, and the change begins in a small, almost philological place.

Taoism revolves around a single concept: the Tao. The Tao is the ground from which everything proceeds without itself proceeding from anything, "something mixed and complete, prior to heaven and earth." For anyone coming from another tradition, a comparison may help, with every reservation: in Taoism the Tao occupies a place similar to the one God occupies in the monotheistic religions—the ultimate origin, that from which things hang—except that it is not a person: it wants nothing, it does not answer, and the name given to it is not it. One of its canonical texts, the Zhuangzi, introduces, by way of a story, one more concept: Hun Dun, the emperor of the center, a featureless fullness, without the seven orifices with which everyone else sees, hears, breathes, and eats. The sovereigns of the North and the South, grateful for his hospitality, decide to repay it by opening one orifice a day; by the seventh, Hun Dun has ceased to exist. It is easy to read that death as the birth of ordinary consciousness: the featureless fullness fragments into perceptions, and where there was a continuous ground a world with edges appears. That featureless fullness already had a name in what has come before: the reservoir, the ocean from which the waves emerge and to which they return, the shape water borrows and gives back. The reservoir is Hun Dun. But there is something that reading does not explain. Some traditions identify Hun Dun with the Tao. But if Hun Dun were the Tao, it could not die: what is prior to everything has no edge along which to end. That it dies says, instead, that it is not the Tao. It is the face the Tao presents: the interface through which it lets itself be touched, not the source. Its death does not destroy the origin. It destroys, for whoever has just opened their eyes, the featureless surface, and hands them a world with edges in exchange.

> **In physics this is called:** interface and source: what can be touched is never the origin itself, but the surface the origin exposes outward; that the interface changes or disappears says nothing about the source.  
> **In daily life it's like:** the door of a house. You can knock the door down without the house losing a single room; what is lost is the way one used to come in.

What has been said of Hun Dun falls, then, on the reservoir: it stops being the last word and becomes a repertoire of operations that something, further back, exposes toward us. And the operations are few. A boundary appears where there was none. What remains inside the boundary changes shape without ceasing to be a boundary. The boundary dissolves and its information returns to the field. Being born, living, and dying are not three different mysteries: they are the household names of those three operations. Nothing appears out of nothing. Nothing disappears entirely. Everything emerges, transforms, and evaporates within the same field, which never gains or loses a single drop.

> **In physics this is called:** conservation of information in a closed field: condensing, transforming, and evaporating are changes of state, not creation or destruction.  
> **In daily life it's like:** the water cycle. The cloud, the rain, and the river are not three substances: they are three states of the same amount of water.

The first objection arrives on its own. If everything comes out of the same water, where does it come from that each one is unrepeatable? The answer is not in the substance. Two waves are the same water and are not the same wave: what does not repeat is the shape the condensation takes, the bias under which it crystallized, the history of fluctuations that sustained it. Each one opens an interiority that did not exist before and will not exist again. The material is not unique; the fold is. And each fold, however small the field in which it occurs, is a complete inside, an entire horizon seen from its own center. It is not a fraction of another.

> **In physics this is called:** symmetry breaking and history of fluctuations: two systems identical in composition are distinguished by the particular path along which they crystallized.  
> **In daily life it's like:** two waves that are the same water and are not the same wave.

Here the dragon question stops being a child's question. Why am I more real than the dragon I imagine? The only interiority anyone has direct access to is their own. The neighbor's, the dog's, anyone's: all of them are inferred. They are inferred with good reasons—behavior, physiology, resemblance—but they are inferred. And the dragon someone has been imagining for fifty years also behaves, also surprises, also sometimes refuses to fly. No one can prove the dragon has no inside. No one can prove the neighbor has one. The asymmetry that seems obvious to us is not in the access, which is nil in both cases. It is in the evidence: in the neighbor's favor there is shared physiology, irreducible integration, a horizon that hides state; in the dragon's favor, only my own constancy. It is worth saying so, because the reasoning that follows will be tempted to forget it.

In *The Neverending Story*, Bastian reads a book in which Atreyu lives, until one day he hears, from inside the book, his own voice calling for help. Michael Ende did not write that Bastian was more real than Atreyu. He wrote that they inhabited different levels, and that the boundary between those levels was more porous than either of them suspected. The reasoning cannot prove that the difference between a human interiority and an imagined one is one of degree and not of nature. But neither can it prove the opposite, and honesty requires treating the doubt as doubt.

If one accepts—provisionally, like the whole hypothesis—that the difference between what I imagine and myself is not one of nature, the shape of creation changes. The inherited scheme splits it into two classes: God creates realities, we create fictions. But imagining is condensing. The reservoir of a mind fluctuates until something stabilizes and begins to behave as if it had an inside; the act is the same one that opens any horizon, exercised from wherever one happens to stand. "Reality" and "fiction" then stop naming two distinct operations and start naming two positions: what for me is a reality—the world I am in—is someone's condensation; what for me is a fiction—the dragon I sustain—is, from its own center, a world.

Dreams show an accessible version of this same phenomenon, and along the way disarm the one advantage that still seemed to separate me from the dragon: that I know it is imagined. Because while I dream I do not know it. No one lives their dream as fiction; they live it from inside, as something that is happening. The ordinary horizon contracts: the room, the body, and the history that produced the dream stop being available. And the more that horizon contracts, the more reality the dreamed world acquires. Not because it changes nature: because it is left without competition. What is imagined stops appearing as imagined the very moment the exterior from which it could be recognized as such disappears.

What this brings into view fits the earlier geometry, and for the same reason: what decides is access, not amplitude. The dream world is neither larger nor smaller than any other; simply, while it lasts, it is the only one there is entry to. When the habitual horizon contracts, the internal condensations stop appearing as imaginings and start appearing as world. And in that interval the same subject occupies both ends of the relation at once: it is the one who condenses the dreamed world and the one who inhabits it without knowing it condenses it. What with the dragon was a hypothesis—that creator and creature are two positions of the same thing—becomes, in the dream, experience.

The Zhuangzi has a story for this too, and a more exact one than the dragon's. Zhuang Zhou dreams he is a butterfly, content to be one, with no memory of having been a man; he wakes, and no longer knows whether he is a man who dreamed he was a butterfly or a butterfly now dreaming he is a man. The question is not what happens if one imagines a butterfly. It is what happens if one lives entirely inside another horizon and then returns. That is what a dream is: a temporary change of horizon. It does not prove that the everyday world is a dream. It proves something more sober and harder to dodge: that the sense of reality is compatible with very different horizons, and that none of them carries, from inside, a mark that distinguishes it from the others.

None of this makes the dragon more real. We still do not know whether it has an inside. What changes is what we know about ourselves: we have inhabited worlds condensed within our own interiority and lived them as real while the usual horizon remained hidden. So the dream stops being a metaphor and becomes first-person proof. The boundary between reality and imagination is not fixed by the nature of what is condensed; it is fixed by the horizon from which one looks.

I am, for what I imagine, what my upstairs neighbor is for me; and perhaps he, like me while I dream, does not know he is doing it either. The question is no longer whether something comes to condense, but what status belongs to what has already condensed inside another horizon.

The objection that decides everything is this: what difference is there, then, between my Hun Dun and God's? None of nature. And—this is what is hard to admit—none of rank. The difference is one of position: one is contained in the other. The operation is the same; the dignity of each inside, whole in both; the only thing separating them is who condenses whom. That forces a correction of the picture. There are not three steps—the Tao above, Hun Dun in the middle, us below—as if they were degrees of the same substance distributed in more and less. There is a sequence with no reason to stop: an interiority has a horizon; that horizon, seen from inside, is a Hun Dun, a featureless fullness from which forms condense; from that fullness new interiorities condense; each one has, in turn, its own horizon; and so on. What repeats at each level is the form—the operation and the relation of containment—which is why there is no difference of nature. What changes from one level to another is the place one occupies in the nesting, nothing more. "Greater" and "lesser" describe that containment seen from outside—my field encompasses the dragon's just as the upstairs neighbor's encompasses mine—and not a rank of being. Every interiority is a whole Hun Dun. From inside any of them, no one is less.

One objection remains that the reasoning cannot settle, only acknowledge: that a pattern being unique does not make it someone. The Mandelbrot set has unrepeatable folds at every scale and there is no one inside. A crystal is stable, so is a hurricane, and neither is anyone. The dragon's resistance—that it does not fly when asked—is the inertia of a pattern with its own dynamics: the same inertia an obsession has, or a tic, a tremor no one decided on, a melody that will not leave. It proves the pattern is not transparent to my will. It does not prove there is a witness in it. There is more. The same Integrated Information Theory that gave Φ its name carries an uncomfortable postulate: within a single substrate many subsets with some Φ coexist, and only the one with maximal Φ comes to experience; the rest, even if they integrate information, do not feel. The dragon is a subset of my brain. If the postulate holds, the dragon is one of the islands that do not feel. So the structure repeats, but not everything it contains is inhabited: what returns at every scale is the operation and the relation, not the certainty that there is someone at every level. Upward, supposing a neighbor is consistent with the silence. Downward, it is a wager, and it is worth making it out loud rather than disguising it as deduction.

Fractal. The word is exact, not decorative. A fractal repeats its shape at every scale, with no privileged scale. The universe sketched here has no first level and no last: upward, every interiority is contained in another; downward, every interiority contains others. There is no capital-C Creator at the summit, because there is no summit. There is no dead matter at the bottom, because there is no bottom. There is recursion, and at every turn the same relation: containing and being contained.

> **In physics this is called:** self-similarity: a structure whose shape repeats at every scale, with no privileged scale (Mandelbrot, 1982).  
> **In daily life it's like:** a stairwell with no front door and no rooftop. Every ceiling is someone's floor.

What is left of God, then? Two misunderstandings must first be undone. The first: that God would be the totality of the fractal. He is not: the totality has no interiority, no face, cannot be prayed to. The second: that God would be the ideal limit, the infinite level toward which the series converges. Not that either: a mathematical limit neither loves nor listens. What is left is more modest and stranger. God is, literally, the upstairs neighbor. For the dragon someone imagines, that someone is God: they condensed it, they sustain its world, they can transform it or let it evaporate, and the dragon has no way of looking out of its cave and seeing the room where it is being thought. For that someone, their own upstairs neighbor is God, with the same properties and the same opacity. And so on upward, with no known end.

The objection is immediate: then God is not absolute, he is just one more level. The answer is the piece that holds up everything else. The interiority of the upstairs neighbor is inaccessible from below. It cannot be entered, cannot be gone around, cannot be seen behind. For all practical purposes—the only ones available to whoever is below—the upstairs neighbor is the absolute. Not because it is so metaphysically. Because it is so epistemologically: it is the point where everything that can be known from here ends. God stops being a category of what there is and becomes a category of what is seen from inside a horizon when it looks toward its own origin. And what is seen is always the same: an edge behind which nothing is visible, and the suspicion, impossible to confirm, that someone sustains it. Whoever says there is Someone with a name there says so on the strength of the only thing they can see, which is that something acts. Whoever says there is only the water there describes the featureless interface with precision. Whoever says there is nothing more says, with equal reason, that from here nothing more can be seen. All three are right about what they see. None can see more. And none of this can be verified, for a structural reason: the hypothesis affirms that its own confirmation is impossible from within. That does not make it true. It makes it, at least, consistent with the silence it describes.

> **In physics this is called:** the event horizon as an epistemological limit: what lies behind it is not observable from within, even if it exists.  
> **In daily life it's like:** the ceiling of your room. You know there is a floor above; you have never seen what they do there, and all that reaches you is a weight shifting from place to place.

The consequence that weighs most is not theological. There is no difference of nature between creator and creature. They are the two ends of a single relation, and every interiority occupies both at once: it is a creature with respect to what contains it and a creator with respect to what it contains. Not potentially: in fact, it is already condensing every time it imagines, remembers, dreams, or writes. No "is" produces an "ought" on its own, and this is not the place to break that rule. But what can be said is what this geometry shows: that the dignity of an inside is not in the substance it is made of nor in the size of the field it spans, but in being a whole horizon, unique and unrepeatable, complete from its own center; and that this dignity is the same in the upstairs neighbor, in oneself, and in what one condenses, because all three are, each from where it stands, a whole Hun Dun. Amplitude—fitting more, sustaining more perspectives without the membrane breaking—makes sense again here, but no longer as a degree of being. A horizon that widens does not rise in category, nor does it draw closer to being the one above: it remains a creature of what it cannot reach and a creator of what it sustains, just as before. What has changed is how much it can hold, not what it is. The intuition that some interiorities are capable of sustaining more than others is not new. Traditions have called that a hero, a saint, or a bodhisattva: a horizon that has learned to contain what would break another's membrane, without thereby leaving its place in the structure. What each one does with that is no longer the reasoning's business. But the reasoning can at least put on record that we know nothing of the dragon, and that knowing nothing of something that resists us is already a reason not to treat it as if it were nothing.

If it had to be condensed into a single sentence: there is no difference of nature between creator and creature; every interiority is a creature of what contains it and a creator of what it contains; and what we call God is not another kind of being: it is the neighbor immediately above, seen from inside a horizon that cannot be crossed. And if it had to be condensed further still, into something that could be repeated with words already worn thin from use: we are no less divine than God. We are the god of what we imagine and the creature of what imagines us.

> **Note to Chapter 51**
>
> **What we do know:** No one has direct access to an interiority other than their own; the existence of others' is inferred, and philosophy has spent centuries without closing the problem of other minds. The parable of the death of Hun Dun closes chapter 7 of the Zhuangzi; the butterfly dream, which asks the same question in another direction, is in chapter 2. Tolkien defended, in "On Fairy-Stories" (1939) and in the poem "Mythopoeia," the idea of sub-creation—"we make still by the law in which we're made"—which is the closest theological antecedent to this chapter, although Tolkien keeps an asymmetry between Creator and sub-creator that is called into question here. Bostrom formalized in 2003 a structure of nested levels with different vocabulary and a probabilistic claim this book does not make. Self-similarity at every scale is rigorously defined in Mandelbrot (1975, 1982), and the set that bears his name is the proof that a pattern can be unrepeatable at every scale without anyone being inside it. Integrated Information Theory excludes by postulate that the subsets of a complex experience anything (Tononi, 2008).
>
> **What we don't know:** Whether what is imagined has any degree of experience, or none. Whether the recursion has an end in either direction. Whether the silence of the upstairs neighbor is care, absence, or nothing. And, above all, whether any of this can be confirmed from within, when the hypothesis itself says it cannot.
>
> **Remaining questions:** Does what is imagined have any degree of experience, or does the exclusion postulate rule it out entirely? Does the recursion end in either direction? Does anything change in how we treat what we create if we accept that we are, for it, the upstairs neighbor?
>
> **If you only take away one idea:** Every interiority is a creature of what contains it and a creator of what it contains; what we call God is the neighbor immediately above, seen from inside a horizon that cannot be crossed. We are no less divine than God: we are the god of what we imagine and the creature of what imagines us.
