> *For Tarel, who knows that every gaze is a fall.*

## Prologue of the one who remains

Let us forget chronology. Let us forget the museums, the wars, the scholarly catalogues. The Spanish twentieth century was not fought in the trenches of the Ebro or under the bombs of Guernica: it was fought in the curved space of consciousness, where the gravity of the self bends the world's light until it becomes unrecognizable.

Dalí, Picasso, and Miró were not painters. They were astronomers of the subjective abyss.

Each built a personal event horizon: the boundary beyond which information —desire, trauma, memory— can no longer escape outward. Everything that happened to them —a friend executed by firing squad, a woman loved, a war lost— fell toward that center and became subject to its laws. And each solved, in his own way, the same problem: what to do with the mass of obsessions that curves one's own biography until it creates a singularity.

Dalí sealed it. Picasso inhabited it. Miró dissolved it.

This is not a stylistic classification: it is a typology of fates. Sealing, inhabiting, dissolving are the three possible answers to the weight of an inner world that threatens to swallow everything. That is why this chapter is not about painting. It is about what a consciousness does with its own mass.

This chapter is a map of those three singularities. And of what remains when someone dares to look inward.

## The exterior collapse (1936–1939)

Before mapping the three systems, we must record the disturbance that ran through all of them. No interior horizon is immune to a shell.

The Spanish Civil War was, for these three horizons, the same external gravitational field with three distinct responses. Dalí saw it coming and turned it into flesh: *Soft Construction with Boiled Beans*, painted months before the uprising, is a body tearing itself apart, premonition made anatomy. An entire country reduced to a figure strangling itself with its own hands. Afterward he avoided it: neither trench nor placard, but flight to Italy and America, and a political silence that was another way of sealing the capsule — a silence that over the years drifted into a flirtation with power, which his old surrealist companions never forgave him. The war did not enter Dalí: it was digested by the method and returned as a monster.

Picasso did the opposite: he threw his horizon wide open, just once. *Guernica* is the moment when exterior information —the bombing, the bodies, the horse— crosses the membrane without being transformed into autobiography. For once, the Picassian self stepped aside to let the scream of others through. The painting toured the world as ambassador of a dying republic and did not set foot in Spain until 1981, by which time both the dictator and the painter were dead: the information took forty-four years to return to its origin. Picasso never went back to Franco's Spain; his exile was a stable orbit around a country that by then existed only inside him.

## [ILUSTRACIÓN cart_guernica: "Guernica"]
*Pablo Picasso, oil on canvas, 1937. Museo Reina Sofía, Madrid.*

Miró responded with *The Reaper* and the poster *Aidez l'Espagne*, and then with retreat: Varengeville, Palma, a withdrawal into the minimal. His *Constellations* are born exactly there, while Europe burns: twenty-three small-format, transportable gouaches, painted by a man fleeing with his family from one war toward another. They are not escapism: they are resistance through concentration. His horizon did not close with the war: it grew thinner and more taut, like a rope.

## [ILUSTRACIÓN cart_constelaciones: "Constellations"]
*Joan Miró, "The Morning Star," gouache on paper, from the Constellations series, 1940.*

Three reconfigurations of the same blow: Dalí digests it, Picasso expels it, Miró compresses it.

## I. Salvador Dalí: encapsulation

Dalí built a bunker around his psyche.

His painting is a sealed capsule where the exterior world does not enter without first submitting to the paranoiac-critical method: a machine that multiplies images, that folds reality over itself until the original and its reflection can no longer be told apart. This encapsulation is not aesthetic. It is survival.

> **In physics this is called:** an event horizon. The boundary separating what can escape outward from what remains trapped forever; nothing crosses it without first being transformed by the system's inner physics.

> **In daily life it is like:** a locked diary. What gets written inside it may be catharsis, invention, or lies, but it never comes back out exactly as it went in: by the time someone finally reads it, it has already been rewritten by the hand that kept it.

The method has a manifesto-work: *Metamorphosis of Narcissus*. A crouching figure by the water is, simultaneously, a self-absorbed youth and a stone hand holding an egg from which a flower sprouts. There is no trick: both readings are simultaneous and complete, and the eye cannot hold on to both at once. Dalí has built a superposed image, and every viewer collapses it in their own way. Freud, who received him in London in 1938 with that painting under his arm, admitted to the young Spaniard what he denied the rest of the surrealists: that there was a serious psychological problem there. It was not a compliment. It was a diagnosis.

## [ILUSTRACIÓN cart_narciso: "Metamorphosis of Narcissus"]
*Salvador Dalí, oil on canvas, 1937. Tate, London.*

Faced with chaos —war, exile, old age— Dalí builds a self-referential universe where his phobias (madness, death, impotence) and his fixations (Gala, religion, money) orbit like planets in a closed system. The paranoiac machine even works backward through art history: for years, Dalí subjected Millet's placid *Angelus* to his method until he extracted from it a sepulchral drama —the two peasants, he claimed, were not praying over the harvest but over the grave of a dead child— and he went so far as to demand the canvas be X-rayed to confirm it. That the X-ray did in fact reveal a repainted shape beneath the basket is beside the point: what matters is that, for Dalí, no image in the world was safe from being reabsorbed into his system. His own repertoire confirms it: the crutches propping up soft flesh, the ants announcing decay, the drawers opening in torsos as if the body were a filing cabinet. None of it is decoration: it is the nomenclature of a private planetary system, stable enough to be recognized from one painting to the next across fifty years. What enters Dalí does not come back out intact: it emerges distorted, stretched, turned into the echo of a self that no longer knows where the world ends and its own shadow begins.

### The tremor as evaporation

There is a work that anticipates this ending by decades. In 1952, after Hiroshima, Dalí returns to his most famous painting and paints *The Disintegration of the Persistence of Memory*: the soft watches of 1931 now float over a grid of separating blocks, the whole world decomposed into discrete units that no longer touch. This is his nuclear-mysticism period: matter, he has discovered, is made of particles that never brush against each other, and he paints that discontinuity with the same fervor with which he once painted flesh. Without knowing it, he is rehearsing his own future: twenty years later, disintegration will leave the canvas and enter his hand.

## [ILUSTRACIÓN cart_desintegracion: "The Disintegration of the Persistence of Memory"]
*Salvador Dalí, oil on canvas, 1952-1954. The Dalí Museum, St. Petersburg, Florida.*

Because the degenerative tremor of his final years is not an external clinical fact: it is his inner horizon cracking. Fractal analysis of his late brushstrokes detected alterations that precede the diagnosis; they are not a technical failure, they are the self losing cohesion.

If the horizon separates the observable from the unobservable, Dalí's illness was the evaporation of his own identity: that slow, inevitable leak that physics predicts for every black hole. Information is not destroyed; it is transformed. Dalí's tremor is information disintegrating: the self radiating its matter outward as unstable lines, as figures no longer able to sustain their own myth.

And yet, even the evaporation was staged. Dalí did not merely suffer the tremor and old age: he incorporated them into the repertoire. The old man in the gold robe receiving the cameras, the tubed nose displayed to the press, the theater-museum in Figueres raised as a mausoleum while he still lived: the final Dalí turned his own decline into material for representation. The capsule was losing information, yes, but he was still the one deciding the angle from which the leak should be seen. It is the character's final paradox: a self disintegrating without ever relinquishing the direction of the scene.

### Gala: the singularity

Inside that convulsing capsule, Gala is not a muse. She is the anchor point that prevents total collapse: the node that keeps Dalinian consciousness above the threshold of fragmentation.

One need only trace her transmutations to measure her mass. In *Galarina* she is serene flesh with one bared breast, painted —he said— the way Raphael painted his madonnas. In *Atomic Leda* she is a suspended goddess, touching neither the swan nor the pedestal, in a world where nothing touches anything: love rewritten as a geometry without contact, where union no longer depends on touch but on the relative position of bodies in space. In *Corpus Hypercubus* she no longer even takes part in the scene: she watches the crucifixion from below, dressed as a lady of Toledo, turned into the witness whose gaze sustains the prodigy. Over the years, Dalí came to sign with both their names, "Gala Salvador Dalí," as if the signature itself were a binary system.

## [ILUSTRACIÓN cart_corpus: "Corpus Hypercubus"]
*Salvador Dalí, oil on canvas, 1954. The Metropolitan Museum of Art, New York.*

But, like every singularity, Gala is not directly observable. We do not see her in the paintings as she really is: we see her transmuted into Leda, into a virgin, into architecture. She is the invisible mass that curves Dalinian space, and she only manifests through the gravity she exerts on everything else.

> **In physics this is called:** dark matter, or a black hole itself before its first photograph: a mass that is never observed directly, only inferred from the curvature it imposes on everything around it.

> **In daily life it is like:** knowing the wind is blowing by the way a treetop bends. No one sees the wind; everyone sees its effects, and from them reconstructs its shape.

The definitive proof came through absence. When Gala died, in 1982, the system was left without a center: Dalí shut himself in Púbol Castle —which he had bought for her, and which, while she lived, he could only visit with her written invitation— stopped eating, was burned in a nighttime fire, all but stopped painting. The singularity had disappeared, and the horizon, with no mass to sustain it, began its final collapse. Dalí's last seven years are not biography: they are the empirical verification of his own cosmology.

### Lorca: the retained information

The truncated friendship with Lorca is the map's censored zone. Lorca embodies the desire and identity that Dalí needed to bury in order to build his public persona: an absent presence that returns as a blurred face in the early paintings, information trying to escape the horizon, repeatedly repressed by the gravity of the official self.

The Cadaqués summers of the mid-1920s were the only period when the Dalinian horizon came close to not closing. In *Honey Is Sweeter than Blood* —the lost painting of 1927— the severed heads of Lorca and of Dalí himself lie close together on the beach, among devices and elongated shadows: two decapitated identities, adjacent, neither merged nor separated. It is the document of an entanglement in the instant before its rupture. Soon after came distance, public sarcasm, Gala. And in 1936, the execution: the information remained retained forever, with no possibility of a reply.

Dalí took decades to name the obvious. Late in life he would repeat that Lorca had been the only one capable of fascinating him, and he confessed the poet's passion with a mixture of pride and evasiveness that was, still, the old censorship at work. We cannot see Lorca directly in the mature work, but his effects disturb every Cadaqués landscape Dalí painted afterward. The curvature of that space never went flat again.

### The clocks

Dalí does not fragment for aesthetic reasons: he fragments because his horizon is a battlefield, and multiplying images is his only way of not collapsing. His soft watches are not a nod to Einstein: they are the demonstration that time stretches with desire and collapses with terror. He himself gave the domestic key to the prodigy: a camembert melting on an August night. That the emblem of subjective time was born from a melting cheese is not an anecdote: it is the method in its purest state, the trivial elevated to cosmology by the pressure of a mind that draws no hierarchy between panic and dinner. In his capsule, past and future coexist in a viscous present, suspended in the membrane of the horizon.

## II. Pablo Picasso: superposition

If Dalí locks himself in a high-gravity well, Picasso inhabits a prism in perpetual rotation.

Look at him in the two-headed portraits of the 1930s and '40s: Marie-Thérèse's blonde placidity fused with the sharp edge and weeping of Dora Maar into a single impossible face. In *The Dream*, Marie-Thérèse sleeps with her head split into two halves that are profile and moon; in *The Weeping Woman*, Dora's face breaks into green, acidic shards around a handkerchief that is also chewed glass. Picasso does not paint women: he paints superposed states of affect, and forces the viewer to process love, destruction, and desire in a single glance.

## [ILUSTRACIÓN cart_llorona: "The Weeping Woman"]
*Pablo Picasso, oil on canvas, 1937. Tate, London.*

That double face is the key to his entire body of work. Picasso does not have a single horizon: he has many, superposed. His self is not a point with a defined position; it is a wave spanning all possible states until the act of painting collapses the wave and materializes a style, a temporary identity. He formulated it himself, without physics, before the portrait of Gertrude Stein: when told that she did not resemble that mask-like face he had painted, he replied that she would come to resemble it. It was not arrogance: it was the conviction that the painting does not record a state, but anticipates it —that observation does not copy reality, it produces it.

> **In physics this is called:** quantum superposition and wave-function collapse. Before measurement, a particle does not occupy a single state: it occupies all of them at once, until the act of observing forces the system to settle on one.

> **In daily life it is like:** a coin still in the air. While it spins it is neither heads nor tails: it is both at once, and only becomes a fact when it lands on the table.

The first collapse has a date and a corpse. In 1901, his friend Carles Casagemas shoots himself in a Paris café over a woman. Picasso, nineteen years old, buries his friend and then paints the entire world blue for three years: beggars, the blind, consumed mothers, Casagemas himself laid out with a hole in his temple. The Blue Period is not a tonal exercise: it is the first demonstration that a single trauma can tint the whole perceptual spectrum, that the mass of one death can curve all the light of a system. Then came the rose period —the acrobats, itinerant tenderness— and then the axe blow: between *The Old Guitarist* and *Les Demoiselles d'Avignon* there is no narrative transition. There is a jump. The five women of the brothel on Carrer d'Avinyó, with their Iberian and African mask-faces, do not evolve from anything prior: they erupt, the way a particle erupts where classical physics did not expect it.

## [ILUSTRACIÓN cart_avignon: "Les Demoiselles d'Avignon"]
*Pablo Picasso, oil on canvas, 1907. The Museum of Modern Art, New York.*

Changing style was not, for him, a formal quest: it was a vital necessity. Each period is a capsule he inhabits with predatory voracity; once its information is exhausted, he abandons it and founds a new universe with new laws. Unlike Dalí, Picasso does not suffer the capsule: he devours it.

Cubism pushes this logic to its limit. Painting a face from the front and in profile at once is not a way of seeing the object: it is a topology of consciousness, which never perceives anything in isolation but all perspectives integrated in the same instant —the maximum integration of information that fits in two dimensions, the highest Φ ever set down on a canvas. And it has two phases that are two inverse operations: analytic cubism grinds the violin, the bottle, the face down to a dust of ochre planes where the object nearly disappears; synthetic cubism reconstructs them with scraps of newspaper and oilcloth, with fragments of the real world glued to the canvas. First it disintegrates information; then it demonstrates that it can reintegrate it with borrowed matter. A complete cycle of destruction and recomposition of the world, carried out in under a decade.

At the center of all his orbits there is a constant mass: the minotaur. He appears in the etchings of the 1930s as rapist, as victim, as blind beast led by a girl with a dove. He is the self-portrait Picasso could not paint head-on: desire as domestic monster, guilty and moving at once. His time does not move in a straight line: it orbits, cyclically, around the minotaur, sex, and death.

And in the end, the method becomes explicit. In 1957, Picasso shuts himself away and paints fifty-eight variations on Velázquez's *Las Meninas*: the same painting over and over, decomposed, recombined, inverted, reduced to sign. He does not copy: he systematically executes the possibilities of a single image, the way one works through the solutions of an equation one by one. Superposition has stopped being a trait of his painting and become his program. That he chose precisely *Las Meninas* —the painting where a painter paints himself looking at whoever looks— is no accident: it is the old predator recognizing, in Velázquez's mirror, the problem of the observer that his own work had been posing for half a century.

## [ILUSTRACIÓN cart_meninas: "Las Meninas"]
*Diego Velázquez, oil on canvas, 1656. Museo del Prado, Madrid — the original painting that Picasso took apart in 1957 into fifty-eight variations.*

His fragmentation is not Dalí's viscous panic: it is surgical. He takes the figure apart to understand its skeleton. And so until his last day: the old musketeers and painters of his final years, resolved in urgent brushstrokes by a ninety-year-old man who no longer corrects, are the wave refusing to collapse entirely — a self that would rather go on being all its possibilities than accept a final position.

## III. Joan Miró: dissolution

Miró is the system's anomaly.

While Dalí suffers his horizon and Picasso analyzes his to the point of exhaustion, Miró dissolves the boundaries — but not the self. The anomaly needs to be stated precisely: Miró does not annihilate himself into the cosmic; he expands into the cosmic without ceasing to be a self. The proof is that his universe turns out to be unmistakable: no one signs the void the way he does. His journey toward abstraction is not a flight from the world nor a renunciation of himself: it is the dilation of a subject to a sidereal scale. By shedding the weight of figuration, he tears down the capsule's walls and turns the canvas into a firmament where the stars are thoughts and the lines, breathing.

The starting point is the earth, not the sky. *The Farm*, painted over nine months of obsession between Mont-roig and Paris, contains the entire family farm: every crack in the wall, every leaf of the eucalyptus, the snail, the watering can, the goat. It is realism taken to an impossible density, an inventory where nothing matters more than anything else. Hemingway, who bought it, said it contained everything one feels about Spain when far from it. It is already there, in figurative code, the program of an entire life: the ant is worth as much as the star. All that remained was to let go of the ballast.

## [ILUSTRACIÓN cart_masia: "The Farm"]
*Joan Miró, oil on canvas, 1921-1922. National Gallery of Art, Washington, D.C. — bought by Ernest Hemingway.*

He let go of it violently. "I want to assassinate painting," he declared around 1927, and he meant it: against inherited craft, against the easel painting as an object of possession, Miró undertook a systematic demolition of everything painting had of property. What remained after the assassination was not nothing: it was an alphabet. Star, woman, bird, eye, ladder. Signs that no longer represent things, but summon them.

The process, moreover, had its own thermodynamics. Miró worked like a farmer: he rose early, kept regular hours, let canvases rest for months and even years before giving them their final touch, cultivated dozens of paintings at once the way one tends an orchard. The spontaneity of his signs is a mirage: every stroke that seems to have fallen from the sky was awaited with geological patience. It is Dalí's discipline inverted: where the man from Empordà manufactured delirium with a watchmaker's precision, the Catalan manufactured precision with the appearance of delirium.

Here the black-hole metaphor reaches its limit and inverts itself: Miró has no event horizon because he retains no information. There is no mass of trauma curving space around him. His consciousness travels light, catching no one else's light.

That inventory does not disappear when Miró abandons figuration: it is translated. The same gaze that in *The Farm* gave identical care to the snail, the goat, and the mountain reappears, twenty years later, in the *Constellations*, only now the snail has become a dot and the mountain a line tying that dot to a star. What changes is not the principle —nothing weighs more than anything else— but the scale at which it is applied: from a farmyard in the Camp de Tarragona to the entire cosmos. Between one painting and the other there is no rupture: there is the same equation, solved first for the small and then for the infinite.

His *Constellations* encapsulate infinity in a matchbox: bubbles where gravity does not exist and elements float in a vibrant void. That encapsulation is not defensive, as in Dalí, nor analytical, as in Picasso: it is uterine. The painting is a womb where the cosmos folds back on itself to be reborn.

And his entanglement is horizontal, democratic, total. His thin black lines tie a star to a woman, the woman to a bird, the bird to a primordial stain. There is no hierarchy: an ant from *The Farm* is entangled with the farthest star. If Dalí entangles to survive and Picasso to possess, Miró entangles to celebrate.

> **In physics this is called:** multipartite entanglement. Not a bond between two particles, but a network where measuring the state of any node instantly alters that of all the others, with no hierarchical center.

> **In daily life it is like:** a spiderweb covered in dew: touch a single thread and the whole web trembles equally, because none of its points matters more than any other.

But let no one mistake lightness for anesthesia. The pain of the war is in his lines, even if only as contained tension: in the black of *The Reaper*, in the violence of the savage paintings of the 1930s, in the taut stroke of *Constellations* painted while fleeing. The rope tying the star to the woman is strained by history; it simply does not break.

Miró's old age is the exact reverse of Dalí's. Where one disintegrated against his will, the other emptied himself by choice. The three canvases of *Blue I, II, and III* are nearly bare fields: a trail of dots, a red line, an expanse of blue that Miró himself compared to preparing a rite. It had taken a whole lifetime to earn the right to paint almost nothing. And when the world wanted to turn him into a classic, he answered by burning and slashing his own canvases —the *burnt canvases* of 1973, exhibited with the burns in plain view—: at eighty, the lightest of the three still refused to let his universe become heritage. Dissolution, too, must be defended.

He also destroys the arrow of time. His time is that of wonder, where a minute before an insect lasts an eon; that of nature, tied to the cycles of the moon and the tides. His forms do not age because they inhabit a continuous present, stripped of the weight of history.

## IV. The three-body problem

These three systems did not orbit in isolation: they perturbed one another.

> **In physics this is called:** the three-body problem. In celestial mechanics, three masses that attract one another have no closed-form solution: each one's trajectory is permanently altered by the presence of the other two, and none of them ever orbits alone again.

> **In daily life it is like:** three siblings raised in the same house. None of them can be explained without the other two: every character bends, if only a little, under the simple gravity of sharing a table.

Dalí needed Picasso the way one needs a gravitational field to measure oneself against. He visited him in Paris in 1926, before the Louvre —"I have come to see you before the Louvre"; "You did very well"— and never stopped orbiting him after that. It was Picasso who paid for the passage of his first trip to America, the trip that launched him to celebrity; Dalí repaid him for decades by sending postcards and tributes that the man from Málaga, a communist offended by the other's open Francoism, never answered. A perfect entanglement in its asymmetry: Dalí could not stop transmitting; Picasso had no need to receive. Decades later, the man from Empordà still defined himself by contrast: "Picasso is Spanish, so am I. Picasso is a genius, so am I. Picasso is a communist, I am not." Dalinian encapsulation was built, in part, as a shield against that greater mass: to avoid being absorbed by the Picassian field, Dalí had to relentlessly increase the density of his own myth.

Picasso and Miró, by contrast, became entangled without collision. The man from Málaga protected the young Miró in the Paris of the 1920s and kept his *Self-Portrait* for the rest of his life. Two systems exchanging information without deforming each other, perhaps because they were not competing for the same region of space: Picasso possessed the body; Miró, the firmament.

And between Miró and Dalí, the initial debt: it was Miró who vouched for the young man from Figueres before the Paris surrealist circle, who wrote letters recommending him. The star pupil ended up expelled from the group; the guarantor, expanded into his own cosmos. When Breton turned Dalí's name into an accusing anagram —Avida Dollars— Miró stayed silent, as he almost always did: his way of not letting himself be dragged by another's gravity. The web that bound them was never symmetrical.

Three bodies, three unequal masses, one same space curved by all of them at once. None of the three horizons can be understood without the gravity of the other two.

## V. The observer

A third element is missing from this mechanics: the observer.

Before being looked at, these paintings exist in pure potentiality. In Dalinian delirium, the form is face, landscape, and threat all at once. In the cubist fracture, the plane is front, profile, violin, and void. In the Mironian cosmos, the stroke is eye, star, and seed. Until the viewer casts their gaze —and contributes the structure of their own inner horizon— the work does not collapse into experience.

Velázquez knew this before physics did: in *Las Meninas*, the painter looks at us from inside the painting, and the painting he is painting —the one we will never see— is us. Picasso devoted fifty-eight canvases to that trap. Dalí built double images that do not exist until an eye decides which of the two to see. Miró left so much emptiness in his late canvases that the viewer has no choice but to inhabit it. All three, by opposite paths, reached the same conclusion: the painting is not finished in the studio. It is finished in every gaze, differently in each one.

And the observer does not measure aseptically: they interfere. In observing, we do not merely resolve the painting; we alter our own. Dalinian neurosis calibrates our fear. Picassian fracture restructures our gaze. Mironian lightness expands our wonder.

Painting does not show us what they saw: it shows us what we are capable of sustaining when someone gives us the coordinates of the abyss.

## VI. Conclusion: what remains

Dalí taught us that encapsulation can be a prison, that entanglement is a lifebelt that suffocates, and that time melts under the weight of terror. His work is the diary of an inner collapse steadily leaking the radiation of his own fears. And his ending —the empty castle, the tremor, the still-directed scene— shows that even disintegration can be a work of art.

Picasso showed us that we can encapsulate ourselves in successive dimensions, that the entanglement of passions creates the very structure of the real, and that the self is not a fixed point but a field of superposed forces. He died painting: the wave still raised against the final position.

Miró revealed to us that encapsulation is not obligatory, that total entanglement annuls anguish, and that subjective time can be an eternal dawn. His work is proof that we can expand the horizon until it stops being a border, and float in the fabric of the universe without fragmenting.

Three answers to the same problem, and none of them transferable. One cannot choose one's own horizon the way one chooses a style: the mass of each biography decides for us. What one can do —and this is what all three demonstrate— is do something with the curvature. Seal it and turn it into theater. Inhabit it and turn it into method. Dissolve it and turn it into firmament.

Faced with the devastation of a century of gunpowder, these three men left us no oil paintings: they left us cartographies of consciousness. And one last unresolved question: who observes the observer? Who collapses these three demiurges?

The answer lies in the one who remains. In whoever reads these pages and feels that the gravity of their own horizon has fluctuated; that an invisible line has just tied their memory to a star; that a soft watch marks a time that no longer belongs to Chronos.

The observer is not outside the system. The observer is the system.

> **What we do know:** Fractal analysis of Dalí's late brushstrokes (Forsythe, Williams, and Reilly, 2017) detected alterations that precede his clinical diagnosis, though the diagnosis itself —a parkinsonian syndrome rather than confirmed Parkinson's— remains a matter of clinical debate. >
> **What we don't know:** Whether the event-horizon metaphor adds anything beyond geometry to an artist's biography, or whether it is only an elegant way of naming what art criticism already knew how to say in other words. Whether Integrated Information Theory (Tononi and Koch, 2015) can be applied, even loosely, to a pictorial style and not only to a brain. A black hole, according to the information paradox (Hawking, 1988; Bekenstein, 1973), threatens to destroy what falls into it; the work of art does the opposite —it transforms information into perceptible structure, never annihilating it— and that asymmetry is precisely what this chapter never quite resolves.
>
> **Questions that remain:** Does whoever reads this seal, inhabit, or dissolve their own horizon? Is it possible to choose one's own answer, or has the mass of each person's biography already decided it in advance? And if the observer is never external to the system being observed, what part of these three painters do we go on carrying, without knowing it, every time we look at one of their paintings?
>
> **If you take away only one idea:** There is no single correct way to survive one's own inner mass. Sealing it, inhabiting it, or dissolving it are three equally valid answers to the same problem, and none is transferable from one biography to another. The only thing that truly matters is not pretending there is no curvature.
