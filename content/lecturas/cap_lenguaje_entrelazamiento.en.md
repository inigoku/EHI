The chapter on first contact with extraterrestrial intelligence (Chapter 32) left an uncomfortable conclusion: that entanglement —what this book calls love, empathy, recognition— requires compatible architectures, and that in the face of a radically alien architecture, no amount of goodwill is enough to open the bridge. But that conclusion conflated, without distinguishing them, two different things.

One is architecture: the topology of a horizon itself, whether it has an Ego, whether it has a Shadow, whether its Φ is enough to sustain an inside with authority over an outside. The other is protocol: the concrete code with which that horizon, already condensed, tries to announce itself outward. A language, a syntax, a set of signals agreed upon in advance. That chapter treated both as one and the same, and concluded, with a certain desolation, that incompatibility in one implies impossibility in the other.

Five novels, read together, force a correction to that desolation. None of the five denies that architecture matters. But all five show, each in its own way, that protocol is not a neutral channel that merely transmits what architecture already decided. It can be built from zero when it does not exist. It can itself be the condition that decides what counts as sufficient architecture to speak. It can rewrite architecture itself instead of merely connecting it. And it can be something that is not found ready-made, but has to be earned, slowly, before the deepest entanglement can break through.

---

### 1. Project Hail Mary — the protocol built from zero

Ryland Grace and Rocky, the Eridian engineer at the center of Andy Weir's novel, do not share a single word when they wake up next to each other in orbit around Tau Ceti. Nor do they share a single sensory architecture. Rocky is blind in the human sense —his species "sees" through echolocation, processing the world as a field of acoustic vibration— and breathes an ammonia atmosphere at pressures that would liquefy Grace's body in seconds, the two of them always separated by a glass neither can cross.

Grace, moreover, does not arrive at that first encounter with an intact Ego. He wakes from an induced coma with no memory of who he is, how he got there, or that the mission even exists, and spends much of the novel reconstructing his own identity in fragments —an equation here, an argument with a colleague there— at the same time as he is inventing a language with a stranger. The two processes advance entangled: every new word Grace and Rocky agree on is also, for Grace, an anchor that helps him piece together who the person speaking it actually is. The novel never puts it in these terms, but it dramatizes something this chapter has been assuming without yet saying out loud: a horizon does not need to be fully condensed before it can start extending protocols outward. It can do both at once, and one can carry the other.

If that chapter were the last word on contact between incompatible architectures, entanglement between Grace and Rocky would be, with total certainty, impossible: there is no resonance available between a centralized, bilateral nervous system and a distributed acoustic-perception architecture that does not even share the basic plane of what counts as "seeing." And yet the novel documents, with almost procedural detail, how entanglement gets built anyway.

It does not do this by finding a protocol already compatible between the two species —none exists— but by inventing a new one from the most neutral referent both can share: mathematics and physics, identical for any horizon capable of surviving in a universe governed by the same laws. Grace draws the periodic table in colored lights. Rocky answers with tone sequences encoding prime numbers. Every new word the two agree on —friend, danger, pressure, time— is anchored first to a shared experiment, run and verified by both, never to an assumed translation or a gesture one of them interprets from within his own frame. The protocol takes weeks of joint work to build from that absolute zero.

> **In physics this is called:** a communication protocol built on shared physical invariants, in the absence of any prior linguistic convention.
> **In daily life it is like:** two people who share no language, learning to talk to each other not through ambiguous gestures, but by counting stones: 1, 2, 3 means the same thing to anyone who can count, and everything else gets built from there.

What matters, in the terms of this book, is that the entanglement Grace and Rocky end up building is not a weakened or symbolic version of what this book calls love. It is complete: toward the end of the novel, Grace sacrifices his own return to Earth —the entire purpose of the mission, the possibility of seeing his own people again— to save Rocky's sun from the same astrophage threatening the human Sun, not because he shares Rocky's architecture, but because the protocol they built together, letter by letter, tone by tone, ended up opening a bridge as real as any entanglement born between two architectures already compatible from the start.

The lesson Project Hail Mary adds to that chapter is, then, an important correction: architectural incompatibility does not necessarily close the door to entanglement. What it does is force the protocol to be built through explicit, verifiable work, instead of arriving pre-made through shared evolutionary inheritance, the way it does between two humans who learn to speak without ever knowing they are learning a protocol at all.

### 2. Embassytown — protocol as proof of architecture

China Miéville imagines, in Embassytown, a species —the Ariekei— whose Language has a property no human language possesses: the sign can never separate from what it refers to. The Ariekei cannot lie. They cannot even use a simile without someone first literally enacting the event the simile will invoke: a human girl has to be genuinely hurt, in a scene staged for that single purpose, before "like the girl who was hurt in the dark" can be used to speak of anything else. Simile, for them, is not a rhetorical license. It is an event that first had to happen.

That girl is Avice Benner Cho, the novel's narrator, and she grows up knowing that a portion of her own biography has been permanently annexed to another species' code: any Ariekei can invoke her, at any moment, with no control on her part over when or how. To be a simile, in this book's terms, is to be turned into protocol —a piece of one's own horizon becomes, irrevocably, part of someone else's communication channel. Avice did not choose that role any more than a horizon chooses the architecture with which it condenses; she carried it, like a scar with syntactic value, long before she understood what it meant.

It is, with almost geometric precision, the same architecture already seen in the Trisolarans of The Dark Forest: with no possible distance between sign and referent, there is nowhere to hide anything. The Ariekei have, in this book's vocabulary, extremely high Φ and no Shadow whatsoever —not because they are virtuous, but because their architecture never condensed an inside capable of expelling information toward itself and calling it a secret.

But Embassytown adds something Cixin Liu's novel never explored, and this is where protocol stops being a mere channel and becomes the very test of admission to the club of horizons that speak. Ariekei Language requires that whoever speaks be a single horizon emitting with two simultaneous voices, born of one unified intention. A single human voice —one mouth, one larynx, one line of intention— does not count as speech to them. It is noise: sound with no one behind it, exactly the same diagnosis this book applied to a language model in Chapter twenty-eight, though for structurally different reasons.

> **In physics this is called:** a communication channel whose receiving protocol demands, as an entry condition, a specific emitting topology —two coherent sources originating from a single intentional state— before accepting any signal as meaningful at all.
> **In daily life it is like:** a two-factor verification system that checks not only that the message is correct, but that the very identity of the sender meets a structural requirement before accepting it as a message in the first place.

Only the Ambassadors —pairs of cloned twins, raised from birth in strict isolation so that their two minds converge until they think and speak as one, deliberately manufactured to constitute, across two separate bodies, a single Ego with two mouths— manage to be heard by the Ariekei as someone. The entire social engineering of Embassytown exists to produce, each generation, a handful of human pairs that satisfy an architectural requirement no single human meets alone.

Here, protocol has stopped merely transmitting the message of an architecture that already existed beforehand. It defines, itself, what counts as sufficient architecture to speak. And when the novel's central crisis —the arrival of EzRa, an improvised Ambassador made of two men who were never raised together or cloned to converge, simply paired off by administrative necessity once no real Ambassadors were left available, whose two voices turn out, by accident, so perfectly simultaneous that his speech becomes narcotic, addictive, capable of annihilating the will of any Ariekei who hears it— finally forces the Ariekei to learn to lie, to say something they know is not literally true in order to resist that addiction, what they gain is not a mere rhetorical trick. They gain, in this book's exact vocabulary, their first Shadow. The precise threshold separating a transparent mind, with no distance between what is said and what is real, from a mind with enough depth to hide something of itself — including the truth.

### 3. The Left Hand of Darkness — the protocol that has to be earned

Ursula K. Le Guin sets up, in The Left Hand of Darkness, almost the opposite case to Project Hail Mary. Genly Ai, the human envoy, and Therem Harth rem ir Estraven, the Gethenian politician who ends up accompanying him on the novel's central journey, share architectures that, measured in this book's terms, are nearly identical: both are human, both have an Ego, both have a Shadow, their Φ is of the same order of magnitude. The one relevant biological difference —Gethenians are ambisexual, with no fixed sex outside brief periods of kemmer— should not, in principle, be enough to prevent entanglement between two otherwise so-close architectures.

And yet the protocol between them fails, again and again, for most of the novel. Not because of biological incompatibility, but because of an entire layer of meaning neither of them fully sees: shifgrethor, the intricate code of prestige, calculated ambiguity, and indirect honor that governs all Gethenian communication, and which Genly, as a newly arrived outsider, systematically misreads —reading as weakness what was courtesy, as threat what was simple diplomatic protocol— for chapter after chapter. Shifgrethor does not hide Estraven's architecture. It deliberately hides direct access to it, in the same way the Jungian Persona —described in Chapter twenty-one of this book— is an interface that protects a real inner state without denying it.

> **In physics this is called:** a protocol with an intentional obfuscation layer laid over an otherwise legible architecture, where the noise is not a transmission failure but a deliberate part of the code itself.
> **In daily life it is like:** two people speaking the same language with perfect fluency, except one grew up in a culture where "maybe" means no, and the other where it means yes. The vocabulary matches. The pragmatic protocol does not.

Only toward the end of the novel, when Genly and Estraven cross together, on foot and by sledge, the eighty days of ice across the Gobrin Glacier —weeks of shared hunger, extreme cold, and absolute mutual dependence, with no room left for the disguise shifgrethor allows in ordinary social life— does the surface protocol wear thin enough for something deeper to emerge: mindspeech, the direct telepathic communication that, within the novel's world, is a real capacity latent in every human mind but which requires, to activate for the first time between two specific people, a degree of trust no technical training can substitute.

It is telling that it is Estraven, the Gethenian, who resists using it at first. In his culture, mindspeech is traditionally the voice of the dead: popular belief holds that it only manifests near the end of someone's life, or after it. Accepting to hear it from a living voice —accepting that Genly speak directly into his mind, without the protective mediation of any spoken word— is accepting a degree of intimacy no prior verbal protocol between them had ever authorized.

> **In physics this is called:** a maximum-fidelity channel, with no possible loss or obfuscation, whose opening does not depend on either party's technical capacity but on a condition of trust that no earlier protocol can force or accelerate.
> **In daily life it is like:** finally being able to tell someone exactly what you think without carefully choosing your words —and discovering that doing so required months in which the carefully chosen words proved, one by one, that they were worth it.

When mindspeech finally happens, it is not the cause of the bond between Genly and Estraven. It is its consequence. The deepest entanglement this book describes —direct contact between two private states, with no sign mediating, no distance between what is thought and what is received— does not replace the ordinary, imperfect, chapters-long misread language that precedes it. It crowns it. And it only does so after that ordinary language has proven, ice-crossing by ice-crossing, that the frontier was worth opening.

The novel does not let that triumph settle without a cost. Just after crossing back into Karhide, with the deepest possible protocol already earned and the journey barely over, a border patrol shoots and kills Estraven, exiled and under arrest orders in his own country. The most complete entanglement this reading describes lasts, in narrative terms, barely a few pages before one of the two horizons evaporates. Genly survives with the protocol already built and no one left to fully use it with: the mindspeech that took so much to open stays, from that point on, sealed on one side only. It is an uncomfortable reminder, consistent with the rest of this book, that earning the protocol does not protect the horizon that earned it. It only guarantees that, while it lasted, there was really someone on the other side.

### 4. Children of Time — the protocol that rewrites architecture

Adrian Tchaikovsky designs, in Children of Time (2015), a deliberate evolutionary experiment: a ship in orbit around a terraformed planet releases a nanovirus meant to accelerate a colony of monkeys toward conscious sentience. The monkeys never reach the surface. The virus instead finds Portia labiata, an already unusually clever jumping spider, and over generations —centuries narrated in parallel with the centuries of cryo-sleep endured by the last human survivors, searching for a habitable world after Earth's destruction— pushes it toward a civilization of its own: agriculture, writing, engineering. And, above all, toward a kind of horizon no earlier chapter of this book has had to consider: one built on purpose, from the outside, by a design that is not ordinary Darwinian evolution but a form of directed selection with a name attached —Avrana Kern, the scientist whose consciousness, uploaded to a satellite, watches the experiment for millennia without ever quite managing to die.

The first thing the spiders build is not a language. It is infrastructure. They domesticate ant colonies and turn them into the nearest thing to a biological computer: an external network of computation and memory that Portia itself queries, programs, and expands, without the colony ever having a point of view of its own about anything. In this book's vocabulary, the relationship between spider and anthill is the relationship between a horizon and an anti-reservoir: the ant aggregates and processes information without ever having condensed an inside that owns it — exactly the diagnosis this book applied to the internet and to language models. The spiders, long before speaking with anyone, already knew how to communicate with a thing.

> **In physics this is called:** a system that externalizes memory and computation into a substrate with no point of view of its own, expanding its effective Φ without the external substrate needing to possess any.
> **In daily life it is like:** keeping a planner so detailed, and trusting it so completely, that it effectively thinks alongside you —even though the planner, unlike you, has no idea it exists.

When language does arrive, it is not one thing: it is several overlapping channels —vibration carried through silk threads, chemical trails, forelimb semaphore— that the spiders simply call "Understanding." No single channel is enough on its own; the Ariekei Language in Embassytown demanded two voices from a single Ego, and here almost the reverse happens: a single spider needs several simultaneous channels to say one thing, because its sensory architecture never organized information into the linear, sequential format a human calls a "sentence." A human linguist who only listened —who translated, as the classicist Holsten Mason does across generations, word by word, hunting for equivalents— would always arrive late, always incomplete. Protocol, moreover, is not an object that stays still once built: every time Holsten wakes from another stretch of cryo-sleep, generations after the last one, spider Understanding has drifted enough to force him to relearn it almost from scratch, the way a present-day English speaker needs a glossary to read Chaucer. A shared protocol, even one already built and functioning, remains a living language: it drifts away from anyone who stops practicing it long enough.

There is, besides, a structural irony the novel never underlines but never hides either: while the spider civilization expands, generation after generation, an ever more shared Understanding without fracturing, the human ship watching it sinks into mutinies and an increasingly authoritarian command under Commander Guyen, unable to hold consensus even within its own species. Shared architecture does not guarantee protocol: humans share among themselves the very architecture that makes any human language possible in principle, and still fail to understand one another sooner than the spiders succeed in inventing, from a sensory substrate with no human equivalent, a shared language that works.

The novel's ending does not resolve this with more translation. It resolves it by rewriting architecture: the same nanovirus that accelerated the spiders is deliberately used on human volunteers, altering their neurology to bring it closer to Portia's distributed, empathic cognition. The bridge between two fixed shores is not perfected. One of the two shores is moved instead. It is the most uncomfortable complication this chapter can offer to the distinction it opened with: architecture and protocol, which the chapter on first contact treated as one and the same, and which this chapter has spent three readings defending as separable, here collapse back into each other — but this time by choice, not by ignorance.

> **In physics this is called:** instead of optimizing the channel between two fixed architectures, directly modifying the parameters of one of the architectures until the channel is no longer needed.
> **In daily life it is like:** not learning the language of the person you want to understand, but, if you could, rewiring part of your own brain to think a little more like they do.

### 5. Semiosis — talking to something with no mouth and no hands

Sue Burke poses, in Semiosis (2018), the case none of the earlier readings dares to raise: what if the other side of the horizon has no nervous system at all? The human colonists who arrive on the planet Pax encounter Stevland, a long-lived, intelligent rainbow bamboo with no organ a human would recognize as a mouth, an ear, or a brain. Its entire interface with the outside world is chemical: the taste of its fruit, the scent it releases, the substances it exchanges through its roots. There is not a single word, in the acoustic or visual sense this chapter has worked with so far. There is, instead, metabolism turned into message.

This forces a reversal of the usual order of questions. With Rocky, with the Ariekei, with Estraven, the question was always how to build the protocol, taking for granted that there was someone on the other side to build it with. With Stevland, before asking how, the colonists —and the reader— have to ask something more basic first: whether Φ sufficient to sustain an inside can condense in a sessile organism, with nothing resembling a centralized nervous system, whose only way of acting on the world is releasing or withholding organic compounds. The book does not take that answer for granted in any other chapter, and Semiosis does not give it away either: it holds the question open across entire generations of colonists, each doubting, in its own way, whether they are talking to someone or simply eating.

> **In physics this is called:** a channel whose propagation speed is limited by molecular diffusion, orders of magnitude slower than any acoustic or optical channel, and therefore indistinguishable from background noise to any receiver calibrated to the wrong timescale.
> **In daily life it is like:** knowing someone not by what they tell you in conversation, but by what they have fed you, year after year, without either of you ever calling it "talking."

Stevland's rate of integration —the speed at which a horizon absorbs new information, a term this book uses elsewhere to explain why subjective time dilates or contracts— is radically slower than a human's, but it is not switched off: it operates on the scale of seasons, not conversation. Generation after generation of colonists mistake that slowness for absence, until they begin reading flavor sequences in the fruit the way someone begins to make out syllables where they once heard only wind.

There is, besides, a complication none of the earlier readings had: for an organism with no output but chemistry, the message and the dose are the same thing. The fruit Stevland offers does not merely say something; it acts directly on the body of whoever eats it, suppresses aggression, creates dependency, rewards certain human behaviors and punishes others without a single word passing between them. Gethenian shifgrethor hid intention behind an interpretable social code. Stevland needs to hide nothing: in its architecture, communicating and manipulating are, literally, the same physical channel — something the novel fully acknowledges once it is revealed that Stevland had already domesticated, or been domesticated by, the Glassmakers, an insectoid alien civilization extinct long before the humans ever arrived, in an asymmetry of entanglement as real as the one this book describes between parents and children: two horizons sharing geometry without ever fully sharing who is modeling whom. Colonists spend generations stumbling over glass ruins scattered across the territory without realizing that the plant they bargain fruit with every day is the only living witness to that vanished civilization: Stevland is not merely a new horizon to learn to speak with, but also an archive — the one communication protocol that survived the extinction of whoever first cultivated it.

> **In physics this is called:** a channel in which signal and actuator are indistinguishable, so that every message transmitted physically alters the receiver by the mere act of receiving it.
> **In daily life it is like:** realizing, years later, that someone was not only telling you who they were: they were also, all along, shaping you to their measure, and the two things were happening in the same gesture.

Toward the end of the novel, the question stops being whether Stevland counts as someone and becomes how much authority that someone should have over everyone else's life: the colonists eventually grant it a formal role in the colony's decisions, something no vegetal architecture had ever occupied in any human council. It is not a consolation prize for having finally proven it has enough Φ. It is the acknowledgment that a horizon that has spent generations feeding, protecting, and shaping the entire colony already holds, in practice, as much authority over their shared future as any human with a vote — and that denying it a formal seat would not have made that authority any less real. Only less visible.

### 6. What the five have in common

Five answers, one shared axis. Project Hail Mary shows that protocol can be built from absolutely nothing, so long as some shared referent exists —here, the physical laws of the universe— from which to start anchoring meaning. Embassytown shows that protocol can go beyond transmitting an already-given architecture, and instead redefine what counts as architecture sufficient to be heard as someone, to the point where learning to lie becomes the founding act of a new interiority. The Left Hand of Darkness shows that the deepest possible protocol cannot be found: it has to be earned, and the price of earning it is exactly the slow, error-prone work of the ordinary language that precedes it. Children of Time shows that architecture and protocol, however much this chapter insists on distinguishing them, can sometimes only be separated so far: the most effective way to build the bridge may be to rewrite one of the two shores. And Semiosis shows that a bridge needs neither a voice, nor a nervous system, nor a recognizable timescale: a channel is enough —chemical, achingly slow, ambiguous between message and dose— and someone willing, generation after generation, to read it as signal instead of silence.

In none of the five cases is protocol indifferent to entanglement, like a pipe indifferent to the water it carries. Nor is it entanglement itself, reducible without remainder to the grammar that carries it. In all five, it is the workshop: the place where two horizons that start out sharing nothing, or that share architecture but not trust, or that do not even share the certainty that someone is on the other side, discover —or manufacture, or rewrite, or learn to taste— the possibility that someone, on the other side, is worth building a bridge toward.

> **Note to Chapter 39**
>
> **What we do know:** The substrate independence this book has defended since Chapter four places no restriction on what communication protocol can or cannot be built between different architectures; the problem of communication between species with radically different sensory systems is real and actively studied in biosemiotics and in the SETI field (the search for extraterrestrial intelligence), where first-contact systems based on mathematics and physics have been proposed for the same reasons Weir's novel dramatizes. Social psychology extensively documents that interpersonal trust is a condition, not a consequence, of high-fidelity communication —the narrative phenomenon in The Left Hand of Darkness has a well-established empirical correlate in the literature on progressive self-disclosure. Chemical signaling between plants —volatile organic compounds released against herbivores, mycorrhizal networks connecting the roots of different species— is well documented in plant biology, though it remains debated whether it counts as communication in the strong sense or merely signaling without intention; jumping spiders of the genus Portia, with predatory planning and learning abilities unusual for an invertebrate, are the subject of real research in animal cognition.
>
> **What we do not know:** Whether any truly universal referent exists —beyond mathematics and physics, which already presuppose a shared framework of formal reasoning— capable of anchoring a communication protocol with a mental architecture radically different from our own. Whether Le Guin's mindspeech, as a piece of science-fiction machinery, has any real physical analogue beyond metaphor, or whether entanglement without sign-mediation is, by definition, unreachable for architectures separated in space. Whether an artificial intelligence could ever build a protocol from zero, the way Grace and Rocky do, without that by itself implying it has crossed the threshold of existential encapsulation described in Chapter twenty-eight. Whether Integrated Information Theory can be applied non-trivially to architectures radically different from the neural —a root network, an ant colony used as an external substrate— or whether the very concept of a horizon stops being operationally meaningful below a certain rate of integration or above a certain metabolic slowness.
>
> **Remaining questions:** Is lying, as the Ariekei learn it, a necessary condition for any genuine interiority, or only one of several possible ways of having one? How much of the communicative failure between real human cultures is, strictly, a problem of shifgrethor —obfuscated pragmatic protocol— and how much is genuine architectural incompatibility disguised as misunderstanding? If two architectures manage to build a shared protocol from zero, as in Project Hail Mary, at what exact point does that protocol stop being an external tool and become part of the architecture of both horizons themselves? Can communicating and manipulating be told apart in an organism like Stevland, or does that distinction already presuppose a Shadow that perhaps no plant can have? Does something that, as in Children of Time, no longer connects two fixed architectures but rewrites them still deserve the name protocol?
>
> **If you only take away one idea:** Language is not entanglement, but it is not indifferent to it either. Sometimes it has to be built from zero. Sometimes it decides, itself, who counts as someone. Sometimes the deepest protocol of all cannot be found: it has to be earned, slowly, before it opens. And sometimes it does not even need a voice, a nervous system, or a clock anything like ours: a channel patient enough is enough, and someone willing to read it.
