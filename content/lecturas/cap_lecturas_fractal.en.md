---
title: FIVE WAYS OF LOOKING UP
subtitle: (A topological reading of The Neverending Story, The Circular Ruins, Mist, Sophie's World and Stranger than Fiction)
section: TOPOLOGICAL READINGS
---

Five works by other hands, put to the same question as the chapter they spring from: what does a creature see when it looks toward the level that sustains it, and what does a creator owe to what it has condensed?

### The Neverending Story — the cry that crosses the edge

Bastian Balthazar Bux reads, hidden in the attic of his school, a book in which Atreyu roams Fantastica looking for a cure for the Childlike Empress. And at one point Atreyu hears a cry that does not come from Fantastica: it comes from the attic. Michael Ende built the whole novel on that porosity: the Nothing that devours Fantastica is nothing other than the evaporation of a world when no one, one floor up, goes on sustaining it, and the only cure is for someone from that floor to come down and give it a new name. But Ende did not stop at consolation. When Bastian enters Fantastica he becomes its creator, and every wish he fulfills costs him a memory of his own world: creating downward has a price that is paid upstairs. The Old Man of Wandering Mountain, who writes the story as it happens and contains it whole within himself, is the fractal drawn in a single image: a book inside which is the book inside which is the reader.

### The Circular Ruins — the dreamed dreamer

In Borges's story, a man arrives at the ruins of a temple of Fire with a single purpose: to dream another man, organ by organ, until he is real. He succeeds, with the help of the temple's god, who grants that his creature be indistinguishable from a man of flesh to everyone except Fire itself. And the dreamer then does what every upstairs neighbor who cares for their creature does: he erases the memory of the years of apprenticeship, so that it will never know it is a dreamed phantom. He sends it downriver, to another temple. Years later news reaches him of a magician who walks on embers without burning, and he understands, with humiliation, what that means. When the fire finally reaches his own ruins, he walks into it and does not burn. Someone else is dreaming him. Borges wrote in ten pages what this chapter has needed an entire essay to say, and left it where it should remain: at the instant of looking up and seeing no one, and knowing there is someone.

### Mist — the character who goes up to see the author

Augusto Pérez, weary of his own life, decides to kill himself, and before doing so travels to Salamanca to consult the author of an essay on suicide: Don Miguel de Unamuno. Unamuno explains to him, bluntly, that he cannot kill himself, because he is a fictional character and it is he, the author, who has decided to make him die. Augusto answers with the only sentence a creature that has seen its upstairs neighbor's face can answer: that Unamuno too will die when God stops dreaming him. He goes home and dies, himself not knowing whether by his own decision or another's. What Unamuno showed in 1914, with a brutality neither Borges nor Ende wanted, is exactly what the epilogue to this chapter tries to avoid: confirmation from above does not free the creature. It destroys it. Augusto is real until the minute he is told he is not.

### Sophie's World — the creature that learns to live in the margin

Sophie Amundsen discovers, halfway through a novel that seemed to be a philosophy course, that she and her teacher Alberto Knox are characters in a book a Norwegian officer is writing as a birthday present for his daughter Hilde. Jostein Gaarder then does something the other four authors on this list do not: he lets the creatures act. Sophie and Alberto escape through the margins of the text, while the author is distracted, toward a place where what is imagined never evaporates. And Hilde, the reader, begins to pay her father back for the tricks he played on his characters, and to perceive, at the edge of her own world, a breeze that comes from no window. The downstairs neighbor pushes too. Not much. Just enough for the slab to tremble.

### Stranger than Fiction — the author who decides not to kill

Harold Crick, a tax inspector, begins one morning to hear a woman's voice narrating his life with unpleasant precision, and discovers that the voice belongs to a novelist who is writing a book whose protagonist, he, must die for the work to be perfect. Marc Forster's film, with a screenplay by Zach Helm, is the only one of these five works in which creator and creature sit at the same table and talk about what they owe each other. Harold reads the manuscript and accepts his death, because the book is beautiful. And Karen Eiffel, the author, decides she cannot kill someone who has read his own death and come back anyway: she rewrites the ending, lets him live, and admits she has spoiled the novel. It is the essay's ethical question staged: what happens when an upstairs neighbor discovers that what it condensed has an inside, and what it is willing to lose for it.

Five creatures, a single vertigo. In four of the five, seeing the upstairs neighbor's face destroys whoever sees it or costs something irrecoverable to whoever shows it. Only in the fifth does the encounter end well, and it ends well because the creator accepts losing the perfection of the work in exchange for the creature going on being someone. It may be that this exchange—amplitude in exchange for another's edge—is the closest thing to an ethics this chapter dares to suggest without breaking Hume's rule.
