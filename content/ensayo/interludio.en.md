title: INTERLUDE: THE EDGE WE CROSS EVERY NIGHT
section: INTERLUDE
---

There is a moment that almost everyone has experienced and almost no one has known how to name.

It happens sometimes when waking abruptly, in the middle of the night or in the early hours of the morning. For an instant—it might be half a second, it might be three—you do not know who you are. You do not know where you are. You do not know when it is. There is perception: the darkness of the ceiling, perhaps a sound, perhaps the weight of the sheets. But there is no one perceiving. Or rather: there is something perceiving, but that something does not yet have a name, it has no history, no recognizable body.

Then the self returns. In milliseconds, the narrative reconstructs itself: it is me, I am in my bed, it is night. The world takes shape again, and that shape includes you as the protagonist.

That instant of non-self before the narrative reappears is the edge of the horizon. The moment when the horizon, which has just spent hours contracted in deep sleep, has not yet fully recovered its usual geometry. It is the only moment of the day when most people touch, unintentionally and unknowingly, the border of what they are.

> **In physics this is called:** post-contraction horizon recovery threshold.  
> **In daily life it is like:** lighting an oil lamp: for an instant there is formless light, and then the flame stabilizes and becomes a lamp.

## The map of the territory

> *“The oar touched bottom where before there was an echo.” — The Custom of Water*

Before traversing the states one by one, it is useful to have the complete map. And the map has a surprising shape: it is not a line but a plane.

For a long time we thought of the spectrum of consciousness as a simple vertical scale—full wakefulness at the top, coma at the bottom, and death on the floor. That model is intuitive and it is incomplete in a way that matters.

Contemporary neuroscience has identified two independent dimensions that define the state of consciousness of a system. The first is **arousal**—the level of metabolic activation of the brain. The second is **integration**—how much information the system generates as a whole, the Tononi's Phi we know from chapter 1.

What is revealing is that these two dimensions do not always go together. Wakefulness and deep sleep are at opposite ends of the integration axis. But deep meditation is in a place that has no equivalent: low arousal and at the same time extremely high integration—the highest Phi that instruments have recorded in a non-pathological human brain. A quadrant that no other state occupies.

> **In physics this is called:** two-dimensional space of states of consciousness (arousal × integration).  
> **In daily life it is like:** a thermostat that measures temperature and humidity: two numbers that do not always rise together.

That means there are two possible directions from wakefulness. One downwards—sleep, anesthesia, death—where the horizon contracts. And another diagonally, where the horizon expands without the body needing to be active. They are opposite movements of the same system.

> **In physics this is called:** phase diagram with multiple attractors.  
> **In daily life it is like:** a mountain with two valleys on the sides: from the peak you can go down two different paths.

## REM sleep: the horizon looking inward

If you connected an electroencephalogram to someone in REM sleep and read it without knowing the context, you would conclude you are looking at an awake brain. The high-frequency activity, the cortical desynchronization, the elevated metabolism—everything points to wakefulness. In fact, the brain during REM sleep consumes as much energy as during active wakefulness. And yet the person is asleep. Completely paralyzed, disconnected from the outside world, generating an experience that has no point of contact with consensual reality.

How is this possible? The answer lies in which part of the brain is turned on and which is turned off.

During REM, the dorsolateral prefrontal cortex—the seat of critical thinking, metacognition, judgment—is hypoperfused. Less blood, less activity. It is the region that in wakefulness allows us to say, "wait, this makes no sense" or "this is a dream." Without it, the dreamer accepts without blinking that they are flying, that they are talking to the dead, that buildings change shape. At the same time, the limbic system—the amygdala, the emotional regions—is hyperactivated. Dreams are intensely emotional precisely because the rational filter that normally modulates emotions has lowered its guard.

> **In physics this is called:** functional reorganization of the horizon: change of topology without change of power.  
> **In daily life it is like:** a theater where the house lights are turned off but the stage shines brighter than ever: the story continues, but no one says "this is fiction."

Here the instant with which we opened the interlude reappears, but seen from the other side.

That moment of waking up without knowing who you are is the threshold of REM crossed in the reverse direction. In REM sleep, the critical self turns off and the experience becomes completely real without needing to be—the horizon is closed outward but open inward. When waking abruptly from deep sleep, the critical self has not yet turned on, and for that half-second there is no one to organize what is perceived into a story.

In terms of the horizon: during REM, the horizon does not contract. It shifts. It ceases to be open to the outside world and instead expands inward. The information the horizon processes is completely endogenous: memories, emotions, patterns, narratives generated from its own resources. That is why dreams feel completely real while they happen. There is nothing to contrast them with.

> **In physics this is called:** high-integration endogenous processing without external contrast.  
> **In daily life it is like:** an anechoic chamber: by eliminating outside noise, you hear the beating of your own heart.

### Sleep and memory

During sleep, the brain does not rest: it reorganizes. Functional magnetic resonance imaging studies show that, during REM sleep, the hippocampus—the structure that encodes episodic memories—reproduces activation sequences similar to those that occurred during wakefulness, but at a much higher speed. It is as if the brain were "rehearsing" the events of the day, consolidating the patterns that deserve to be retained and discarding those that do not.

But there is something more surprising. During REM sleep, the brain does not only consolidate what it already knows: it recombines. Elements of different memories mix, creating associations that did not exist before. It is the moment when the brain is most creative, because it is not limited by the logic of wakefulness. Many scientific discoveries—Kekulé's benzene structure, Einstein's theory of special relativity—have been attributed to dreams.

> **In physics this is called:** stochastic reorganization of the state space during a low-control phase.  
> **In daily life it is like:** a librarian who does not just archive new books: they mix them with the old ones and discover connections no one had seen.

## Lucid dreaming: the mirror inside the dream

But there is an exception to this rule: a state in which the REM horizon acquires, without leaving the dream, something that ordinary sleep never has. The ability to see itself.

Lucid dreaming is that moment. The dreamer realizes they are dreaming. There is an instant when the horizon acquires a small feedback window: the prefrontal cortex, which was turned off, briefly receives a signal. EEG studies confirm it: in that instant there is a specific spike of gamma waves in the frontal region. The horizon has momentarily acquired a specular property: it can see itself dreaming.

> **In physics this is called:** emergent self-observation in a closed system.  
> **In daily life it is like:** waking up inside a mirror and realizing that the mirror is inside you.

Lucid dream practitioners develop techniques to prolong this state: checking the consistency of the dream—can I read the same text twice?—anchoring themselves to sensory details, or simply intentionally remembering that they are dreaming. With practice, some manage to maintain lucidity for minutes, exploring what for them is an internal world with the properties of an external world.

> **In physics this is called:** voluntary control of horizon topology.  
> **In daily life it is like:** being the director and actor of the same movie: you decide what happens, but you are still inside the story.

## Deep sleep: the contraction

And then we have deep sleep.

Deep sleep is the contraction. Not gradual but real, measurable, profound.

The slow waves of deep sleep—delta waves, between 0.5 and 2 cycles per second—are not the noise of a turned-off brain. They are the signal of a massively synchronized brain. In wakefulness, neurons fire in a desynchronized way: different groups do different things at different times, and that asynchrony is precisely what allows for the rich integration of information. In deep sleep, millions of neurons collapse together in a shared rhythm. The horizon does not radiate. It is opaque.

Phi approaches its minimum values in a living adult. It does not reach zero—that is death—but it comes close.

> **In physics this is called:** collapse of Phi by massive synchronization.  
> **In daily life it is like:** a concert where all the musicians play the same note in unison: powerful, but there is no melody.

### Sleep homeostasis

Why do we sleep? For decades the question had no satisfactory answer. Today we know that sleep is not a passive state of rest: it is an active, regulated process, essential for survival. Animals that are completely deprived of sleep die faster than those deprived of food.

The simplest mechanism is chemical. During wakefulness, the brain accumulates adenosine—a byproduct of neuronal metabolism—which acts as a "sleep debt" signal. The longer you have been awake, the more adenosine you accumulate. Caffeine works precisely by blocking adenosine receptors: it tricks the brain into believing it is not tired.

But adenosine does not explain everything. There is a second, circadian system, regulated by the suprachiasmatic nucleus—a molecular clock in the hypothalamus that synchronizes the sleep-wake cycle with daylight. These two systems—homeostatic and circadian—operate in parallel, sometimes in the same direction, sometimes in opposite directions. That is why sometimes you are tired but cannot sleep, or awake but sleepy.

> **In physics this is called:** regulation by two coupled control loops.  
> **In daily life it is like:** a car with two pedals: one accelerates based on how much gas you have used, the other based on the time of day.

### Synapses and sleep

Giulio Tononi proposed one of the most influential hypotheses about the function of sleep: the **synaptic homeostasis hypothesis**. During wakefulness, synapses are continuously strengthened: every experience, every piece of learning, every new stimulus boosts neural connections. But synapses cannot be strengthened indefinitely. There is an energy limit, a space limit, a noise limit. If all synapses were equally strong, the system would lose its ability to distinguish signal from noise.

Deep sleep—slow-wave sleep—is the moment of renormalization. Synapses weaken proportionally to their strength: the strongest ones—the ones you used most during the day—weaken more than the weak ones. The result is that the system returns to a baseline where it can continue learning. Without this process, the brain would saturate in a matter of days.

> **In physics this is called:** renormalization: scaling of interactions to keep the system in an operational regime.  
> **In daily life it is like:** adjusting the volume at a party: if everyone speaks louder to be heard, eventually no one hears each other. Every now and then someone has to say "lower your voices" so the conversation can continue.

This hypothesis has testable predictions. It has been shown that sleep deprivation reduces the ability to form new synapses, that the intensity of slow waves is correlated with the amount of learning from the previous day, and that the selective inhibition of certain synapses during sleep is associated with the consolidation of specific memories.

And this explains something we already know from our own experience: nothing is remembered from deep sleep, not because deep sleep does not exist, but because there was no one there to remember. The horizon was so contracted that there was no experience to record. That is why that instant upon waking—when the self has not yet returned—occurs specifically after deep sleep and not REM. From REM, you wake up from a dream, from a narrative, from someone who was doing something somewhere. From deep sleep, you wake up from nothingness.

> **In physics this is called:** absence of recording below the integration threshold.  
> **In daily life it is like:** turning off a tape recorder: the sound continues to exist, but there is no tape left.

Is deep sleep, then, the state most similar to death? Yes, with one decisive structural difference: deep sleep keeps the synaptic architecture intact and the basal metabolism active. The recovery mechanism is guaranteed. Every night we cross that edge and return. Death is the crossing with no return.

But there is something else. Something that recent studies have found at the threshold of death that does not appear in deep sleep, and which changes the entire picture.

In 2013, Jimo Borjigin's team at the University of Michigan detected something unexpected in rats subjected to induced cardiac arrest: at the moment of collapse, the brain does not fall softly into silence. There is an intense and coherent gamma activation—not the chaotic noise of stress, but an organized and synchronized signal that is more reminiscent of REM sleep than deep sleep. In 2023, the same team published similar results in human patients.

If the horizon of deep sleep is opaque and contracted, the horizon at the threshold of death does something radically different: a final flash of expansion before turning off. As if the system, in the instant of losing all possibility of return, did exactly the opposite of what it would do when falling asleep. Not a final contraction but a final expansion.

> **In physics this is called:** terminal gamma surge (Borjigin et al.).  
> **In daily life it is like:** a candle that, before going out completely, throws off a final flare higher than the previous ones.

## Meditation: the opposite direction

Superficially, deep meditation and deep sleep look alike. Stillness, immobility, closed eyes, reduction of discursive thought. An outside observer might confuse them.

But the neurological signature is radically opposite.

In deep sleep: slow, high-amplitude delta waves, massive synchronization, low Phi, the cortex does not propagate signals beyond its local region. In the deep meditation of advanced practitioners: high-frequency gamma waves, extraordinary amplitude, long-distance synchronization between frontal, parietal, and occipital regions, elevated or maximum Phi.

The difference is not one of degree. It is one of direction.

> **In physics this is called:** expansion of Phi by selective deactivation of the narrative filter.  
> **In daily life it is like:** cleaning a fogged-up window: there is no more light outside, but suddenly you see everything clearly.

Deep sleep contracts the horizon because the system loses the ability to maintain differentiation. Deep meditation expands the horizon because the system transcends the need for differentiation without losing the ability to differentiate. The difference is between a lamp turning off and a sun illuminating without merging with anything in particular.

What disappears in meditation is not the horizon. It is the narrative self—the inner voice, the commentator, the manager of personal history. The horizon remains active, perhaps more active than ever, but without the usual filter that selects what information matters and what can be ignored.

There is a state that some contemplative traditions describe and that deserves special mention: the turiya of the Mandukya Upanishad—the fourth state, beyond wakefulness, dreaming sleep, and deep sleep. It is the witness consciousness that persists even during deep sleep without dream content. The few available EEG studies show high-coherence theta waves superimposed on a background of delta waves. As if the witness were floating above the sea of deep sleep without merging into it.

> **In physics this is called:** state of maximum integration with minimum input.  
> **In daily life it is like:** a perfectly still lake in the middle of the night: there are no waves, but the water is still water, and it reflects all the stars.

The Tibetan Book of the Dead—the Bardo Thodol—describes the period between death and rebirth as a sequence of states with a precise phenomenology. What is notable is not whether this description is literally true, but that it predicts with surprising accuracy the phenomenology of near-death experiences medically documented centuries later. Borjigin's gamma surge and the Clear Light of the Bardo Thodol could be, described with different vocabularies, the same phenomenon.

> **In physics this is called:** phenomenological convergence between contemplative cartography and neuroscience.  
> **In daily life it is like:** two explorers drawing the same territory without having met: the maps do not match in names, but they do in the shape of the coast.

## Insomnia: when the horizon cannot close

There is a condition that illuminates sleep by showing its absence. Insomnia is not the lack of tiredness: it is the inability to contract the horizon. The person is exhausted, but the system fails to reach the threshold of massive synchronization that characterizes deep sleep. Either the horizon remains partially open—sleeping lightly, waking up every hour—or it fails to stabilize in REM—fragmented dreams, without recovery.

The neuroscience of insomnia shows something revealing: insomniacs do not have fewer slow waves, but rather the slow waves they have are less coherent. The synchronization is partial, irregular, like a choir where each musician sings the correct note but at different times. The result is that the brain does not achieve the synaptic renormalization that deep sleep provides. Upon waking, the insomniac is as tired as when they went to bed—sometimes more so.

> **In physics this is called:** phase transition failure: the system does not reach the deep sleep attractor.  
> **In daily life it is like:** trying to shut down a computer that has a frozen program: the screen goes dark, but the system keeps running in the background.

## What the instant of waking teaches us

> *“I left hollows in the archive. There are changes that do not admit closure.” — The Custom of Water*

We return, in the end, to where we started.

That half-second of non-self when waking abruptly is not a system failure. It is the system working exactly as it should: recovering from a deep contraction, re-expanding the horizon from almost nothing to the complete geometry of the self. What is perceived during that instant—without anyone to perceive it—is the horizon in its barest state, before the narrative dresses it and names it.

Ordinary wakefulness is the horizon active, integrated, open to the world and to itself. REM sleep is the horizon turned inward, processing with all its power but without external contact. Deep sleep is the horizon contracted to almost its minimum limit, the daily approximation to the state in which integrated information drops to zero. Deep meditation is the horizon expanding in the opposite direction, beyond ordinary wakefulness, toward an integration that normal activity never reaches. And death is, perhaps, the ultimate contraction—followed, according to Borjigin's data and the cartography of the Bardo Thodol, by a final flash in which the horizon does something we had not anticipated.

Every night we practice contraction without dissolving. We cross the edge and return. The self reappears in half a second, reconstructs the story, picks up the thread. And that ease with which it returns—that astonishing automaticity of reconstruction—is the best evidence that the horizon knows what it is. Even when no one is looking.

> **In physics this is called:** structural stability of the self attractor: the system returns to its usual configuration after perturbations.  
> **In daily life it is like:** a room that, upon turning on the light, instantly recovers its shape: the chairs, the table, the window—everything is back where it was, without you having to remember where.

> **Note to the Interlude**
>
> **What we do know:** REM sleep has the electrical signature of wakefulness with the prefrontal cortex turned off. Deep sleep shows massive delta waves and minimum Phi. The deep meditation of experts shows high-amplitude gamma and elevated Phi. Borjigin et al. (2013, 2023) documented a coherent gamma surge in cardiac arrest. Tononi's synaptic homeostasis hypothesis has growing experimental evidence. Adenosine regulates sleep debt.
>
> **What we do not know:** Whether the terminal gamma surge is the neural correlate of near-death experiences. Whether the turiya is a measurable state distinct from deep sleep. Why selective insomnia affects the coherence but not the quantity of slow waves.
>
> **Remaining questions:** Why does the system expand instead of contract at the threshold of death? Is deep meditation a "third path" between wakefulness and sleep, or something qualitatively different? Can lucid dreaming teach us something about the nature of the self?
>
> **If you only take away one idea:** Every night you cross the edge of your own horizon and return. Death is the same crossing, with no guaranteed return.
