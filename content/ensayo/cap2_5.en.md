With that established, we reach the point where the experiment must shift its register.

Up to here, the reader has traversed the physics of horizons and integrated information theory. They have seen how a black hole encodes information on its boundary, how Phi describes the irreducible integration of a system, and how a bubble, upon closing, suddenly creates an inside and an outside. It might seem like all of this is still physics. It is not. Or at least, not solely physics anymore.

What follows is not a literal extension of physics, but a philosophical interpretation that uses those concepts as scaffolding. The central thesis of this book is the following:

> *Consciousness is the interior of an encapsulated informational domain.*

Not a metaphor. Not a decorative possibility. Not a poetic way of talking about the brain. It is the hypothesis that organizes everything that comes next.

---

A conscious system would not simply be an information processor observable from the outside, but an architecture with two levels of access. From the exterior, we only see its public interface: behavior, language, neural activity, temperature, movement, response. From the interior, however, there is a private state: the unrepeatable way in which that information is integrated for someone. That private state is not a separate substance nor a ghost housed in the machine. It is the inside that appears when information is organized in a sufficiently integrated manner and remains protected by a boundary.

Classical neuroscience does not find thought under the microscope for the same reason that an external observer cannot directly inspect the interior of an event horizon. It is not that thought does not exist. It is that it is not public. What the scanner sees are correlates, surfaces, emissions, changes in flow, electrical signals: the interface. Subjective experience is the private state of the system, and asking it to appear as an external object is to confuse the level of observation.

> **In physics this is called:** access dualism; public interface vs. private state.
> **In daily life it is like:** seeing the exterior of a lit house from the street: you can see that there is light inside, you can see the shadows moving, but you cannot know what conversation is happening inside without entering—and entering destroys the condition of being an external observer.

## Encapsulation as a cosmic law: From nature to engineering

When software engineers coined the term *encapsulation* and the principle of *information hiding* (David Parnas, 1972), they were not inventing a design rule out of thin air. They were discovering and formalizing, for artificial systems, the same principle that nature has used for billions of years to build its **hierarchy of abstraction**.

In the real universe, complexity is not governed in a flat manner. Physics and biology manage complexity by packaging states and behaviors into hermetic layers. Quarks are encapsulated in protons; protons are packaged into atoms; atoms are encapsulated in molecules; molecules are encapsulated in organelles; organelles are encapsulated in cells, and cells in organisms. 

Each level of this hierarchy functions as a boundary of abstraction. A cell processing glucose does not calculate the quantum states of the electrons in the carbon atoms; it does not need to, because that immense lower-level complexity is hidden behind a simplified interface (chemical gradients and molecular signals). If the universe did not encapsulate information in this way, reality would be an unstable and noisy quantum soup: any fluctuation at the Planck scale would propagate immediate destructive effects to the higher levels, making the emergence of stable structures impossible. Encapsulation is what allows scales to exist and interact safely.

When software engineering reached its own crisis of complexity in the 20th century (the problem of flat, coupled code prone to global failures), system designers were forced to imitate this natural order. They discovered that to build stable complex systems (operating systems, networks, applications), they had to draw boundaries of abstraction: group related logic and data within an object or module, hide their implementation details behind a public API, and prohibit direct access to their internal variables (`private`). 

Consciousness, according to the horizon model, is not a magical phenomenon disconnected from the rest of the cosmos; it is simply the ultimate level in this natural hierarchy of abstraction. The brain, a hypercomplex biological system with trillions of chemical fluctuations and neural firings per second, encapsulates all that immense low-level parallel computation behind a single simplified interface: the first-person perspective, the self. Subjectivity is the public API through which the horizon interacts with its environment, hiding the private cellular implementation to allow the system to make decisions in real time without drowning in its own execution data.

## The Hard Problem reformulated

The Hard Problem of consciousness—why is physical processing accompanied by subjective experience?—begins to be reformulated here. Perhaps it is not a matter of explaining how a mental substance miraculously arises from matter, but of recognizing that our expectation of a completely public universe was mistaken. If reality admits encapsulated domains, if there are regions where the interior and the exterior are not symmetrical, then subjectivity is not an anomaly: it is what an encapsulated informational domain is from the inside.

We do not live, therefore, in a dualism of substances. We live in a **dualism of access**.

This is different and more manageable. Substance dualism says that there are two types of things in the world—matter and mind—that are fundamentally distinct and whose interaction is mysterious. Access dualism says that there is only one type of thing in the world, but that certain structures of those things can be accessed in two mutually exclusive ways: from the outside, as an object; from the inside, as a subject.

> **In physics this is called:** informational access asymmetry; closed vs. open topology.
> **In daily life it is like:** a sealed letter: it exists for the one who keeps it even if no one else can read it. There are not two letters—one material, one mental. There is one letter, and two reading positions: the one who keeps it and the one who observes it from the outside.

---

From the outside, the horizon is a boundary. From the inside, it is a world.

And the interior of that horizon—that inside which no one can inspect from the outside without destroying the very condition of interiority—is what we call consciousness.

Inside a head there is no literal black hole. But perhaps there is something equally strange: an informational boundary that, upon closing, defines an interior. And that interior, while it lasts, says: **I**.

> **What we do know:** That there is a real distinction between systems with inaccessible internal states and completely transparent systems. That neuroscience can describe correlates but cannot access the private state. That access dualism does not require postulating any new substance.
>
> **What we do not know:** Whether every domain with high Phi is conscious, or if encapsulation is a necessary but not sufficient condition. Whether the public/private distinction is fundamental or emergent.
>
> **Remaining questions:** Can an encapsulated system be conscious without high Phi? Where exactly is the threshold of encapsulation? Does current AI have inaccessible private states, or only hidden states?
>
> **If you only take away one idea:** Subjective experience is not an anomaly in a public universe. It is what an encapsulated informational domain is from the inside. The abyss between matter and mind is not ontological—there are no two substances. It is architectural: there are two levels of access to the same structure.
