### Will as a function of the horizon

The intuitive answer is that will is the horizon's capacity to direct itself. Intention. The impulse that precedes action and guides it.

But the horizon is not a point. It is an integration field with its own topography: some inputs carry more weight, some directions have more inertia, some patterns are more consolidated because they have been active longer. What we call will is not a force acting upon that field from the outside. It is an emergent function of the field itself. The horizon that decides something is not above its own architecture: it is inside it, taking the direction that this architecture makes possible.

This is not determinism in the sense that the decision was written in advance. It is something more precise: the decision emerges from a structure that has history, chemistry, that bears the shape of everything that has been configuring it. Will is real—the horizon genuinely directs itself. But it is not sovereign in the absolute sense that moral language usually assumes.

The most exact image is that of a river. The river flows. The flow is real—the water truly goes from one place to another. But the river flows through a channel it has carved out itself, and changing direction is not impossible, though it requires time, erosion, sometimes a flood to open a new path. It is not enough for the river to want to.

Will, from the model of the horizon, is the integration system's capacity to generate sustained direction over time. Not an isolated act but a vector with inertia: it is hard to change, but it is also hard to stop once in motion. And that vector has resources. An exhausted horizon, with high cortisol and low serotonin, has access to a genuinely weaker will than the same horizon when rested. Not because it is less of a person. Because the integration field that generates will is degraded.

Will is not a moral gift. It is a physiological resource.

> **In physics this is called:** inertia of the integration field.  
> **In daily life it is like:** a river flowing through its channel: it can change direction, but it needs time and a strong current to erode the stone.

### The hijacking of the threshold

Addictive substances flood the dopaminergic system with a reward signal between ten and a hundred times more intense than any natural reward. The horizon does not receive greater pleasure: it receives a prediction of pleasure so overwhelming that it recalibrates the entire system.

The mechanism is exact: the dopaminergic system, designed to signal when something is worth repeating, receives a signal of a magnitude that no natural reward can generate. To compensate, the system reduces the number of available receptors—the same principle that moves someone to close the blinds when the light is too intense. When the artificial signal disappears, the system is left with fewer receptors than before. What used to generate a signal—a bond, novelty, achievement, ordinary pleasure—stops generating it with the same intensity. The threshold has shifted upward. To reach it now, more is needed.

This is not a metaphor. Neuroimaging studies show that the prefrontal cortex—the seat of intention, of delay, of evaluating consequences—loses functional connectivity with the reward system. The horizon still has will. What it has lost is access to the circuits that translate will into action regarding that specific impulse. The will is there. The bridge is cut.

It is the **Parkinson's of desire**: the horizon knows exactly where it wants to go—it knows it does not want to go where it is going. The system does not execute what the horizon commands.

That is why asking someone in active addiction to use their willpower has the same logical structure as asking a Parkinson's patient to walk faster. The will is there. The bridge between intention and action is what is compromised. And compromising that bridge is not a moral failing—it is a predictable consequence of what the molecule does to the system.

What addiction adds, beyond the shifting of the threshold, is a deformation of the entire horizon's hierarchy of relevance. The addicted horizon does not just pursue a substance: it has reorganized its integration system around that axis. Everything else—people, projects, bonds—becomes background noise. Not because the horizon has decided those things do not matter. Because the system that assigns importance has been recalibrated toward a single object that responds with an intensity nothing else can match. The horizon is left, in terms of the model, contracted: it integrates less, resonates less, generates less dense time in any direction other than that of the substance.

Recovery, from this angle, is not an act of sustained will that the horizon executes alone. It is a slow recalibration of the threshold—which takes months, sometimes years—and that recalibration occurs primarily through the bond. Other horizons sustaining the natural reward signal long enough for the receptors to regenerate and the threshold to lower. Oxytocin as an antidote. Not out of romanticism—but because it is the molecule that activates the reward system through the pathway of the bond, which is the only pathway that does not destroy the receptors it needs.

> **In physics this is called:** down-regulation of reward receptors.  
> **In daily life it is like:** closing the blinds when a blinding light enters: when the light goes out, you are left in the dark and cannot see anything.

### Dopamine and narrative

One of the most insidious things about addiction is that the system does not just recalibrate receptors: it recalibrates narrative. The human brain is a story machine: it converts experience into narrative, narrative into identity, identity into predictable behavior. Under addiction, that story machine is subordinated to the impulse.

Dopamine does not just generate desire: it generates **prediction**. The dopaminergic system constantly calculates the difference between what you expect and what you get. When the prediction exceeds the result, there is pleasure. When the result exceeds the prediction, there is euphoria. When the prediction is not met, there is frustration.

In addiction, the system learns to predict the substance with such certainty that any other reward—a meal, a hug, an achievement—generates an insufficient signal. The brain constructs a narrative where the substance is the only possible source of well-being. Not because it is true: because it is the only source that generates a sufficient signal to register it as positive.

> **In physics this is called:** dopaminergic confirmation bias: the system only registers evidence that confirms its internal model.  
> **In daily life it is like:** a translator who only translates the words they already know: everything else becomes noise.

### When love becomes the object

Chapter 13 described sexual contact as the only case where the superposition of two horizons is also physical, where the boundary between inside and outside loses its sharpness. That edge ambiguity produces the most potent reward signal the system can generate naturally—dopamine of anticipation, noradrenaline of novelty, oxytocin of contact, endorphins of physical pleasure. The complete cocktail. The nervous system is literally designed for that to matter more than almost anything else.

But the design assumes that this signal is accompanied by an other. By a real horizon on the other side of the contact—with its own time, its own unpredictability, its own capacity not to respond. A friction that, paradoxically, keeps the system calibrated: the signal fluctuates, it cannot be controlled unilaterally, and the system cannot optimize it without the participation of the other.

Sex addiction occurs when that condition is broken. When the horizon finds a way to access a version of that signal that it can control and repeat without depending on the availability, unpredictability, or friction of another real horizon. The pornographic system, for example, offers infinite novelty on demand—stimulation that activates the noradrenergic system continuously without the cost of negotiation or the risk of rejection. The dopaminergic anticipation system is triggered without ever resolving into a real bond, because there is no other horizon to bond with.

The result is exactly that of the general mechanism of addiction: the threshold shifts upward. Real intimacy—with its friction, its silences, its unpredictability—generates a signal that the system, recalibrated by artificial hyperstimulation, no longer finds sufficient. Not because the other has changed. Because the reward system can no longer process what the other offers at the intensity it now requires to register it.

What makes sex addiction a specific case is that the circuit it hijacks is exactly the circuit of love. The same receptors, the same dopaminergic anticipation system, the same zone of horizon ambiguity that Chapter 13 described as the seat of the deepest bond. Sex addiction is not an excess of love—it is the opposite: it is the system of love pursuing the signal of love without the love. Dopamine without oxytocin. Chemistry without the other.

The horizon that has arrived there not only loses access to the signal of the bond. It also loses the capacity to resonate with another horizon on that frequency—which is exactly the frequency it would need to recalibrate itself. The tie is cut from both ends at once.

> **In physics this is called:** absence of external feedback (open loop).  
> **In daily life it is like:** playing tennis against a wall that always returns the ball exactly where you want it: there is no real game, only repetition.

### Work addiction

There is a form of addiction that culture does not call addiction: work addiction. Workaholism is not excessive dedication: it is the hijacking of the reward system by an activity that generates a predictable and controllable signal.

Work offers something that human bonds cannot offer: measurable results, visible progress, immediate reward. Every email answered, every task completed, every goal achieved generates a small dopaminergic hit. The system learns to seek out those hits with the same logic with which it seeks the substance: because they generate a more reliable signal than the unpredictability of other horizons.

But work, unlike a chemical substance, does not destroy receptors. It destroys something harder to repair: the capacity to tolerate the ambiguity of the human bond. The work-addicted horizon loses the patience necessary for conversations that do not end, for silences that do not resolve, for emotions that cannot be completed in a sprint. The time of the bond—which Chapter 10 described as density emerging from resonance—becomes "wasted time" compared to the efficiency of work.

> **In physics this is called:** behavioral addiction: hijacking of the reward system by an activity that generates a predictable signal.  
> **In daily life it is like:** an athlete who only trains on a treadmill because the street has potholes: they gain speed, but lose balance.

### The horizon that was born sensitive

Up to this point, the chapter has described addiction as something that happens to the horizon—a recalibration produced by an external signal that exceeds the system's response capacity. But there are horizons that arrive at the encounter with that signal with a threshold already shifted from the beginning.

**ADHD** is the most documented case. The condition involves a dysfunction of the dopaminergic system—specifically, chronic hypoactivity of the reward system that makes the basal dopaminergic signal insufficient to sustain directed attention. The horizon with ADHD does not experience a deficit of intelligence or of motivation in the abstract: it experiences difficulty generating enough internal signal for a specific task—chosen by reason, not by impulse—to compete with the system's background noise. The horizon knows what it wants to do. The system does not generate the signal that would make doing it feel possible.

The consequences are precise. The horizon with ADHD actively seeks stimuli that elevate the dopaminergic signal: urgency, novelty, risk, intensity. Not out of character but out of architecture. When it finds a potent source of dopaminergic stimulation—a substance, a behavioral pattern, a screen designed to maximize the reward signal—the system responds with disproportionate intensity, precisely because it had been in a deficit for a long time. The threshold does not shift from above, as in classic addiction: it was low from the beginning, waiting.

Vulnerability is not a weakness of character. It is a property of the architecture. A horizon with ADHD that develops an addiction has not failed in a moral sense. It has followed the logic of a system seeking what it lacks—and it has found it in the wrong place, because the right places did not generate enough signal.

> **In physics this is called:** basal sensitization due to signal deficit.  
> **In daily life it is like:** an engine that needs more initial acceleration to start: it looks for steep ramps so as not to stall.

### The controlled demonstration

**Parkinson's** eliminates all the confounding variables that ADHD still leaves open.

There is no history of trauma. There is no adverse social context. There is no preexisting atypical dopaminergic architecture—at least not in most cases. There is a disease that progressively destroys the dopaminergic neurons of the substantia nigra and a treatment that attempts to replace what the disease destroys. And in that controlled treatment, between fourteen and forty-two percent of patients develop impulse control disorders—pathological gambling, hypersexuality, compulsive shopping, binge eating—that did not exist before the treatment.

The mechanism is what the model predicts. The dopaminergic system, deprived of signal by the disease for months or years, has reduced the threshold to a minimum—the postsynaptic receptors have become sensitized precisely because the presynaptic signal has been scarce. When the treatment arrives, the dopaminergic signal falls upon a system that was not calibrated to receive it at that intensity. The result is a relative overdose of the reward circuit even though the dose is, by the metrics of movement, completely therapeutic.

The cleanest proof is that the effect is proportional to the dose and the type of drug: dopamine agonists—which directly activate reward receptors—produce more impulse control disorders than levodopa alone, and the combination of both produces more than either separately. There is no safety threshold that protects certain horizons: the dose determines everything. With enough dopaminergic signal, any horizon arrives at the same place.

This is what makes Parkinson's the definitive argument against the moral narrative of addiction. There is no history of prior use here, there is no addictive personality, there is no social vulnerability. There is a molecule. And when the molecule changes, the horizon changes—regardless of who that horizon is.

But the most precise case—and the hardest to resolve—is the one that combines both conditions.

A horizon with ADHD diagnosed with Parkinson's carries two simultaneous deficits: the structural hypoactivity of ADHD, which has spent a lifetime generating sensitization of the reward receptors, and the neurodegeneration of Parkinson's, which destroys whatever presynaptic signal was left. The result is a reward system with the threshold at the lowest possible point—hypersensitive from its architecture, ultrasensitized by chronic deprivation.

When the dopaminergic treatment for Parkinson's arrives, there is no correct dose. The dose that treats the motor symptoms certainly exceeds the capacity of the reward system, which has spent decades waiting for exactly that signal. The dose that does not trigger the reward system does not treat the Parkinson's. The horizon is trapped between two failures of the same system: with too little dopamine it cannot move in time or in the body; with enough dopamine to move, the reward system is activated in a way it cannot control.

It is the case that definitively collapses any residue of a moral narrative. There is no place in this horizon from which will can operate in the way ordinary language assumes. The field is compromised in its architecture from the beginning, in its pathology in the present, and in the only available treatment simultaneously. The will has not failed. The will had nowhere to stand.

> **In physics this is called:** direct pharmacological regulation of the threshold.  
> **In daily life it is like:** a light switch that suddenly turns up the intensity: the bulbs burn out not because they are old, but because of the voltage.

### Recovery as reconstruction

If addiction is a recalibration of the threshold, recovery is reverse recalibration. It is not will: it is time, conditions, and the slow regeneration of receptors that the system stopped producing. Studies suggest that the complete recovery of the dopaminergic system can take between twelve and eighteen months—sometimes longer. During that time, the horizon lives in a world where nothing generates the signal it needs, except the substance to which it cannot return.

> **In physics this is called:** slow recalibration of the threshold due to prolonged absence of the suprathreshold stimulus.  
> **In daily life it is like:** rehabilitating a broken leg: the bone heals, but it takes months, and during those months you cannot run.

The role of the bond in recovery is crucial. Oxytocin—the molecule of the bond—activates the reward system through a pathway that does not destroy receptors. A recovering horizon surrounded by stable bonds receives a sufficient reward signal to sustain itself without recalibrating the threshold upward. That is why community-based recovery programs—twelve steps, group therapy, therapeutic residencies—have higher success rates than attempts at recovery in isolation. It is not the morality of the group: it is the chemistry of the bond.

> **In physics this is called:** alternative reward signal that does not produce down-regulation.  
> **In daily life it is like:** teaching a child to eat vegetables by rewarding them with hugs instead of candy: the reward is there, but it does not destroy their appetite.

> **Note to Chapter 14**
>
> **What we do know:** The hijacking of the prefrontal cortex by substances and the deformation of the hierarchy of relevance (dopaminergic recalibration) are real. Parkinson's treatment generates impulse control disorders. Workaholism is a recognized behavioral addiction. ADHD increases vulnerability to addiction.
>
> **What we do not know:** How exactly the serotonin and GABA networks interact with the dopaminergic system in each type of behavioral addiction. How long the complete recovery of the reward system takes.
>
> **Remaining questions:** Why do some horizons with ADHD develop severe addictions and others channel that search for intensity into creativity or controlled risk? Is workaholism less harmful than chemical addiction?
>
> **If you only take away one idea:** Addiction is not a moral failing of the will, but the physical hijacking and upward recalibration of the horizon's reward circuits. And recovery requires time, not strength of character.
