title: PARKINSON'S, TRAUMA, AND OTHER BROKEN GEOMETRIES
section: PART THREE: THE LIMITS OF THE HORIZON
---

Addiction is a hijacking. Someone—the substance, the compulsive behavior—takes control of the horizon and diverts it toward an attractor the subject did not choose. The addicted patient *knows* they are destroying themselves, but their reward system has been hijacked by a signal stronger than survival itself. It is as if an intruder burst into the control room and turned off every monitor except one.

Parkinson's is something else: it is not an invasion, it is a decoupling.

The horizon remains intact. The intention is clear. The inner world retains its coherence, its music, its desire to move. But the body no longer responds. The signal goes out. The score is written. The musicians do not listen.

---

## The intact horizon

In Parkinson's, the dopaminergic neurons of the substantia nigra degenerate progressively, one by one, in a silence the body does not report until the loss is massive. The motor result is well known: resting tremor, rigidity of the limbs, slowness of movement—the bradykinesia that turns the simplest gesture into an undertaking of minutes—and eventually the postural instability that transforms every step into a gamble. But something deeper than movement also fails, something classical neurology took a long time to name because it leaves no visible trace on imaging tests or in the reflexes of the hammer.

Subjective time is altered.

The patient generates intentions at normal speed. Their event horizon works. The *now* remains an active edge, a frontier where the past is integrated and the future is anticipated. But the body does not execute at that speed. It is not that the patient thinks more slowly—in fact, many Parkinson's patients describe an inner agitation, a mental rush that contrasts cruelly with the slowness of their gestures. It is an almost unbearable paradox: they do not feel they are moving slowly. They feel they are trying to move properly, with the same urgency and clarity as always, and that something external—the world, the body, gravity itself—is not responding.

> **In physics this is called:** decoupling between generator and executor.  
> **In daily life it is like:** screaming in a soundproof room. Your voice comes out with all its force. But it does not reach. There is no echo. There is no ear.

This is different from Alzheimer's. There, the horizon itself disintegrates, fragments, loses the narrative coherence that allows one to say "I was, I am, I will be." In Alzheimer's, the membrane of the horizon cracks and information escapes. In Parkinson's, the horizon remains the same—perfectly coherent, perfectly intact—only it can no longer speak to the body. It is like an orchestra conductor who continues to conduct with passion while the musicians go deaf one by one. The music is in his head. The harmony is real to him. But it does not come out. And the worst part is not the silence. It is that he does not know, sometimes, that the others are not hearing the same thing.

There is something of a Greek tragedy in this dissociation: consciousness condemned to fullness without execution, the Oedipus who sees destiny with absolute clarity but whose legs do not run fast enough to avoid it. The event horizon of the Parkinson's patient remains a perfect circle. Only it no longer coincides with the circle the body traces in space.

## The time that decouples

Subjective time is not the ticking of the clock. It is integration density: how much new information enters per unit of real time, how many edges of the horizon are updated, how many gradients are crossed in every lived second. When we are bored, time stretches because the integration density is low—few changes, little novelty, few boundaries crossed. When we are in danger or in love, time compresses because the density is extremely high—too much information per second, too many gradients, too many events to integrate.

Dopamine calibrates that internal clock. It is not the only neurotransmitter involved—glutamate, serotonin, and opioid peptides participate in the chorus—but dopamine is the main metronome. When it is scarce, as in Parkinson's, the calibration fails. The internal clock keeps beating time, but it no longer coincides with the clock of the world.

The horizon keeps generating "nows" at its own pace. Each "now" remains a unit of integration, a bite of coherent experience. But that pace no longer synchronizes with the outside world. The patient who takes three minutes to cross a room—three minutes that for their companion are an eternity of patience—does not feel them as eternal. They feel normal. Or more precisely: they do not feel them as slow at all, because their internal horizon has not generated fewer "nows" than it should. What they feel is a discordance, a lag, as if the world had been put on fast-forward while they remain at normal speed.

Treatment with levodopa—the dopamine precursor that crosses the blood-brain barrier and converts into the missing neurotransmitter—restores synchronization before movement. This is a surprising clinical fact: patients first describe that "the world returns to its speed," and only later that "the body moves again." Time is repaired before the body. Dopamine operates first as a temporal calibrator, then as a motor facilitator. It is as if you adjusted the tempo of the score before the musicians could play in unison.

> **In physics this is called:** phase resynchronization in a coupled system.  
> **In daily life it is like:** two clocks running at different speeds. The first thing to fix is not the mechanism of the hands, but the pendulum that sets the rhythm.

When levodopa works, the horizon returns to sharing the same time as others before returning to sharing the same space. Simultaneity is restored before motor function. This suggests something profound about the architecture of the horizon: shared time is more fundamental than shared gesture. We can move the body in temporal solitude—as in a dream—but we cannot truly be with others if our "nows" do not coincide.

There is also a lesson about medicine. For decades, Parkinson's was treated as a movement disorder. But patients knew—and many described it in interviews that neurology did not know how to hear—that the first thing they lost was not strength, but synchrony. That they felt "out of phase" before feeling slow. That they lived in a slightly different time, a parallel temporal dimension where intentions remained valid but no longer coincided with execution. The horizon model allows us to hear that: it allows us to take the patient's phenomenology seriously as data, not as an epiphenomenon.

## Trauma as frozen geometry

If Parkinson's is a progressive decoupling between intention and execution, trauma is something subtler and older in the history of the brain: a geometry that froze at a specific moment and that, ever since, distorts everything else.

When an event exceeds the system's capacity for integration—when the information is too intense, too fast, too contradictory to be processed in the available "now"—the horizon does not reject it. It cannot reject it, because rejection requires processing. Instead, it encapsulates. It saves the unprocessed information, puts it in a watertight compartment, seals it hermetically. It does not forget—forgetting would be integration, it would be quiet assimilation into the past—but rather it stores without distributing. The information remains there, intact, in its original form, like a fly in amber.

In software engineering terms, this encapsulation of trauma acts as an **isolation or sandboxing process**. To prevent a critical exception (emotional collapse) from propagating an error that crashes the entire operating system of the self, the system isolates the corrupted fragment inside a closed container. This container does not communicate with the main event flow (the ordinary *event loop*) nor does it respond to requests from the garbage collector (it cannot be *scrambled*). It remains running in the background as a ghost process or a blocked thread (*thread deadlock*). When an external stimulus hits the container's API (the *trigger*), it responds by throwing its frozen exception handler (the *flashback*), without processing the global timeline.

Trauma is information that could not be *scrambled*. In information theory, scrambling is the process by which local information is redistributed into global correlations, ceasing to be a hot spot to become uniform heat, experience, narrative memory. Trauma should have become global correlations, assimilated experience, a memory that tells a story. Instead, it remains trapped in a point, like a singularity that distorts everything else. It is not that the horizon forgets the event. It is that the event remains a *now*, not a *then*.

The symptoms of post-traumatic stress are manifestations of that gravitational distortion. The flashback is not memory: there is no narrative distance, there is no "memory of," there is no possibility of saying "that happened to me in another time." It is the sensation that the horizon is crossing the same point again, recreating the geometry of the traumatic moment as if time had not passed. The body reacts with the same adrenaline, the same tremor, the same dissociation. The prefrontal cortex, which should say "this has already passed," is offline or overridden. The horizon does not *remember* the trauma. It *re-lives* it, because for that part of the system, it never stopped happening.

> **In physics this is called:** local gauge trapped in a degenerate phase.  
> **In daily life it is like:** a song that always skips at the exact same second. It is not that you remember it. It is that the record is scratched right there. And every time the needle passes through the damaged groove, the sound is not a memory of the sound: it is the sound itself, intact, torn.

For that part of the horizon, time has not passed. The information remains trapped in the same instant, waiting—in some sense we do not fully understand—to finally be integrated. It is like an informational debt the system cannot pay, because the creditor no longer exists or because the currency has changed.

### The body that remembers what the mind cannot name

There is a dimension of trauma that the horizon model illuminates with particular clarity: the body as a repository of unintegrated information. When the horizon encapsulates a traumatic event, it does not store it only in the cortex—in fact, the cortex often lacks narrative access to it. It stores it in the body: in the tension of the trapezius muscles, in the hunched posture, in the held breath, in the vagal tone that does not recover. The body becomes the archive of what could not be processed.

This explains something somatic therapists have known for decades: that trauma is not cured by talking alone, because trauma is not in language. It is in the geometry of the body, in the topology of muscular tension, in the rhythm of the heart that cannot slow down. To "unfreeze" trauma, one must intervene in the body: in breathing, in movement, in posture. Not because the body is the *key* to trauma, but because the body is where the horizon deposited the information it could not integrate.

> **In physics this is called:** holographic information stored on the boundary.  
> **In daily life it is like:** a safe that is not opened by a numerical combination, but by a physical key. The code is in another register.

### Complex trauma and blurred edges

Not all trauma is an isolated event. Complex trauma—the kind that stems from years of abuse, neglect, a hostile environment—does not have a clear "scratched record." It has thousands of scratches, thousands of micro-traumas that accumulate until they deform the entire surface. In this case, the frozen geometry is not a singular point, but an entire region of the horizon where integration fails chronically.

The result is a horizon that functions, but functions with a permanent region of shadow. The subject can live, work, love, but there is always a part of the "now" gripped by unprocessed information, a phantom gravity pulling everything downward. This is chronic depression, structural dissociation, the feeling that one is never completely *present* because a part of the horizon remains trapped in another time.

## Neurodiversity as a different topology

The horizon model allows us to speak of autism, ADHD, schizophrenia, and other conditions not as deficits that should be corrected, but as different topologies: distinct ways of organizing the same information, valid informational architectures that do not coincide with the statistical standard.

In physics, a topology is the way the points of a space are connected. Two spaces can have the same metric—the same local distances—but different topologies: one might be a sphere, another a torus, another a plane with holes. In each case, the local rules are the same, but the global properties—which paths are possible, which regions are connected, what information can circulate—are radically different.

The neurodivergent brain is not a "broken" brain. It is a brain with a different topology.

### Autism: local hyperdensity

In autism, the distribution of information is different from childhood. Functional connectivity studies show denser local connections—sensory hyperconnectivity—and less efficient long-range connections. The horizon is extraordinarily rich in local detail, at the cost of a more fragmented global integration.

From the outside, it looks like social difficulty, rigidity, insistence on sameness, meltdowns in the face of unexpected changes. From the inside, it is possible—and increasingly, autistic voices describe it this way—that the horizon is experiencing information densities that neurotypical horizons do not reach. That "sensory overload" is not a filtering failure, but a data input too faithful, too detailed, too rich to be integrated at the pace the world demands.

> **In physics this is called:** a system with strong local couplings and weak global couplings.  
> **In daily life it is like:** a microscope in a world of magnifying glasses. The microscope is not broken. It is designed to see what magnifying glasses cannot see. But in a world designed for magnifying glasses, the microscope seems slow, clumsy, excessive.

The problem with autism is not in the topology itself. It is in the friction between that topology and an environment that assumes another. A world with fewer simultaneous stimuli, with predictable routines, with time to process, with respect for internal rhythms, would not "cure" autism—there is nothing to cure—but it would drastically reduce the suffering that accompanies the topology when it must function in an environment hostile to it.

### ADHD: the shifted threshold

ADHD is, in terms of the model, structural dopaminergic hypoactivity in the reward and sustained attention circuits. The horizon does not generate enough internal reward signal to sustain attention on tasks that are not intrinsically stimulating. It is not a lack of willpower: it is a lack of fuel for willpower. Willpower, in this model, is not an act of moral strength. It is an informational function that requires dopamine to sustain itself.

The ADHD horizon seeks stimuli that elevate the signal: urgency, novelty, risk, conflict, intense interest. Not because it is impulsive in the moral sense of the word. Because its reward system needs intensities that the ordinary world—repetitive tasks, bureaucracy, traditional schooling, office work—does not provide.

> **In physics this is called:** a system with a shifted activation threshold that requires supraliminal stimuli.  
> **In daily life it is like:** a car that only starts on a slope. On flat ground, it seems broken. It is not. It needs an incline. And when it finds the right slope, it accelerates with a power that flat-ground cars cannot reach.

There is a kind of inadvertent heroism in this. Adults with ADHD who have built functional lives have not done so by "overcoming" their condition. They have done so by finding slopes: high-urgency professions, intense creativity, high-novelty environments, passionate relationships. They have built a world that provides the incline their engine needs. And in that world, their topology is not a deficit. It is an advantage.

### Schizophrenia: the horizon that generates without input

Schizophrenia is perhaps the most difficult condition to map onto the horizon model, because here it is not just about a different topology. It is about a horizon that generates integration where there is no information to integrate: voices without a speaker, intentions without an agent, connections between events that are not correlated in the shared world.

It is as if the brain's prediction system—the apparatus that generates models of the world to anticipate what is to come—were activated without sensory input, producing models of other horizons that are not there. The patient hears voices not because their ears malfunction, but because their predictive system generates predictions of speech without there being speech. They see connections not because they are "paranoid," but because their integrating apparatus assigns correlations where the rest of us do not see them.

> **In physics this is called:** spontaneous mode generation in a system near instability.  
> **In daily life it is like:** a radar that detects signals where there are no planes. The radar is not broken. But the sky it shows is not the sky we share.

Here the suffering is deep and real. It is not just about friction with the environment. It is about living in a world where predictions do not coincide with reality, where the internal model is chronically out of phase with the external model. Antipsychotic treatment—which blocks D2 dopamine receptors—works, in terms of the model, by lowering the gain of the predictive generator, reducing the system's sensitivity to internal signals, forcing a greater correspondence between what is generated and what is perceived.

But even here, the model suggests something traditional psychiatry has ignored: that schizophrenia is not an "absence of reality" but an "excess of internal generation." That the patient is not "disconnected" from reality, but "overconnected" to their own predictive apparatus. That the difference is not qualitative—they see things that do not exist, we do not—but quantitative: we all generate internal predictions, we all have automatic thoughts, we all hear our own internal voice. In schizophrenia, the volume of those internal generations exceeds the threshold where they can be distinguished from external perceptions.

## Resonance between different horizons

A consequence of the model that is rarely discussed is what happens when two horizons with different topologies try to communicate. Empathy, in this framework, is not just "putting oneself in the other's shoes." It is synchronizing two informational topologies that may be incompatible in their rules of integration.

A neurotypical horizon and an autistic horizon do not just perceive different things. They perceive *differently*. One integrates top-down—prioritizing context, intention, the social gesture. The other integrates bottom-up—prioritizing detail, sensation, the exact sequence. When they meet, it is not that one "does not understand" the other. It is that their horizons are generating "nows" with different internal structures.

> **In physics this is called:** weak coupling between oscillators with different fundamental frequencies.  
> **In daily life it is like:** two musicians playing the same song but one in 3/4 time and the other in 4/4 time. Both have rhythm. Both have melody. But they cannot play together unless one of them adapts their time signature.

Adaptation, historically, has always fallen on the divergent horizon. It is the autistic person who must learn "social skills." It is the ADHD person who must "concentrate." It is the schizophrenic person who must take medication to "adjust." Rarely is the neurotypical environment asked to adapt its frequency, to lower its volume, to respect the integration rhythms of the other.

The horizon model suggests that this asymmetry is not necessary. That resonance is possible if both horizons are willing to modulate their frequencies. That communication between different topologies does not require one to disappear, but rather that both find a shared rhythm—even if it is temporary, even if it is partial.

## What is not broken

Calling these conditions "disorders" assumes a standard horizon from which they deviate, a "correct" architecture of which all others are deformations. But if the horizon is an emergent informational architecture, there is no reason to assume that only one is valid.

There are horizons that process differently, that integrate differently, that resonate at different frequencies. There are horizons that see the world through a microscope and horizons that see it in wide-angle. There are horizons that need a slope to start and horizons that work better on flat ground. There are horizons that generate music without it sounding, and horizons that hear music where others only hear noise.

This does not deny the suffering. Suffering is real when the topology cannot function in the world that surrounds it. But suffering is not the same as difference. A horizon with autism in a world designed for neurotypicals suffers. That does not mean its topology is incorrect. It means the world has no place for it. That the stairs have no ramps, that the lights flicker too fast, that the instructions presuppose a speed of integration that is not its own.

The model cannot say what world we should build. It is not a political manual or a treatise on urban planning. But it can say something that medical language often hides: the difference lies not in the quantity of consciousness but in its shape. A horizon is not more or less real for being different. It is not more or less worthy. It is not more or less human.

And the suffering of many of these conditions stems not from the topology itself—the topology is what it is, neither better nor worse—but from the friction between that topology and an environment that assumes another. Friction generates heat. Heat generates pain. Pain generates pathologization. And pathologization generates a prison of labels from which it is very difficult to escape.

> **In physics this is called:** interaction between systems with broken symmetries.  
> **In daily life it is like:** a dress made for a single size. It is not that the other sizes are "disorders." It is that the dress assumes a body that is not the only possible one.

> **What we do know:** Parkinson's decouples the horizon from the body; it does not destroy the horizon. Dopamine calibrates time before movement. Trauma freezes information in points of the horizon that cannot be reintegrated. Neurodivergent conditions have different informational topologies, not absent horizons.
>
> **What we do not know:** Whether levodopa restores temporal synchronization through the same mechanism as motor function, or through independent pathways. Whether trauma can be "unfrozen" without reactivating the singularity. What environments would make currently pathologized topologies functional.

> **If you only take away one idea:** Not everything that seems broken is broken. Some geometries just do not fit in the world that measures them.
