---
title: FIVE MAPS OF THE SAME HORIZON
subtitle: (God, the soul, and nothing, compared)
section: PART FOUR: THE LIMIT OF THE EXPERIMENT
---

The previous chapter left a question unclosed: what happens to information when a horizon evaporates, and whether that question—"am I that information?"—even makes sense phrased that way. Three names already appeared there in passing—Buddhism, Vedanta, Taoism—mentioned the way one mentions a neighbor without knocking on their door. This chapter knocks on that door, and on two others that were missing: Christianity's and Islam's, and the door of whoever doesn't believe there's any door to knock on.

Not to arbitrate who is right. The experiment already said, in chapter 43, that ethics—and with it, a good deal of theology—exceeds its vocabulary. But the experiment can do something more modest and perhaps more useful: place five human answers, completely different from one another, side by side, and ask what they share and exactly where they part ways. Not to melt them into one. To see their disagreement with precision.

> **In physics this is called:** five distinct models fitted to the same incomplete data, each with different background assumptions.
> **In daily life it is like:** five witnesses to the same accident, describing what they saw from angles that don't quite overlap: none of them is lying, but none of them saw the whole scene.

---

### Four questions, not one

Every religious tradition—and also the deliberate absence of one—answers, whether it knows it or not, the same four questions this book has been asking in its own vocabulary:

1. **What is the reservoir?** Is there something with will and name on the other side of the horizon, or only field, only physics, only faceless possibility?
2. **What happens when the horizon evaporates?** Does the "self" persist as a recognizable boundary—a horizon that doesn't dissolve, only relocates—or does it return to the reservoir without keeping its shape?
3. **Where does the code come from?** When chapter 43 pointed to Hume's is-ought gap—that no "is" by itself produces an "ought"—each tradition offers a different way of closing, or refusing to close, that gap.
4. **What is living, in the meantime?** A test, a loan, an illusion to be crossed, or simply the only thing there is?

Christianity, Islam, Buddhism, Hinduism, and atheism are not five variations on a single theme. They are five distinct architectures built to answer the same four questions, and the point of this chapter is to show the exact shape of each one, not to file them down until they look compatible.

---

### Christianity: the horizon that never collapsed

For Christianity, God is not the reservoir in the sense this book has used the word—an undifferentiated field, potential without form. He is, rather, a horizon that existed before the vacuum described in chapters 4 and 5 ever did: personal, willed, named. A horizon of infinite Φ that did not emerge from anything, because nothing precedes it.

The structural difference from Vedanta and Taoism is radical. Nirguna Brahman and Hun Dun are plenitude *without features*: the reservoir of chapter 5 has no opinion about what you do. The Christian God does. Creation is not a Bose-Einstein condensation—an impersonal phase transition—but an act of will: *fiat*, let it be. And the relationship between the creature and that origin-horizon does not dissolve over time, as it does in the Brahman-Atman model of chapter 5: it is sustained, dialogically, to the end.

That completely changes the answer to the second question. Vedanta says the wave never stopped being ocean; Christianity says the soul is a drop that God intends to keep as a drop, not dissolve. The resurrection of the flesh—not the immortality of a floating soul, but the promise of a restored body—is, in this book's vocabulary, the claim that the individual horizon does *not* evaporate without remainder. Of the three options chapter 43 left open—remnant, imprint, nothing—Christianity bets, with its full doctrinal weight, on the remnant: something that keeps shape, memory, recognizable identity, and that one day is "read" again, at the Judgment, with all its information intact.

> **In physics this is called:** strong conservation of the horizon's identity through evaporation, rather than irreversible scrambling.
> **In daily life it is like:** keeping a letter in a safe instead of letting the wind carry off the ashes: the promise isn't that the letter turns into something else, but that it remains, word for word, the same letter.

The third question—where the code comes from—has a direct answer here: from the commandment. But Christianity complicates it with a second layer that sets it apart from Islam: law is not enough, and grace—unearned love—weighs more than compliance. The "ought" is born not only from an external order, as in the simplest reading of divine command theory, but from a relationship: one acts well because one has been loved first, not to earn that love. Hume's gap is closed by appealing not to a fact, but to a bond—the same kind of entangled bond chapter 8 described between people, transposed onto a vertical scale.

Living, in this framework, is a pilgrimage: a time of trial and choice with consequences that outlast the life itself, not a cycle that repeats.

---

### Islam: transcendence without a crack

Islam shares with Christianity the basic structure—a personal origin-horizon, prior to and outside the physical reservoir, that sustains the creature's identity beyond evaporation—but takes it to an extreme of purity that Christianity does not maintain: *tawhid*, absolute oneness. There is no incarnation, no God who becomes a finite horizon to dwell inside the system he created. The distance between Creator and creation is never closed, not even partially: it is administered, through revelation—the Qur'an as direct word, unmediated by a second divine nature crossing the border.

That purity has a clear consequence for the third question. If there is no mediation through incarnation, the ethical code does not arrive wrapped in a personal relationship that softens it: it arrives as law—*sharia*—directly derived from revealed divine will, with a degree of practical specification (what to eat, how to pray, how to settle disputes) that Christianity largely delegates to tradition and natural reason. Hume's is-ought gap closes here without ambiguity: the "is" that produces the "ought" is God's own word, taken as revealed fact, not as an argument still to be built.

On the second question, Islam agrees with Christianity in betting on the remnant, not dissolution: there is bodily resurrection, a Day of Judgment (*Yawm al-Qiyamah*), a garden (*Jannah*) and a fire (*Jahannam*) that are destinations, not metaphors for reabsorption into a nameless field. The formula a Muslim recites facing death—*inna lillahi wa inna ilayhi raji'un*, "from God we come and to Him we return"—uses the same word, *return*, that chapter 7 of this book uses for evaporation. But the destination of that return is not an impersonal reservoir: it is an account, before someone who weighs it.

> **In physics this is called:** an absolute access boundary between the reservoir and the origin-horizon—no condensation, no incarnation crosses that boundary in any direction.
> **In daily life it is like:** a light source so intense it cannot be looked at directly even for an instant: it is known by what it illuminates, never by contact.

Life, here, is *dunya*: a temporary loan, a test (*ibtila*) whose full meaning is only revealed at the end. No cycle, no repetition: one life, one final weighing.

---

### Buddhism and Hinduism: dissolution revisited

These two traditions have already been discussed in detail—Vedanta in chapter 5, the Buddhist cycle of anattā and karma in chapter 7—so here it is enough to name the contrast that sets them apart from the Abrahamic pair.

Neither has, in its classical form, a personal creator with whom the individual horizon maintains a permanent, dialogical relationship. Hinduism has Brahman—which can dress itself in named gods, Shiva, Vishnu, Devi, in its *saguna* aspect—but the ultimate destination, *moksha*, is not reunion with a God who remains distinct from you: it is discovering there was never a distinction to be reconciled. Buddhism, more radical still, doesn't even need a Brahman: *nirvana* is not union with anything, it is the cessation of the conditions that generated the illusion that there was something separate to unite.

That's why, against the remnant that Christianity and Islam bet on, Buddhism and Hinduism bet—each in its own already-described way—on an imprint without substance: karma as texture in the field, not as a file with your name on it waiting to be read.

And on the third question, neither closes Hume's gap with an external commandment. Hindu dharma is not an order arriving from outside: it is a description of how the world is woven, and acting well means acting in consonance with that weave, not obeying a legislator. Buddhism goes further still: the ethics of non-harm (*ahimsa*) and the four noble truths are derived from an empirical observation about suffering and its cause, not from revelation. The "ought" is born from an "is"—suffering exists, it has a cause, the cause can cease—that Hume would have recognized as a legitimate, if not definitive, attempt to build the bridge he himself declared impossible.

---

### Atheism: the map that refuses to draw a beyond

Atheism—and its more cautious cousins, agnosticism and secular naturalism—is not the absence of an answer to the four questions. It is a different answer, as worked out as the other four, and in a sense it is the one this very experiment has been giving by default for nearly nine hundred pages: the reservoir is exactly what physics describes, no more and no less. There is no origin-horizon with a will. There is no remnant that keeps your name. What persists after evaporation is scrambled correlation, with no reader, no outstanding account.

This is not, as it is sometimes caricatured, an absence of moral code. It is the bet that the code needs no external author: it emerges from social biology, from the evolution of empathy and reciprocity, from centuries of cultural negotiation over what makes shared life livable. Hume's is-ought gap, here, is not closed: it is accepted as structural, and lived with anyway—which is exactly what this book itself did explicitly in chapter 43, refusing to say what you "should" do with the information it offers.

> **In physics this is called:** the null model—the hypothesis that adds no unobserved entities to explain the data.
> **In daily life it is like:** reading the map from chapter 43 and accepting that it ends where it ends, without drawing territory out of what one would like to find beyond the edge.

Living, in this framework, is not a test, a loan, or an illusion to be crossed: it is the phenomenon in full, not an antechamber to something else. Which doesn't make it less dense—as the previous chapter on practice showed, the absence of a transcendent promise does not stop meditation, bonding, or creativity from reorganizing the horizon with full force. It only changes where meaning is expected to be found: not after the horizon, but inside it, for as long as it lasts.

### What actually separates these five answers

It is not, at bottom, whether something persists after evaporation. In a weak, technical sense, all five traditions agree that *something* persists: scrambled information, karma, an imprint in the field, a preserved soul, or simply physics that isn't destroyed—the same conservation principle, however much it troubled Hawking in 1976, reappears disguised as eschatology in all five. What truly separates them are two other questions, orthogonal to each other.

The first: does the "self" that persists keep a **recognizable boundary**, or does it dissolve into something larger? Christianity and Islam say it keeps a boundary—that's why they speak of judgment, of an account presented to *you*, not to a diffuse field. Buddhism, Hinduism, and physical naturalism say it doesn't, each with its own nuance: Hinduism because there was never a real boundary to lose; Buddhism because the boundary was a functional illusion from the start; naturalism because the boundary is simply a temporary thermodynamic pattern, with no grace to preserve it.

The second: does the ethical code arrive **from outside** the system, with authority that needs no justification before human reason, or is it **built from within**, always subject to revision? Christianity and Islam answer: from outside. Hinduism answers: from the very weave of what is real, which isn't exactly "outside" but also isn't negotiable. Buddhism and atheism answer: from within—by observation of suffering, or by evolution and social agreement.

Crossing these two axes—boundary preserved or dissolved, code external or constructed—doesn't produce a clean two-by-two table, because Hinduism occupies a middle position on the second axis that no single box captures well. But the exercise makes one thing clear: the difference between these five traditions is not, as it's often simplified, "believing in something" versus "believing in nothing." It is a difference of architecture, on exactly the points this book has had to leave unresolved: what the reservoir is, whether the horizon survives it with shape intact, and where the "ought" comes from.

> **In physics this is called:** five different boundary conditions applied to the same unsolved equation.
> **In daily life it is like:** five families inheriting the same empty house and furnishing it in irreconcilable ways: some keep the key to a room they say will one day be opened; others throw the key away and say there was never a door.

---

### What this chapter does not do

It does not say which of the five is right. It cannot: chapter 43 already established that the horizon hypothesis is a map, not a territory, and a map does not adjudicate between destinations it has never visited. Nor does it reduce the five traditions to "the same thing with different names"—that reduction, tempting because it's comfortable, erases precisely the differences that make them incompatible with one another on the points that actually matter: whether someone is on the other side, whether that someone preserves you or dissolves you, whether the code you follow was dictated or built.

What it can say, with the same honesty as the previous chapter, is this: nearly eight billion horizons are, right now, entangled with one of these five architectures—or with one of the many this chapter had no room to name—and that entanglement, as chapter 27 described, is neither weakness nor naivety. It is the same technology any horizon uses to hold coherence in the face of a question it cannot answer with direct evidence: what lies on the other side of the edge, and whether anyone, on that other side, is waiting there with your name still written on it.

> **Note to Chapter 44**
>
> **What we do know:** The five traditions described here have internal formulations far more diverse than any table can hold—there are universalist Christianities that doubt eternal hell, devotional Buddhist schools very close to theism, atheist currents with dense ritual and community. The table compresses; it does not replace any of the traditions cited.
>
> **What we don't know:** Whether any of the five correctly describes what happens after the horizon evaporates. Physics, as chapter 43 said, has no tools to arbitrate this.
>
> **Remaining questions:** Can the "external code / constructed code" axis be maintained without collapsing into the false dichotomy of "with God there is morality, without God there isn't"? What happens to a tradition when it loses its coupling technology—ritual—but keeps its doctrinal map? Can someone be entangled, without contradiction, with more than one of these five maps at once?
>
> **If you only take away one idea:** The five answers are not competing for the same prize. They are competing to answer the same four questions that physics, alone, leaves open: what the reservoir is, whether the horizon survives it with shape intact, where the code comes from, and what living means in the meantime. Knowing exactly where their answers differ is more useful than pretending they all say the same thing.
>
