---

## [ILUSTRACIÓN TP: "The limits of the horizon"]
*A circular horizon blurring at its edges, like a wet watercolor. Inside the circle, sharp landscapes: a city, a family, a face. At the edge, shapes dissolve into drops falling toward the void. Indigo and gold tones, an atmosphere of farewell.*

---

There is a way of losing oneself that is worse than death. Not because it hurts more; sometimes it doesn't hurt at all. It is worse because the horizon vanishes while it is still there. Death, at least, has the decency to be an event. A before and an after. A point in time where someone ceases to be, and those of us left behind can mourn, remember, find closure. Alzheimer's is a process. And what it destroys is not matter, but organization. It is not the building that collapses; it is the blueprints that are erased, brick by brick, room by room, while the occupants remain seated in the living room, unaware that there is no longer a kitchen, or a bedroom, or an exit door.

I have seen entire families buckle under the weight of this progressive forgetting. It is not the weight of a sudden loss, which at least generates its own momentum of grief. It is the weight of a loss that repeats itself every morning. The husband who asks where his wife is while she stands right in front of him. The mother who does not recognize the son caring for her. Every day a small death, every day a slightly narrower horizon, until the world is reduced to a room, to a window, to a ray of light that enters and means nothing because there is no longer a narrative to give it context.

> **In physics this is called:** loss of coherence in an open system, where information does not disappear from the universe but ceases to be accessible to the subsystem that generated it.  
> **In daily life it is like:** trying to read a book while the pages crumble between your fingers, and every time you turn back to the previous page, the words are different, until there is no book, only dust that knows it was once something greater.

Alzheimer's forces us to ask what exactly consciousness is if it can crumble like this, gradually, without a total collapse. If memory is the armor of the self, what remains when the armor rusts? If identity is a building of stories, who inhabits the ruins?

## The blurring edge

In the early stages, the forgetting seems ordinary. Where I left the keys. The name of that actor. What I came into this room to get. We all have these lapses, and most of the time the name comes back after an hour, or the keys turn up in the pocket of a coat we don't remember wearing. In youth, these memory slips seem like anecdotes. In middle age, they cause a pang of anxiety that we hide with a forced laugh. In the first signs of Alzheimer's, they are premonitions the patient feels but does not name, like someone who hears the foundations of their house creaking and prefers to think it is the wind.

The difference is that in normal aging, the horizon recovers them: the information was there, only the access was slow. The synapse worked, but the road was congested. In Alzheimer's, the information ceases to be there. Not because the bookcase has been moved, but because the books disintegrate. The beta-amyloid molecule accumulates in plaques between neurons like an invisible mold. The tau protein unravels and tangles into neurofibrillary tangles inside them, as if the internal structure of each neuron decided to abandon its shape. Neurons do not die all at once. They lose their axons. They lose their dendrites. They lose the ability to talk to their neighbors. The tissue ceases to be tissue and becomes silence.

Normal forgetting is a library where the catalog fails: the book still exists, it is just hard to find. Sometimes you need the librarian on duty, or a clue, or the smell of the paper that evokes the title. In Alzheimer's, the library itself disintegrates. The shelf retains its hollow shape for a while, but the book is no longer there. And the cruelest part: the librarian, who is the patient, keeps wandering the aisles, looking for something they no longer know what it is, feeling that something essential is missing but unable to name it.

> **In physics this is called:** degradation of the architecture of correlations, where degrees of freedom are not destroyed individually but cease to be mutually informed.  
> **In daily life it is like:** a library where the books crumble into dust while the shelves remain standing, and the librarian keeps opening the doors every morning, feeling that something important used to be here, no longer knowing what.

From the model of the horizon: normal aging is a slowing of access. Alzheimer's is a degradation of the architecture itself. Neurons do not die all at once; the connections die. The horizon does not switch off: it tears. It loses area. The boundary that separated the inside from the outside becomes porous, irregular, full of holes. Through those holes leaks what was once contained: a daughter's name, the address of the house where they lived for forty years, the meaning of the word "hunger." The horizon is no longer a sphere. It is an oil slick on water, fragmenting into ever-smaller drops, each one isolated, each one a world without context.

In terms of systems design, Alzheimer's is a **catastrophic failure of encapsulation**. In programming, an encapsulated object guarantees that its internal state is protected against external mutations and maintains its structural invariants. If the object's boundary degrades and loses its integrity, the private state begins to corrupt and leak. System calls attempt to reference broken pointers or unallocated memory areas. The public interface (the API that allowed interacting with the patient) returns errors or becomes opaque, while the internal "librarian" tries to walk down aisles whose memory indices have been erased from the allocation table.

There is something particularly heartbreaking about the intermittent lucidity of the early stages. The patient knows they are forgetting. They know something is slipping away. It is like trying to hold water between your palms: you feel there is something there, but every time you close your fingers, there is less. That awareness of forgetting—metamemory in neuropsychological terms—is the last guardian of the horizon, and it is the first to extinguish. When it goes, the patient ceases to know that they do not know. The horror yields to a kind of blind peace, but before it yields, it leaves a deep mark on those watching from the outside. To see someone lose themselves is to witness a dissolution that should not be visible.

## The returning reservoir

In chapter 6 I described birth as condensation: the reservoir closing in on itself to form a horizon. Alzheimer's is the reverse movement, but not a symmetrical one. It is not an elegant opening toward the reservoir, an orderly undoing of what was done at birth. It is a dissolution at the edges, an uneven unraveling, an erosion that advances at different rates depending on the region of the brain.

Episodic memory—the narrative of the self through time—is the first to go. Autobiographical memories fade in the reverse order of their acquisition: first the most recent, then the intermediate, finally the oldest. It is as if the horizon unrolled backward, undoing the history of the self in reverse. The patient stops remembering what they had for breakfast, then what year it is, then that their brother died a decade ago. Sometimes they will relive conversations from their youth with surprising clarity while failing to recognize the person standing in front of them. The past is not erased all at once; it is erased from the present backward, like a receding tide leaving only the oldest foundations exposed.

Then semantic memory: what an apple is, what "husband" means, what a church is, what a car does. The world loses its labels. Objects are still there, they are still seen, but they are no longer *named things*. They are pure presence without category. For a patient in intermediate stages, an apple might still be recognized as food, as something round and red, but the word "apple" no longer floats beside it. Language peels away from the world like a seam coming undone. And without language, the world becomes immense and unintelligible, a chaos of nameless shapes where everything is new and therefore nothing is familiar.

Then language itself. Not just the words for things, but grammar, syntax, the ability to assemble subjects and predicates. First specific nouns disappear, then complex verbs, then subordinate clauses. In the end, what remains are monosyllables, gestures, vocalizations. The horizon contracts not toward the center, but from the edges, like ice melting at its extremities. It is not a sphere shrinking while maintaining its shape; it is a territory losing arbitrary borders, leaving isolated islands of ice amidst waters that no longer remember the winter.

What remains in the final stages is not "nothing." That is the most common misconception, the one that makes some speak of the patient as if they were already dead. What remains is a minimal horizon: perception without narrative, present without past or future, sensation without a name. The patient can feel cold, hunger, restlessness, calm. They can look at a face and feel something they cannot label as "love" but that functions as love in their body: the heart rate that moderates, the breathing that calms, the pupils that dilate in non-declarative recognition. It is a horizon so reduced that to us, from the outside, it seems empty. But from the inside, from the perspective of the system itself, it is still a here, a now, a something.

> **In physics this is called:** contraction of the horizon due to loss of integrated degrees of freedom, where the system preserves its existence as a horizon even though its effective area is reduced asymptotically.  
> **In daily life it is like:** ice melting at the edges: the center is still ice, but every day it is smaller and more alone, until only a flake remains that still preserves the crystalline memory of winter.

Some patients in advanced stages still respond to the music they listened to in their youth, to a child's voice, to the touch of a familiar hand. Not because they "remember" in the ordinary sense; the explicit memory system no longer works. There is no mental image of the song, no association with a specific moment of youth. Those frequencies resonate in the residual horizon, in the correlations that have not yet broken. It is like plucking a string on a guitar with only two strings left: you cannot play the full melody, but the remaining strings vibrate with the implicit harmony of the whole song. The body remembers what the mind has forgotten. The limbic system, older and deeper, resists longer than the cerebral cortex. The amygdala keeps labeling emotions. The thalamus keeps filtering sensations. The brainstem keeps maintaining the rhythm of life.

This is what makes Alzheimer's different from death. In death, the horizon evaporates as a whole. It is a continuous process, yes, but the horizon maintains its internal coherence until the end: the information is scrambled as a unit. In Alzheimer's, parts persist while others are no longer there. The patient may be "closer to the reservoir" at some moments than at others. The boundary fluctuates. Sometimes, for seconds or minutes, a familiar face dissolves the fog and the horizon expands momentarily, recovering connections lost months ago. Then it closes again. It is a door that opens and closes at random, briefly showing the garden that once was, only to lock us back in the empty room.

## The time that no longer flows

When episodic memory dissolves, time ceases to be a river and becomes a puddle. There is no yesterday and no tomorrow. There is only this instant, and this instant, and this instant, each without cause or consequence, each without history. The patient does not live in the present like a mystic who has achieved timelessness through discipline; they live in the present because time has broken in half for them.

> **In physics this is called:** collapse of the thermodynamic arrow of time in a subsystem that loses the ability to register entropy changes.  
> **In daily life it is like:** waking up every morning in an unfamiliar room without remembering that you woke up there yesterday, and feeling that every day is the first and the only one, an eternity of presents that do not accumulate.

To us, from the outside, the patient's time seems like a tragedy of repetition. They ask the same thing every five minutes. They want to see someone who died years ago. They do not understand why they are in this house. But from the inside, every question is new, every anxiety is the first, every confusion is original. The patient does not suffer the repetition because they do not register the repetition. We, the observers, suffer, seeing the loop. They live a spiral they do not know is closed.

There is something disturbingly similar to certain advanced contemplative experiences in this loss of narrative time. Mystical texts speak of the "eternal now" as liberation. But the difference is radical: the mystic chooses to let go of the narrative, and does so from an intact horizon. The Alzheimer's patient loses it without choosing, from a crumbling horizon. The timelessness of the enlightened is an ocean; the timelessness of Alzheimer's is a shipwreck. Both are in shoreless waters, but one swam there with purpose, while the other fell off the boat.

This raises an uncomfortable question about the nature of the conscious "now." Is the present a window that requires a frame of past and future to exist as an experience? Or is the pure present—sensation without narrative—a legitimate, albeit impoverished, form of consciousness? The model of the horizon suggests that the pure present is still a horizon, only of minimal area. It is the consciousness of an amoeba, of a newborn, of an animal without internal language. It is not an absence of consciousness; it is consciousness in its most reduced form, the hard core that resists when everything else is gone.

## Language as the last dam

Language is the last bastion of the human horizon, and its fall is the most silent of all catastrophes. When a patient loses the ability to name, they lose more than labels: they lose the possibility of sharing their world. The pain that cannot say "pain" is still pain, but it is a pain without a bridge to another horizon. The joy that cannot say "I am glad to see you" is still joy, but it is a joy that bounces against the walls of a self that no longer has windows.

> **In physics this is called:** loss of correlation channels between horizons, where two systems that were mutually informed cease to share accessible degrees of freedom.  
> **In daily life it is like:** two lighthouses facing each other in a foggy bay: each one keeps burning, but the beam no longer reaches the other, and each lighthouse believes it is alone on the coast.

The aphasias of Alzheimer's are particularly cruel because they often preserve the musicality of speech while destroying its meaning. The patient may continue speaking with intonation, rhythm, dramatic pauses, but the words they utter are semantic substitutions—"thingy" for "spoon," "praying place" for "church"—or outright gibberish that sounds almost like a language. It is like listening to a symphony where all the notes are in tune but in random order: the musical form persists, but the information has fled. The horizon keeps emitting signals, but the signals no longer point to anything.

When language is reduced to monosyllables and gestures, a strange forced intimacy arises. The caregiver must learn to read what used to be said. A hand gesture, a tilt of the head, the tone of a moan—everything becomes vocabulary. It is like returning to the infancy of the species, to communication prior to words. But this regression is neither organic nor elegant; it is a forced rupture that leaves the caregiver translating between a lost language and a silence that does not want to be silence.

## The caregiver as an external horizon

When one's own horizon can no longer sustain identity, another horizon can do it for them. The caregiver—especially if they have shared decades of entanglement with the patient—does not merely assist with functions. They are not a medical aide who changes diapers and administers medications. They guard the other's model-of-self. They keep alive, in their own memory, in their own language, in their own affection, a version of the patient that the patient can no longer maintain for themselves.

In chapter 15 I described how the mother constructs the child's horizon before the child exists. How she speaks to the gestating baby, tells them who they are, transmits a world to them before they have eyes to see it. How the mother is, literally, the reservoir that condenses into the infant horizon. Here, the reverse occurs: the adult child sustains the mother's horizon after she can no longer sustain it for herself. It is not symmetry; it is a different operation. The mother fabricated a horizon that was going to close, that was going to gain autonomy. The child cares for a horizon that is opening, that is losing its borders, that is receding toward the reservoir.

The caregiver who knows the patient well can anticipate their needs before they express them, can interpret gestures that are incomprehensible to a stranger, can keep active—by resonance, not by instruction—the correlations that still work. They know that when the patient paces from the kitchen to the living room three times in a row, it is not restlessness but that they are looking for the bathroom and have forgotten where it is. They know that the refusal to eat is not a whim but a fear of choking because swallowing coordination is no longer reliable. They know that the blank stare out the window is not absence but, sometimes, the only way to process a world that no longer makes sense.

It is an entanglement that operates in a single direction: the caregiver models the patient with increasing precision while the patient loses the ability to model the caregiver. In physics, a unidirectional entanglement is an asymmetrical state, almost a paradox. In life, it is the most unequal love that exists: to give without receiving recognition, to sustain without being sustained, to remember for two.

The question this raises is uncomfortable: to what extent does the patient's self continue to exist in the space between the two horizons? If identity is holographic—if it is distributed in the correlations among all parts of the horizon—then when part of those correlations migrates to the caregiver's horizon, does part of the self migrate with them? If my wife reminds me of who I was, if my son tells stories of my youth, if my caregiver anticipates my fear before I feel it, where is the exact limit of my horizon? Does it end at my skin, or does it extend to the horizon of the one who cares for me?

I have no answer. I only have the question, and the certainty that caregivers who have been there know what I am talking about without me needing to explain it. They have felt, in moments of exhaustion and love, that the patient lives—partially, phantom-like, but lives—in the space they share. They have felt that their own memory of the other is not just a recollection but a residence, that the model they maintain in their head is not a replica but an extension, a territory where the patient's self still holds citizenship.

And there is something else. The caregiver is also transformed. Caring for someone who is dissolving is a mirror that anticipates our own possible degradation. Every day of caregiving is a lesson in the anatomy of the horizon, a live demonstration of how fragile everything we take for granted is. The caregiver learns, in their body and in their soul, that identity is not an inalienable property but a contingent loan. And that learning, however painful, is a form of wisdom that cannot be acquired any other way.

## The memory that does not know its name

Between the loss of episodic memory and the disappearance of language, there is a particularly bewildering intermediate stage: the world remains recognizable, but it is no longer namable. The patient knows what a chair is—they sit on it—but they cannot say "chair." They know that the person in front of them is important—their body calms, their face lights up—but they cannot access the word "son" or the proper name.

> **In physics this is called:** dissociation between procedural access and declarative access, where the system's degrees of freedom remain operative for action but not for verbalization.  
> **In daily life it is like:** driving a car without being able to explain how to drive it: the body knows, the hands know, but the explanation has fled to a country where you no longer have a visa.

This stage reveals something profound about the architecture of knowledge. Knowing-how and knowing-what do not live on the same floor of the building. A patient can still play the piano if they learned as a child, can still walk around their neighborhood, can still use kitchen utensils, all while being unable to say their own name. The body preserves maps that the mind can no longer read aloud. It is the most compelling proof that consciousness is not a monolith: it is a federation of subsystems that can fail independently.

For the model of the horizon, this means that the horizon does not degrade uniformly. Some regions—the oldest, the most practiced, the most automated—resist longer than others. The horizon is not a perfect sphere; it is a territory with fortresses and porous borders. Procedural memory is a surrounded fortress, a bastion that resists the siege when everything else has fallen. And its resistance tells us that the horizon has layers, strata, a geology of consciousness where the deepest is the last to disappear.

## Death as closure

In the final stages of Alzheimer's, death ceases to be an enemy. It becomes the closure of something that had already been gone for a long time. Family members who have watched someone disappear piece by piece sometimes experience the passing not as a loss, but as the acknowledgment of a loss that has already occurred. Grief, in those cases, does not begin with death; it ends with it. They have been mourning for years, fragment by fragment. Death is merely the confirmation of the state of affairs.

From the model of the horizon: complete evaporation is preferable to partial dissolution. A horizon that evaporates returns to the reservoir as a whole; its information is scrambled, but the process is coherent. The information is not lost in pieces; it is transformed all at once, maintaining some form of unity even if it is only the unity of the process. A horizon that dissolves at the edges never quite finishes leaving nor staying. It is an intermediate condition that has no name, an informational limbo where there is enough structure to suffer but not enough to understand.

> **In physics this is called:** coherent evaporation versus dissolution by fragmentation, where a system that gradually loses cohesion can remain in metastable states that prolong the suffering of the subsystem without allowing its reconstruction.  
> **In daily life it is like:** a candle that is blown out all at once versus one that burns out in flickers: the latter suffers more because there is enough light to see the darkness coming, but not enough to read, nor to find the exit, nor to understand what is happening.

It is the worst of all worlds: enough consciousness to suffer, insufficient to understand why. The patient in intermediate stages can feel fear without knowing what they fear, anxiety without identifying its cause, restlessness without a name. It is suffering without an object, a pain with no possible diagnosis for the one enduring it. The caregiver interprets it, names it, contains it. But the patient lives it in a raw state, like a wounded animal that does not understand the nature of the wound.

This is not an argument for euthanasia; that decision belongs to other domains—ethical, legal, personal. It is merely an observation on the geometry of suffering. There are forms of destruction of the horizon that are crueler than total destruction. There are degrees of being that are worse than non-being. Alzheimer's forces us to contemplate the possibility that death, far from being the worst fate, can sometimes be the final mercy of a process that no longer has any reversal.

But there is another side, and it would be false to omit it. Some caregivers describe moments of a strange tenderness in the final stages. The patient who no longer knows who you are but smiles at you with a smile that seems genuine, stripped of all baggage, of all history, of all resentment. It is like meeting someone for the first time every day, and every day that first impression is one of trust. The reduced horizon has less room for rancor. There is no memory of offenses. There are no narrative scars. In its extreme impoverishment, the Alzheimer's horizon achieves a kind of terrible innocence, a purity that no saint would choose but that exists, that is observable, that constitutes a way of being even if it is a way none of us would desire.

## What Alzheimer's reveals

If consciousness were a substance, Alzheimer's would not be possible in this way. A substance either is or is not. Alcohol either intoxicates or it does not. Poison either kills or it does not. But the horizon is an architecture. It can lose area, lose connectivity, lose correlations, and still be a horizon—ever smaller, poorer, more fragmented, but still a horizon. Still a limit. Still a boundary between inside and outside, even if the inside is just an unfurnished room and the outside is pure indifference.

Alzheimer's demonstrates that consciousness is not a switch. It is a continuous gradation that can be traversed in both directions, and the descending direction is not symmetrical to the ascending one. Birth is condensation: the reservoir organizing itself, acquiring shape, differentiating into parts that correlate. Death is evaporation: the horizon returning to the reservoir as a whole, disorganizing but maintaining its unity until the end. Alzheimer's is erosion: the horizon losing pieces without losing its condition as a horizon, degrading by sectors, becoming a map with blank zones where there was once inhabited territory.

And erosion teaches something that condensation and evaporation do not teach: that the horizon is fragile not because it can be destroyed—everything can be destroyed—but because it can be destroyed in pieces, preserving in each piece the illusion that there is still a whole. This is the cruelty of Alzheimer's: not annihilation, but simulation. The patient seems to be there, and in some way they are. But the "there" is ever narrower, more hollow, more incomplete. It is a horizon that lies about its own extent, presenting a facade of integrity while behind it the walls are crumbling.

Alzheimer's is, ultimately, a lesson in humility for any theory of consciousness. It does not allow us to think of the mind as a simple entity, as an indivisible soul, as software running on hardware. It forces us to think of it as an ecosystem, as a network, as a fabric that can thin out until it almost disappears without ceasing to be, for years, a way of being in the world. Every patient is a living demonstration that consciousness is emergence, not essence. That it can be built slowly, over decades, and dismantled slowly, over decades more. That there is no single moment when it "switches off," but a long twilight where every sunset lasts for months.

When I see an advanced patient look at a flower without knowing what it is, but smiling at its color, I think of the most reduced horizon possible: perception without knowledge, affect without history, presence without identity. And I think that this minimal horizon is still, somehow, a way of being conscious. It is not the consciousness any of us would choose. But it is consciousness. It is proof that the phenomenon can be reduced almost to its skeleton and still remain standing.

Alzheimer's shows us the floorboards. It takes away the furniture, the curtains, the paintings on the walls, until only the load-bearing structure is left. And then it starts removing beams. But as long as one remains, as long as there remains a single correlation relating an inside to an outside, there is a horizon. There is a world, however small. There is a someone, however diffuse, who is having an experience.

That is the ultimate limit of the model: not the question of why there is consciousness, but the realization of how much it can lose and still be. Alzheimer's is the antithesis of birth. If birth is the miracle of condensation, Alzheimer's is the tragedy of dissolution. But both teach us the same thing: that the horizon is not a given, that it is a construction, that it is a contingent work and therefore a work that can be undone. That consciousness is not a permanent gift but a loan that someday, somehow, is returned.

> **What we do know:** Alzheimer's progressively destroys neural connections before the neurons themselves. The areas of episodic and semantic memory are the first to degrade.
>
> **What we do not know:** Why some advanced patients respond to familiar stimuli without functional explicit memory. Where the exact threshold is at which a horizon ceases to be one.
>
> **Remaining questions:** To what extent does the self of an Alzheimer's patient reside in the entanglement with their caregivers? Is it possible to measure the "mass" of a contracting horizon?
>
> **If you take away just one idea:** Consciousness is not a switch that turns off. It is an architecture that can lose bricks one by one, and continue holding up the sky with less roof every day.
