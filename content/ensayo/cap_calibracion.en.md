Chapter 28 described two selections that act on the Reservoir before a horizon exists as such: one that decides which configurations are stable enough to last, and another — the bias of the immediate environment at the moment of condensation — that decides which specific shape, among the stable ones, that horizon takes. Neither selection asks permission. Neither is reversible after the fact.

This chapter applies that same machinery to three questions that culture has almost always treated as one: whom a horizon desires, who it knows itself to be, and what body it has. These are three distinct axes. None of the three is chosen. None of the three is a calibration error.

> **In physics this is called:** three independent degrees of freedom of the same system, each with its own dynamics and its own selection process.  
> **In daily life it's like:** a person's height, eye color, and blood type: traits that coexist in the same body without any one of them determining the others.

### Orientation as bias, not choice

No one remembers deciding whom to desire. That alone is data: decisions leave an introspective trace — the moment options were weighed — and sexual orientation leaves none, in either direction, for anyone. What research has documented over recent decades is the concrete shape of that bias, without ever finding a switch.

Bailey and Pillard's twin studies, in 1991, found that when one identical twin is gay, the other is too around 52% of the time; among fraternal twins, that concordance drops to roughly 22%; among adopted brothers with no genetic relation, it drops further still. The progression — more concordance the more genome is shared — points to a real heritable component, but a 52% concordance in genetically identical twins also means that nearly half the time the twin sharing the exact same genome does not share the orientation. Genetics influences. It does not dictate.

In 1993, Dean Hamer believed he had located part of that influence in a region of the X chromosome called Xq28, after studying gay brothers. The finding became famous — "the gay gene," the press called it, simplifying what Hamer never claimed. George Ebers's replication attempt, with a different sample, found no such signal. The largest genome-wide association study done to date, Ganna et al. in 2019, with close to 500,000 people, confirms the general shape of that first finding and disproves the detail: there is no single locus. There are at least five regions of the genome with a weak statistical association, which together explain between 8% and 25% of the observed variation, and which cannot predict any one person's orientation from their DNA. Orientation, like most complex behavioral traits, is polygenic: the bias from chapter 28 is not written in a single gene an editor could cross out. It is distributed across the whole architecture, in a way no current instrument can read with individual precision.

> **In physics this is called:** a polygenic trait of low individual heritability: the signal is real at the population level and nearly useless as a prediction for any one case.  
> **In daily life it's like:** a person's height: we know it depends heavily on their parents' genes, and yet no genetic test done at birth can tell you exactly how tall they will grow.

There is a second line of evidence, stranger and better replicated than Xq28 itself: the fraternal birth order effect. Ray Blanchard and Anthony Bogaert documented, and later studies across very different populations have confirmed, that the more biological older brothers a man has, the higher the statistical likelihood that he is gay — an effect that does not appear with older sisters, nor with adoptive or non-biological brothers raised in the same household, which rules out purely social explanations for this specific correlation. The best-supported hypothesis is immunological: a mother's immune system might generate, with each successive male pregnancy, a growing response against a Y-linked protein involved in fetal brain development, cumulatively altering how that development is calibrated in successive male offspring. It is not a closed certainty — a critique published in 2023 argues part of the effect could be a statistical artifact of how samples are selected — but, like the genetics, it points to the same place: something that happens before birth, beyond the reach of any decision, any upbringing, or any later "correction."

> **In physics this is called:** a prenatal boundary condition that biases the developmental trajectory without the resulting system being able, afterward, to reverse that condition on its own.  
> **In daily life it's like:** the current that pushes a paper boat off course before the child who launched it ever gets a chance to steer it: the drift was already in the water, not in the boat.

### Identity as the internal model, not the interface

Chapter 23 described encapsulation: a software object holds an internal state that nothing outside can touch directly, and all that's visible from the outside is its public interface. The body a horizon is born with — its anatomy, the sex a doctor writes on a form minutes after birth — is, in that vocabulary, the interface. Gender identity is the internal state: the model a horizon has of itself, one it doesn't even access through direct introspection, but discovers over time, the same way it discovers any other persistent fact about its own architecture.

That interface and internal state coincide most of the time says nothing about which of the two is real: both are. All it says is that, for most systems, the bias of the condensation environment and the bodily interface ended up aligned. When they aren't, there is no error in the internal state and none in the interface. There is a mismatch between two layers of the same system that, in most cases, don't even communicate with each other directly.

> **In physics this is called:** decoupling between a system's internal state and the interface that exposes it externally, without either one ceasing to be valid within its own domain.  
> **In daily life it's like:** a suitcase with a tag someone else stuck on it at the airport: the tag can be wrong without that changing, in the slightest, what's actually inside the suitcase.

In 1995, Zhou, Hofman, Gooren, and Swaab published in *Nature* the finding that the central subdivision of the bed nucleus of the stria terminalis — a brain region involved in sexual behavior, whose size differs consistently between men and women — had, in trans women, a size statistically indistinguishable from that of cisgender women, regardless of hormones received in adulthood or the participants' sexual orientation. It is the first structural finding of its kind, and it deserves the same caution the scientific community has treated it with ever since: the sample was small, in-vivo replication (as opposed to post-mortem) is methodologically difficult, and later neuroimaging studies have produced mixed results, some concordant and some not. What rests on much firmer ground than any single neuroanatomy finding is the World Health Organization's own decision: in 2019, gender incongruence was removed entirely from the chapter on mental and behavioral disorders of the International Classification of Diseases — where it had lived, under various names, for as long as the ICD has existed — and moved to a chapter on sexual health. Not because it stopped being real. Because the accumulated evidence no longer supported calling it a disorder.

> **In physics this is called:** taxonomic reclassification driven by updated evidence, not by a change in the phenomenon being classified.  
> **In daily life it's like:** removing something from the "malfunctions" list that was never actually broken: the file changes, not the thing filed.

### Sex, body, and reproduction: axes that don't travel together

Chapter 28, discussing surrogate gestation, in-vitro fertilization, and ectogenesis, already described a principle worth making explicit here: reproductive capacity, sexual anatomy, gender identity, and orientation are four variables that, in the vast majority of horizons, travel together — and precisely because of that, most human cultures historically treated them as a single thing wearing four different names.

They are not one thing. There are people with intersex anatomy — chromosomal, gonadal, or genital variations that don't fit cleanly into the two categories most administrative forms offer — whose very existence demonstrates that even "biological sex," held to the rigor biology itself demands, is not binary in the strict sense of the word; estimates of its frequency vary widely depending on which variations get counted, but no serious estimate gives zero. There are fertile and infertile people on both sides of any of the other three variables, with fertility determining neither anyone's identity nor their orientation. There are people whose gender identity doesn't match their anatomy and whose sexual orientation — whom they desire, not who they are — is an entirely separate question to answer. Treating these four axes as though knowing one automatically resolved the other three isn't just imprecise: it's the same kind of error a doctor would make inferring a patient's blood type from their height.

> **In physics this is called:** approximately independent variables that superficial observation conflates because of their high statistical correlation in the most visible sample.  
> **In daily life it's like:** assuming everyone who can swim can also surf, because most of the surfers you know happen to swim well too.

### Discrimination as a failure of translation

Chapter 38 described translation as the function that converts a horizon's inner signal into something the outside world can receive, and it also described what happens when that translation is denied to someone capable of providing it for themself: there, the translator was missing by absence. Here, it is missing by refusal. Discrimination against a horizon for its orientation or its identity is not the absence of a translation channel. It is the active decision, sustained institutionally for centuries, not to translate a signal that arrives perfectly clear, and to declare instead that the signal itself is corrupted.

So-called conversion therapy is the best-documented case of what happens when that refusal turns into active intervention: the attempt to forcibly recalibrate a system that was never miscalibrated. The American Psychiatric Association, the World Health Organization, and virtually every relevant medical and psychological body have condemned it as ineffective and harmful. The study by Turban, Beckwith, and Reisner, published in 2019 using the largest survey ever conducted of transgender people in the United States — over 27,000 responses — found that those who recalled having been subjected to such conversion attempts were more than twice as likely, over their lifetime, to have attempted suicide; if the exposure had happened before age ten, the likelihood was four times higher. The harm didn't depend on whether the person administering the "therapy" was a licensed professional or an untrained religious guide. The mechanism of harm wasn't in the method. It was in the premise: the conviction that there was something to correct.

> **In physics this is called:** active intervention on a stable system, aimed at forcing it toward a state its own dynamics do not support, with measurable cost and no functional gain.  
> **In daily life it's like:** forcing a lock that was never actually jammed: the mechanism doesn't give because it was miscalibrated — it gives because something breaks from the forcing.

### Rights as recognition, not as concession

In 1990, the World Health Organization removed homosexuality from its list of diseases; that May 17th is now commemorated as the International Day Against Homophobia, Transphobia, and Biphobia. In 2006, a group of twenty-nine jurists and human rights experts from twenty-five countries met in the Indonesian city of Yogyakarta to draft a set of principles on how existing international law — the law protecting dignity, non-discrimination, private life — applies to sexual orientation and gender identity; a 2017 update added sex characteristics and the right to legal recognition of one's own body. Neither document invented a new right. Both simply declared that rights already recognized for any horizon applied to these as well.

That is, perhaps, the most precise description of what a right means in this particular domain: not a concession a majority grants to a minority, but the belated recognition that the translation was owed all along. The speed of that recognition remains radically unequal between countries — in some, orientation or identity are still grounds for criminal prosecution; in others, marriage, parentage, or legal name and sex changes have been settled for decades — and that inequality is not a minor detail of the problem. It is, itself, the exact measure of how much translation is still missing by refusal, chapter by chapter, country by country.

> **In physics this is called:** geographic heterogeneity in the speed of recognizing the same structural fact, not heterogeneity in the fact itself.  
> **In daily life it's like:** the same time zone that some clocks already show while others, by the choice of whoever sets them, still refuse to.

### What this chapter doesn't resolve

This chapter doesn't fully explain any of the three variables it describes. It can't: the science that studies them can't either, not yet, and this book has tried, chapter after chapter, not to claim more than the evidence supports. What it can say, with the same precision chapter 28 used to separate stability from bias, is this: a horizon doesn't choose the shape of its own calibration, any more than it chose the reservoir it condensed from; the absence of a complete explanation is not evidence that something is miscalibrated, only that the calibration is more complex than any simple explanation can capture; and the right question, faced with a horizon whose calibration doesn't match the majority's, was never "why is it like this?" It was the same question this book has asked, in different guises, in nearly every one of its chapters: what does this particular horizon need for its translation to the outside world to arrive complete?

> **What we do know:** twin studies (Bailey & Pillard, 1991) show partial heritability of sexual orientation; the genome-wide association study by Ganna et al. (2019), with close to 500,000 participants, identified five loci with weak statistical association that together explain between 8% and 25% of observed variation, without allowing individual prediction; the fraternal birth order effect (Blanchard & Bogaert) is widely replicated across diverse populations; Zhou et al. (1995) documented a structural difference in the bed nucleus of the stria terminalis between trans women and cisgender men; the World Health Organization removed homosexuality from its disease list in 1990 and reclassified gender incongruence out of mental disorders in 2019; major international medical and psychological bodies have condemned conversion therapy, and the Turban, Beckwith & Reisner study (2019) of 27,715 transgender people found an association between recalled exposure to such attempts and more than double the lifetime risk of a suicide attempt.
>
> **What we do not know:** why Hamer's original finding on the Xq28 locus (1993) failed to replicate in later studies with different samples; whether the fraternal birth order effect fully reflects a prenatal immunological mechanism or whether part of it is a sampling artifact, as a 2023 critique has argued; how much of Zhou et al.'s finding on the stria terminalis holds up in in-vivo neuroimaging studies, whose results have so far been mixed; and what exact fraction of the variation in any of the three variables — orientation, identity, biological sex — corresponds to prenatal factors versus later developmental ones, a question current science cannot close with the evidence available.
>
> **Remaining questions:** Is the near-total absence of a single determining cause, across all three variables, a temporary limit of research or a structural property of genuinely polygenic, multifactorial traits? Does the ethical or legal criterion for recognizing a right change if the underlying biological evidence changes? Can a society recognize the validity of a calibration without fully understanding its origin?
>
> **If you only take away one idea:** no horizon chose how it was calibrated. The question worth asking is never why it was calibrated that way, but what it needs for its translation to the world to arrive complete.
