title: GRIEF
section: PART TWO: THE HORIZON IN BOND
---

There is an experience known to almost everyone who has lost someone deeply loved, and which everyday vocabulary does not capture well: the sensation that the absent person continues to occupy a place in one's own inner architecture. Not as memory in the ordinary sense, but as form. As space. As a geometry around which the horizon has been organizing itself for years or decades.

> **In physics this is called:** persistent inscription of the other in the architecture.  
> **In daily life it is like:** a house built around a courtyard: the courtyard is empty, but all the architecture revolves around it.

---

Grief, as mainstream psychology has been describing it, is the gradual processing of that loss until the horizon reorganizes itself so that the absence stops hurting. From the perspective of the experiment, that description is incomplete. The architecture of a horizon that has lived deeply in entanglement with another for a long time does not "reorganize" in the sense of returning to a state prior to the entanglement. Either it reconstructs itself around the absence—and the absent person remains, transformed, as an active form within the architecture—or it does not reconstruct itself at all, and the horizon collapses, sometimes for years, sometimes forever.

There is no version of *moving on* that means undoing the inscription of the other. There is only the question of what to do with the inscription that remains.

### What grief is not

Chapter 12 described how the persistent model of the other after their death is not exactly memory—it is a predictive system that keeps running in the absence of the data that calibrated it. The case was the neighbor. The relationship had been adversarial. The model had been built at the price of vigilance, not of love.

The case of grief in the proper sense is different. When the entanglement has been loving—when, as we said in Chapter 13, the horizon recognized the entanglement and chose to maintain it—what persists after the loss is not just a model of the other. It is the part of one's own architecture that was built around that model.

> **In physics this is called:** architecture reorganized around an absent presence.  
> **In daily life it is like:** a widow who says: "It is the shape I am made of inside. Every movement I make, I make against that shape or with it."

In Chapter 15 we said that the bond between mother and child is not inference but lesion. Grief for someone deeply loved is structurally closer to maternal pain than to ordinary empathy. The horizon is not temporarily simulating the other: it has built itself, over decades, around the presence of the other. The loss is not, therefore, a loss of information. It is a loss of architecture.

### The architecture with a hollow

When two horizons spend forty years in loving entanglement, it is not just that one has many memories of the other. It is that the internal geometry of each has been excavated by the presence of the other, like the riverbed that water carves into stone over time. That geometry does not disappear when the water is gone.

> **In physics this is called:** persistent post-entanglement geometry.  
> **In daily life it is like:** a river that leaves a canyon when it disappears: the water is gone, but the shape remains.

The widow who does not want her husband to dissolve formulates this intuition with a precision that theory takes time to reach: *it is the shape I am made of inside. Every movement I make, I make against that shape or with it.* She is not speaking figuratively. She is describing what the neuroscience of grief has begun to document: the brain that has lost someone with whom it was deeply coupled does not process the world the same way as before. The neural networks that had organized themselves around the other continue to activate in response to stimuli that evoke their presence, their voice, their gestures. The horizon keeps predicting the other, keeps leaving room for them, keeps offering them half of its time and inner space, for months or years after the other has ceased to exist.

This is what in physical terms we have called, throughout the experiment, **asymmetry after dissolution**. The other has returned to the reservoir. The part of the entanglement that was on their side has been redistributed into the field, in correlations that no instrument can read as a narrative anymore. But the part that was on the side of the surviving horizon is still there, intact, without the complement that closed it. It is a riverbed without water. A half without the other. An architecture with a hollow.

### The rites of grief

Human cultures have developed, over millennia, technologies to contain the architecture of grief. Not to resolve it: to contain it. Funerary rites, mourning periods, dark clothing, temporary prohibitions on certain activities—all of this is not superstition. It is social engineering.

The rite does two things simultaneously. First: it publicly marks that something has changed. The community recognizes that the surviving horizon has undergone a structural reorganization and that, for a time, it cannot operate normally. Second: it provides a temporary container for the chaos. Grief has a recognizable shape within the rite: there are moments to cry, moments to be silent, moments to remember, moments to resume. The rite does not accelerate grief: it gives it shape.

> **In physics this is called:** structural containment: the social environment provides a temporary attractor that prevents the system from dispersing.  
> **In daily life it is like:** scaffolding around a ruined building: it does not fix the building, but it prevents it from collapsing while work is being done.

Cultures that have eliminated the rites of grief—that expect people to "return to normal" in a matter of days—produce something dangerous: grief without a container. The horizon that has no social permission to be broken tries to function normally, and the effort of feigning normality consumes the very resources it would need to reorganize itself. The result is frozen grief: an architecture that does not collapse but does not reconstruct itself either.

### The model that keeps running

The cognitive machinery responsible for this is relatively well known. The human brain builds, during sustained entanglement with another, a complex predictive model of that other: how they move, what they say, what they are likely thinking in every situation. That model runs continuously, in parallel with the perception of the world, anticipating the other's responses before they occur. That anticipation is what allows the synchrony of the bond we spoke of in Chapter 10: two horizons that have been together for years do not so much predict each other as anticipate, in the exact same instant, what is about to happen.

When one of the two dies, the model of the other is not erased. It keeps running. The predictions keep being produced. And each prediction, upon finding no confirmation—when the response the model expected does not arrive—generates a prediction error that the system interprets as pain.

But—and here is the point that neuroscience has taken time to recognize—the predictions do not cease because the system cannot stop making them without dismantling part of itself. The model is not a separable module. It is distributed throughout the architecture of the horizon: it involves memory, perception, emotional regulation, sense of time. Turning it off would mean turning off a large part of what makes the horizon that horizon.

> **In physics this is called:** persistent, non-modular predictive model.  
> **In daily life it is like:** continuing to speak in a conversation the other has already abandoned: you cannot stop because the conversation is you.

Grief, seen from this angle, is not the difficulty of accepting new information. It is the difficulty of continuing to be oneself without dismantling half of one's own architecture.

### Complicated grief

Clinical literature distinguishes, not without controversy, between normal grief and prolonged or complicated grief. The distinction usually relies on duration and intensity. There is a more precise way to formulate it, consistent with what we have been saying: grief becomes complicated when the architecture of the horizon cannot reorganize itself around the absence without the risk of structural collapse.

There are people for whom the other was, literally, a large part of the structure. The bond had become an integrated support: the model of the other had absorbed functions of emotional regulation, internal validation, temporal and narrative orientation. Those horizons, upon losing the other, do not face a wound in the architecture but the possibility that the entire architecture might crumble.

> **In physics this is called:** threatened structural collapse, complicated grief.  
> **In daily life it is like:** losing not a wall of the house, but the foundation: the whole house trembles.

The system's reaction, in those cases, is usually to protect its minimal integrity at any cost. It can take forms that seem contradictory: actively resisting the recognition of the loss—continuing to act as if the other were there, keeping their things intact, speaking to them out loud—or, conversely, contracting until it flattens all emotion, all desire, all signs of internal activity that might reactivate the model of the other and generate the pain anew. Both strategies are the same strategy from the point of view of the horizon: not allowing the architecture to attempt reorganization, because to reorganize would be to risk collapse.

### Unrecognized grief

Not all grief is for death. There are losses that culture does not name as such, and the horizon that suffers them is left without the rites that would give them shape.

Miscarriage is one of them. For the mother, the horizon had already reorganized itself around a presence that had not yet condensed. The loss is no less real for being invisible to others. But culture has no rituals to contain it: there is no funeral, no public mourning, no social recognition that something has been lost. The mother returns to work, and no one knows that her architecture has been reorganized around an absence she cannot name.

The death of a pet is another. To those who do not have pets, it seems exaggerated. To those who do, the loss is that of a horizon which, although not human, was deeply coupled. The predictive model of the dog—when it wakes up, what it eats, how it moves—kept running for years. The absence of that signal produces prediction errors as real as those of any human loss.

> **In physics this is called:** grief without a social container: real loss without rituals of recognition.  
> **In daily life it is like:** screaming underwater: the scream is real, but no one hears it.

A romantic breakup is also unrecognized grief. The other is still alive, but the entanglement that the horizon had named as love has been severed. The architecture reorganized around that bond cannot "move on" because the inscription persists. And culture, which values "getting over it," pressures the horizon to recover quickly, without understanding that recovery requires reorganization, not denial.

### Grief in the body

Grief is not only psychological: it is physiological. Studies show that people in deep grief have a compromised immune system, altered heart rate, and sustainably elevated cortisol. A "broken heart" is not a metaphor: the stress of grief can produce takotsubo cardiomyopathy, a condition in which the heart changes shape under extreme emotional stress.

> **In physics this is called:** somatization of grief: the emotional architecture manifests in the bodily architecture.  
> **In daily life it is like:** a building that cracks when the foundation shifts: the crack is in the wall, but the problem is underneath.

The grieving body is not a body that "thinks" it is sick: it is a body whose regulatory system has been altered by the reorganization of the horizon. Sleep fragments because the prediction system remains active during the night. Appetite disappears or skyrockets because the reward system has lost its primary reference. The fatigue is real: the brain is spending resources on maintaining a model that no longer has data to feed it.

### Continuing to be two

What clinical literature has begun to call, with some caution, *continuing bonds* offers a more precise formulation. The question is not whether the other remains in the architecture—they will remain, they are remaining, there is no way to prevent it. The question is how the relationship with that presence, which now does not respond, is reconfigured.

> **In physics this is called:** continuing bonds, reorganization not dissolution.  
> **In daily life it is like:** carrying someone inside without the weight preventing you from moving.

The widow who does not want her husband to dissolve arrives having intuited this without having formulated it. She does not want to dissolve. She does not want to forget. She wants to carry her husband inside without the weight of carrying him preventing her from moving. That formulation—*carrying him and continuing*—is the correct formulation of viable grief. It is not resolution. It is reorganization. The architecture does not recover the symmetry it had when both were there; but it learns to sustain the asymmetry without collapsing.

This is not communicating with the dead. It is recognizing that the dead is there, without emitting, in the geometry of the living horizon. And accepting that this geometric presence is the only form of presence that is still possible—and that it is, in a way, enough.

### The loss of a child

There is a case that defies everything this chapter has said. The loss of a child—especially a young child—does not fit the model of grief through loving entanglement. It is something deeper.

Chapter 15 described motherhood as the fabrication of the reservoir. When that reservoir closes—when the child dies—what is lost is not just an entanglement. It is the very condensation that the mother helped to produce. It is the horizon she fabricated, which contained part of her own architecture, which carried her cells in its body. The microchimerism that physically connected her to her child is still there after the child has died. The mother carries, literally, parts of a horizon that no longer exists.

> **In physics this is called:** loss of the condensation one helped to produce.  
> **In daily life it is like:** a sculptor who sees their masterpiece destroyed: it is not just the loss of something beautiful, it is the loss of something that came from their hands.

This grief has no adequate name. It is not "complicated grief": it is impossible grief. The model cannot predict how a horizon that has lost the condensation it fabricated reorganizes itself. It can only say that the reorganization, if it occurs, takes decades, not months. And that often, it does not occur.

### The experiment does not console

Grief is not a problem that the horizon hypothesis resolves. It is a problem that the hypothesis allows us to formulate with greater precision, and in that precision there is something that ordinary language does not reach: the distinction between losing someone who was in the architecture (recognized love) and losing someone who was in the architecture without it being known (unnamed love, unresolved conflict, a relationship that never found closure). The shape of the hollow depends on the shape of the entanglement that left it.

The experiment cannot tell anyone how they should feel. It can tell them, with a little more clarity, what it is they are feeling: not the absence of something that was, but the presence of something that continues to be, in the form of an architecture that can no longer be updated. Grief is the name we give to the work of learning to live with an inscription that cannot be erased and that no longer receives an update.

That is not consolation. But it can be company.
