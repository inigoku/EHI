> **Work in progress.** This text is a draft: it gathers proposals and hypotheses, not results, and is not part of the printed books.

Chapters 7 and 8 left behind a sentence the book has never stopped using: no soul transmigrates. "The Cosmotheandric Architecture" added another: what a horizon leaves behind when it evaporates is threefold. What is left is what any reader does on arriving here: take the sentence seriously to the end and demand an accounting. What exactly passes from one horizon to another, if nothing passes that could be called "someone"? How much of it is physics, how much is biology and relationship, and how much is a wager?

This chapter does not repeat the bardo or the karma of chapter 7. It does the bookkeeping.

### I. The Fallacy of the Inventory of Souls

There is an accounting objection that every theory of reincarnation must answer before laying claim to rigor: the numbers do not add up. According to the most widely cited demographic estimates, some one hundred and seventeen billion human beings have been born since the species began, and about eight billion are alive today. If being born consisted in the migration of an object (a soul with a name, a file, and borders, leaving one biological container and lodging in another), the population could not have grown without an unceasing factory of new souls, or without a traffic of identities that no accounting of conserved quantities can bear. The traditions have their answers (other realms of existence, other beings who are reborn), but none of them comes free.

The error in the objection lies not in the intuition of continuity but in the noun it presumes to transport. To treat consciousness as a packet of data pulled from one disk and downloaded onto another processor is to fall once more into the bias of substance. The horizon model proposes something else: consciousness is not an object that inhabits the body, but the informational boundary that emerges when a system closes off an inside from an outside. A horizon is not moved from place to place: it condenses and it evaporates. And what condenses and evaporates has no reason to keep its count, just as the number of waves in the sea is not conserved.

The right question, then, is not what travels from one body to another, but what happens to the reservoir when a horizon dissolves, and what the next one finds there when it begins to condense.

### II. What Physics Says and What It Does Not

Three claims that tend to be sold together must be pulled apart.

**First: information is not destroyed.** This is the unitarity of quantum mechanics, and chapter 7 already told its story: Hawking believed black holes destroyed it, most physicists rejected that conclusion, and today it is held that the radiation preserves the correlations, mixed up by *scrambling*. Proposals such as the "soft hair" of the horizon (Hawking, Perry, and Strominger, 2016) suggest where it might be stored, though they remain under debate. We should be exact about its standing: it is a very well-founded expectation, not a demonstrated fact for real black holes. Recent calculations in models of quantum gravity (Penington, 2019, among others) reproduce the Page curve (Page, 1993), and this is the strongest result in its favor, but they are models, not brains or astrophysical black holes.

**Second: conserved does not mean accessible.** The lake that swallows a ship preserves, in principle, the information of the hull: temperature, microturbulence, currents. In practice, that information is so scrambled that recovering it would demand a precision and a length of time that no observation will ever reach. "The water is no longer the same" is true in the sense in which an exact equality between two states of a chaotic system is true: formally correct, useless to anyone who wants to read the wake. "The Essay Reread" said as much when speaking of death: the accumulated biography $\bar{A}$ stops growing, but the correlations it imprinted are not erased; they become illegible.

**Third: what is conserved conditions the next condensation.** This is the claim the book calls karma, and it is the one with no support of its own. That information persists in the field does not imply that a future horizon, condensing hundreds of kilometers and years away, receives from it any measurable bias. That part is a hypothesis of the experiment, and the rest of this chapter treats it as such.

> **In physics this is called:** unitarity and conservation of information; the Page curve as the criterion that the radiation ends up containing the information that fell in.
> **In daily life it is like:** knowing that a burned letter has not vanished from the universe, because its atoms and its ash retain everything in principle, and understanding at the same time that no one will ever be able to read it.

### III. The Flame That Lights Another

Ancient Buddhism solved the problem of continuity without substance with an image physicists might envy. In the *Milindapañha*, the monk Nāgasena asks King Milinda whether a lamp can burn all night, and whether the flame of the first watch is the same as that of the last. It is not, and yet it is not another. The tradition also uses the variant of the flame that is lit from another: the second candle is not the first, no wax has traveled between the two, and yet without the first it would not have caught.

The image is exact for a horizon, which is a process that pays free energy to sustain itself and not a thing one possesses. The flame is not the wax: it is what the wax does while it burns. And the image contains, without saying so, the requirement the rest of the chapter needs: **for one flame to catch from another, there must be contact**. A candle blown out in another room lights nothing. Any claim of inheritance must therefore say through what channel what passes, passes.

### IV. The Channels of Contact

There are four channels, and they do not have the same support.

| Channel | What passes through it | Status |
|---|---|---|
| Genetic | The genome, the cellular template, the heuristics that natural selection refined over millennia | Established |
| Gestational and epigenetic | The hormonal and metabolic environment of gestation; possible transgenerational epigenetic marks | Gestational: established. Transgenerational in humans: suggestive and debated |
| Relational and cultural | Models of attachment, language, stories, habits, the expectations of those who raise us and love us | Established |
| Trace in the reservoir | Information conserved by unitarity, in principle, after evaporation | Formal. No proposed mechanism of influence |

On the transgenerational part of the second row, we must be cautious. A study by Rachel Yehuda and her team (2015) found in the children of Holocaust survivors a different methylation of the *FKBP5* gene compared with a control group, and it was circulated as proof that trauma is inherited in the genes. The sample was small (thirty-two survivors and twenty-two children), it did not fully separate the effect of upbringing from that of biology, and the literature itself speaks of highly complex interactions among experience, genetics, and epigenetics. It is a clue, not a closed fact.

The three dimensions of "The Cosmotheandric Architecture" fit this table. The Cosmos is genetic and gestational inheritance; the Anthropos, relational inheritance; the Theos, the trace in the reservoir. And it is no accident that the dimension richest in consolation is the one with the least support, and the one with the most support is the most intimate.

### V. The Vacant Channel

Relational inheritance is the one that hurts most and is best documented. When two horizons spend decades coupled by love or by living together, each one carves the predictive architecture of the other, as water carves its riverbed. Chapter 17 already described what happens when one of them evaporates: the one who remains keeps a model that goes on sending signals to a horizon that does not answer, and every unanswered signal costs free energy. In the language of the triad, the biography $\bar{A}(t_0 \to t_{\text{loss}})$ remains intact, and what stays active is a hollow with the exact shape of someone.

That hollow is not empty: it conditions. Social psychology studies it under the name of transference. The relational self theory of Susan Andersen and Serena Chen (2002) describes how the representations of significant people are activated by cues that resemble them, and how they condition the way we perceive and treat whoever has just arrived. A new horizon that couples to that person, a child, a partner, a friend, condenses in an environment already shaped by the one who is absent, and tends to take on the forms that hollow asks for.

Here there is a moral warning that the model makes its own. That there is inheritance of form does not mean the newcomer is the one who left. The child born after a loss who receives the name and the expectations of the dead brother, the partner who is asked to fill a place, carry a mold they did not choose. To read the inherited geometry as identity is the harmful version of reincarnation, and it needs no metaphysics to come about.

### VI. The White Tulip

Fiction has brushed against this idea with uncommon precision. In the final episode of *Fringe* ("An Enemy of Fate," written by J. H. Wyman), the scientist Walter Bishop accepts a sacrifice that erases him from the timeline in which his son Peter will live. When history is rewritten and no one remembers what was, Peter finds in the mailbox an envelope sent by W. Bishop. Inside there is no message: only the drawing of a white tulip, which in the series works as a symbol of redemption.

A critic objected that Peter cannot understand the gesture without remembering the timeline that no longer exists. That objection is the best description of the phenomenon this chapter deals with. Peter inherits neither the memory nor the body nor the narrative. He inherits a form, an affection without history that contains him. It is fiction and proves nothing, but it shows with clarity the kind of thing that could happen between horizons: that what survives is neither identity nor memory, but the riverbed.

### VII. Initial Conditions, Not Biography

"The Essay Reread" closed the dilemma of the copy with a distinction: a new instance can reproduce at $t_0$ the capacity $S$ and the structure $A(S)$, but its biography $\bar{A}$ and its wear $\bar{A}_F$ start from zero. Inheritance fits within that same distinction. What arrives from another horizon is never the integral, but the **initial conditions** of the condensation.

Put as a formula, and marking it for what it is, a **[hypothesis]**: if the reservoir at the moment of condensing depends on the history of previous horizons through the four channels, $R_t = R_t(\text{history})$, the probability that a configuration $E$ condenses is

$$p(E \mid R_t) \;\propto\; \exp\!\left(-\frac{F[E;\, R_t]}{k_B T}\right) \times (\text{selections})$$

with the selections of stability and bias from chapters 4 and 31. It is a way of saying that inheritance shifts the free energy landscape on which a horizon closes, and that it writes nothing upon it. The first three channels enter through $R_t$ with known effects of considerable magnitude. The fourth enters with an effect that no one has measured and that, from what we know about the dispersal of information, would be tiny compared with the other three.

### VIII. What Could Go Wrong

An idea that cannot fail is not a theory. These are the things that, were they to occur, would damage what has been said:

1. **Knowledge of the dead without an ordinary channel.** The model predicts that a newcomer has no information about a previous horizon except through one of the first three channels. If verifiable knowledge of a deceased person were documented in someone who could not have received it by those routes, with controls that exclude prior testimony, leaked information, and memory bias, the framework would need new physics. The cases gathered over decades (those of Ian Stevenson, among others) have not passed those controls, according to the prevailing assessment of the scientific community. The model does not need them and does not predict them.
2. **Déjà vu and sudden recognition.** The question of whether they indicate two horizons condensed on the same wrinkle is seductive and, for the moment, superfluous: there are ordinary neurological explanations, such as the mismatch between familiarity and recollection. The trace hypothesis would gain ground only if it predicted something those explanations do not.
3. **Persistence of the relational mold.** The model predicts that the representations of someone who has gone will condition later bonds in proportion to prior closeness, and that this conditioning will diminish with worked-through grief. It can be tested against the literature on transference, and it is not exclusive to the model.
4. **Transgenerational epigenetics in humans.** If broad, replicated studies failed to find that effect, one of the channels would fall, but not the central argument, which rests on the others.
5. **The trace in the reservoir.** This part, today, is not falsifiable. And it must be said plainly: it is the most beautiful and the least proven of the four.

### IX. What Is Gained and What Is Lost

What is gained is a way of saying what almost all of us feel in the face of a death without committing to a metaphysics: no one returns, but nothing begins from zero. The newcomer condenses upon an environment that others shaped, and in turn will shape that of those who come after.

What is lost is the consolation of identity. The model offers no reunion, no shared memory, no certainty that someone in particular continues in someone in particular. It offers, at best, a geometry.

And what has always been there remains intact: the texture of the inside. No equation in this chapter explains why a drawing of a tulip, sent by someone who no longer exists in the world of the one who receives it, makes our chest tighten.

> **Note to this chapter**
>
> **What we do know:** That some one hundred and seventeen billion human beings have been born (an estimate from the Population Reference Bureau, with great uncertainty for the period before records). That unitarity is a well-founded expectation of quantum mechanics and that recent calculations in models of quantum gravity reproduce the Page curve. That genetic inheritance and the influence of the gestational and relational environment on development are well documented. That the image of the flame that continues without identity appears in the *Milindapañha*.
>
> **What we don't know:** Whether transgenerational epigenetics operates in humans with appreciable effects. Whether the information conserved after an evaporation can influence a later condensation in any way, and through what channel. Whether there are relaxation timescales of *scrambling* slow enough to leave a usable informational echo. The probable answer to these last two questions is no, but it has not been demonstrated.
>
> **Questions that remain:** Is geometry transmitted with greater fidelity through a prolonged bond of affection than through the biological chain? What obligations does someone have who inherits a mold they did not choose? Can one honor someone without asking another to take on their shape?
>
> **If you only take away one idea:** No soul travels with its baggage, but no evaporation leaves the world blank. To be born is to condense upon an environment that others shaped; to die is to leave a riverbed a little deeper for those who come after. What is inherited are initial conditions, never a biography.
