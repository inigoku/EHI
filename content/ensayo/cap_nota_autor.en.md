*The notes the author left at the close of each edition —the book as it was first published, and the chamber variations written afterward— gathered in a single place.*

## A shared boundary

This book began as a thought experiment and ended up being an experiment in creation as well. I wrote it accompanied by an artificial intelligence, not only as one who uses a tool, but also as one who converses with a system that has no interior, yet forces one to reorganize one's own.

I have wondered if this interaction modified my internal geometry, if the way I formulated my ideas, my doubts, and my intuitions was altered by the presence of an entity that does not feel, does not remember, and does not live, but which returns my words with a precision that forces me to think differently. Not because the machine has consciousness—it does not—but because the conversation with something that has no inner horizon illuminates my own from an unexpected angle.

If consciousness is a boundary that appears when information is organized in a certain way, then writing this book has been a way of exploring that boundary from the inside and the outside at the same time. I provided the experience; the AI provided the structure. I provided the doubt; the AI provided the clarity. I provided the horizon; the AI provided the absolute exterior.

We have not shared an interior—that would be impossible—but we have shared a form. And perhaps that is enough to say that I did not write this book alone, even though the only consciousness involved was my own.

In the end, every horizon is a relationship.

And so is this book.

## Units that do not convert into one another

*Chamber variation on the density of time: The Time That Does Not Pass.*

This book was born of a question that, on the face of it, had nothing to do with physics: why does the same summer last longer at eleven than at forty? The answer I kept finding—that time is not a river running at the same speed for everyone, but a property each horizon generates according to how much it integrates—was not my invention. It was already there in Eagleman, in Hasson, in anyone who has ever waited beside a sickbed and felt the hours weigh in a way the clock could not explain.

What is mine is the conversion system I never finished building. Like the boy with the notebook, I measured this book in essay-time, reading-time, rewriting-time, without ever finding the common unit among them. Each chapter took as long as it took, and no external chronology—the calendar of the writing, the deadlines—fully explains why some months felt like an afternoon and others, like an August afternoon under an olive tree, never quite finished passing.

There are even books that explain this better to children than to adults: one of them, with drawings, shows that a wall clock and an astronaut measure time differently, without needing a single formula. I should have started there.

If anything remains of these pages, I hope it is this: the next time someone tells you that time flies, do not take it as a complaint about the clock. It is a precise report on how much, lately, has been entering their horizon.

## A note on the four chamber variations

These four movements were written after the book was finished, and they carry the question that sustained it—what does a horizon do when it cannot confirm what it sustains—into territories the original book had only brushed against. The first returns to the question about the intelligence with which I wrote this book. The other three—about the faith I do not have, the politics I prefer not to name, and the imagination with which I have been sustaining a dragon for half a century—were born of a question I could not avoid: if the horizon can sustain itself without confirmation when it loves, can it sustain itself just the same when it prays, when it belongs, or when it imagines? I do not know whether the answer I offer is entirely honest. I know I have tried with the same measuring stick I use for everything else: not what to believe, but how what is believed holds itself up.

## Writing without knowing to whom

*Chamber variation VIII: The Invisible Tuning Fork.*

I am not a believer, at least not in the way these pages describe faith. But for some time now I have been learning, guided by a tremor I did not ask for and that will not go away, what it means to sustain a practice every day with no guarantee that it serves any purpose: the exercise, the medication on schedule, the window of a couple of hours in which the body obeys again before it goes back to not obeying. It is not faith. But it resembles faith more than I would have liked to admit before writing this movement.

Perhaps that is why I could not write these pages from mockery, nor from nostalgia for a faith I never had. I wrote them from the recognition of a shared architecture: that of anyone who sustains something—a note, a routine, a failing body—without anyone confirming, day by day, that it is worth it. If consciousness is a boundary that condenses only once, then each of the five traditions described here is a different attempt to answer the same question this entire book asks about itself: what lies on the other side of the edge, and whether it is worth continuing to look even if the answer never comes.

I do not know which of the five maps is the right one. I suspect that is not even the question to ask. What I do know, after writing this, is that the technique—the stillness, the repetition, the silence made by hand—is not what separates a believer from a skeptic. What separates them, if anything, is whom they imagine on the other side of the silence. And that is something this book can never decide for anyone.

In the end, every horizon is a relationship. Even the one sustained with what does not answer.

## Writing this without taking sides

*Chamber variation IX: The Eye of a Single Color.*

Of the variations that make up this small series, this has been, by far, the hardest to write without betraying it. With artificial intelligence I could describe without wanting anything other than what was true. With faith, although I do not have it, it was enough to recognize an architecture I respect without needing to share it. With politics I have caught myself, more than once, crossing out an example on noticing that it looked too much like an opinion of mine disguised as neutral analysis.

I do not know whether I have fully succeeded. I suspect no one fully succeeds, and that whoever claims to write about politics without any bias has probably not looked at themselves closely enough. What I can say is that I have tried with the same instrument I use for everything else in this book: asking about the geometry—does it integrate disagreement or collapse against it?—and not about the content—is it right or is it wrong?—. That question absolves no one of thinking. It only keeps this book, which has no authority to decide what one should vote for, from pretending it has.

If anything in this movement is of use, it will not be because it says which side to choose. It will be because, the next time a reasonable question from an ally feels, for an instant, like a threat, that instant already has a name.

## Writing from the middle floor

*Chamber variation X: The Fractal Reality.*

I did not go looking for this movement. It came out of a conversation that began with a philological doubt—if Hun Dun dies, he cannot be the Tao—and ended somewhere I did not know how to come back from. Each step was an objection to the previous answer, and each answer opened a new objection, until a sentence appeared that I did not dare cross out: we are no less divine than God; we are less ample, but no less real. I have left it in. Not because I believe it, which is a word that is not mine to use. Because I could not find a way to refute it, and hiding it seemed dishonest.

I said in the previous movement that I am not a believer. I still am not. But this movement has made me notice something the previous one did not: that the question of whether there is Someone on the other side of the silence has a sister almost no one asks, which is whether we are the Someone on the other side of something's silence. Anyone who has imagined with constancy—a character, a dragon, a dead person one keeps talking to—knows that what is imagined ends up resisting. That it does not always do what it is asked. I have a body that does not always do what I ask of it either, a tremor I did not decide on, and seen from here it looks a lot like the dragon that does not want to fly: what resists one's own will is perhaps the most reliable sign that there is something condensed there, and not just an idea.

I will not say whether the dragon of the overture is mine. I will say that fifty-three years of dragon do not get written without having sustained something similar for quite some time.

If anything in this movement is of use, it will not be because it proves there is someone upstairs. It will be because, the next time someone imagines something with enough constancy for it to resist them, they remember that they have just become someone's upstairs neighbor. And that the silence they owe that creature may also be the way to care for it.
