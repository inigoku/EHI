## [ILUSTRACIÓN 10: "The horizon in bond"]
*Two bubbles touching. At the point of contact, the skin becomes transparent. Inside each bubble, distinct landscapes that overlap in the contact zone. Particles of light traveling between them without crossing space.*

---

There is an experience almost everyone has had and very few have known how to articulate. It happens in conversations you don't want to end. On afternoons with someone you haven't seen in years. In the moment you look at the clock and three hours have passed without either person checking their phone. Time hasn't disappeared—it has changed density. More has happened per unit of hour than what usually happens.

The opposite experience also exists: a meeting that drags on without purpose. A wait with no information. Time doesn't accelerate—it thickens. Every minute has the weight of ten.

Same clock. Radically different experience.

> **In physics this is called:** the perception of time is not derived from physical time: it is an emergent property of the perceiving system.  
> **In daily life it is like:** two people watching the same movie: one says "it dragged on," the other "it flew by."

---

### Time as a property of the horizon

If consciousness has the structure of a horizon, its time is an internal property—not an external parameter that the horizon registers, but a consequence of how it integrates. The system's integration rate—how much new information is incorporated per unit of chronological time—determines the density of subjective time.

A four-year-old child integrates at a very high rate: every input reconfigures the system, every experience is new, the horizon is in constant expansion. That is why a childhood summer afternoon has a density that an adult summer afternoon rarely reaches. Not because the child is happier—because they are processing more per unit of time.

> **In physics this is called:** integration rate determines temporal density.  
> **In daily life it is like:** a child seeing everything for the first time vs. an adult who has already seen everything.

Old age reverses the process: the horizon has highly consolidated patterns, few inputs generate new integration. The elderly are not lying when they say time passes faster. Per unit of chronological hour, less happens in the horizon.

> **In physics this is called:** decrease in integration rate due to pattern consolidation.  
> **In daily life it is like:** reading a book for the tenth time: the pages turn faster because you already know what happens.

Pain and boredom confirm the model from the other side. Pain overloads the system with a signal that can neither be processed nor ignored—integration collapses upon itself in a loop that doesn't advance. Boredom occurs when the system does not receive enough input to sustain integration—the horizon spins in the void. In both cases, chronological and subjective time decouple dramatically.

> **In physics this is called:** temporal decoupling due to system overload or underload.  
> **In daily life it is like:** a traffic jam: the hands of the clock move forward, but you aren't getting anywhere.

**Flow**—the state that Csikszentmihalyi documented in artists, athletes, and surgeons—is the opposite case. The integration rate is maximal: the system absorbs and processes without friction. And time disappears.

> **In physics this is called:** flow regime: maximum Phi without resistance.  
> **In daily life it is like:** a river flowing downhill: you go fast, you feel no effort, and when you look back you don't know how far you've traveled.

### The brain as a butterfly

David Eagleman, a neuroscientist at Stanford University, proposed an image that illuminates all of this. The brain, he said, is like a butterfly trapped in glass: it lives in a world of its own creation, generating internal time from the information it processes. When something new happens, the brain "writes" more memory per unit of real time. When remembering, it reads that memory: more details per unit of time equals a sensation of longer duration.

This explains the "tower effect": in free-fall accidents, people report that time slows down dramatically. Eagleman tested this directly: he had volunteers drop from a height of 30 meters with a device on their wrist that flashed numbers too fast to read under normal conditions. If time actually slowed down, they would be able to read them. They couldn't. What happens is that extreme fear generates a much higher memory density: when remembered, the fall seems longer because there are more "frames" per second.

> **In physics this is called:** mnemonic encoding density determines retrospective temporal density.  
> **In daily life it is like:** a slow-motion movie: it didn't slow down when recorded, but when played back with more frames it seems slower.

### The chemistry of time

The integration rate is regulated, minute by minute, by neurotransmitters:

| Molecule | Effect on time | Analogy |
|----------|----------------|---------|
| **Adrenaline** | Maximum emergency integration. Dense time, but due to threat. | The accelerator of fear. |
| **Noradrenaline** | Excitement and novelty. The other is a new, unpredictable stimulus. | Early infatuation: everything is a first time. |
| **Dopamine** | Anticipation, not pleasure. Calibrates the internal vs. external clock. | The central pacemaker: "is this worth it?" |
| **Oxytocin** | Social calm. The horizon stops being on guard. | The social handbrake: "I am safe with this other." |
| **Cortisol** | Chronic stress. Degrades the capacity for resonance. | The noise that drowns out the music. |

> **[SIDEBOX: The Parkinson's of time]**
>
> Parkinson's disease progressively destroys the dopaminergic neurons of the substantia nigra. The best-known result is bradykinesia: slow movements, sometimes almost impossible.
>
> What almost no one mentions—and patients describe with disconcerting consistency—is that from the inside, this slowness is not perceived. The patient who takes three minutes to cross a room does not feel like they are taking three minutes. They feel like they are walking at a normal pace. Their horizon generates movement at the usual speed. The body does not execute what the horizon commands.
>
> Treatment with levodopa restores the calibration almost immediately, even before movement improves. Time synchronizes before the body does.
>
> **In physics this is called:** dopamine as the calibrator of the internal clock.  
> **In daily life it is like:** a clock that runs fast: you think you're late, but the clock is broken.

### Memory as the constructor of time

We do not remember time as we lived it. We reconstruct it. Every time we remember an event, the brain rewrites it: it adds details that weren't there, omits others that were, colors the memory with the present emotional state. Memory is not an archive: it is a narrative in constant revision.

This has profound consequences for the perception of time. Two people who lived the same event—a trip, an argument, a rainy afternoon—can remember it with radically different durations. Not because one is lying: because their horizons integrated the event differently, and upon reconstructing it, they generate distinct densities.

> **In physics this is called:** memory as a reconstructive, not reproductive, process.  
> **In daily life it is like:** two people telling the same story: one says "it lasted hours," the other "it was an instant." Neither is lying: both constructed their time.

There is a phenomenon that confirms this dramatically: Capgras syndrome. Patients visually recognize their loved ones—the face is identical—but feel they are impostors. There is no affect associated with the recognition. The circuit connecting visual perception with emotional memory is damaged. The consequence is disturbing: for these patients, time with their loved ones ceases to have density. Hours pass without anything "happening," because emotional integration—the part that gives weight to time—is absent.

> **In physics this is called:** disconnection between the recognition circuit and the affective valuation circuit.  
> **In daily life it is like:** watching a movie in silence: the images are there, but there is no soundtrack to give them emotion.

### Time in chronic pain

Acute pain contracts time: every second expands to fill consciousness. But chronic pain does something more insidious. After months or years of constant pain, the system stops registering it as novelty. The pain becomes background, a baseline. And with it, time flattens. Days become interchangeable. Morning is the same as afternoon. Tuesday is the same as Thursday.

> **In physics this is called:** habituation: the system stops responding to constant stimuli because they provide no new information.  
> **In daily life it is like:** living next to a highway: at first you hear every car, but after a month the noise disappears—even though it is still there.

Patients with chronic pain describe this with a precision that physics should take seriously: "it's not that time passes slowly or quickly—it's that it stops passing." The horizon, saturated by a signal it can neither process nor ignore, stops generating the novelty that gives density to time. The result is a kind of temporal death: the person is still alive, but time has stopped happening.

> **In physics this is called:** collapse of the integration rate due to channel saturation.  
> **In daily life it is like:** a radio tuned to a single frequency: it keeps broadcasting, but there is no more music, only a continuous tone.

### The system of two

When two people hold a face-to-face conversation, their patterns of brain activity converge. The **neural coupling** documented by Uri Hasson at Princeton shows that the listener's brain doesn't just process what the speaker says—it anticipates their activation patterns. In pairs with greater mutual understanding, the listener's activity *precedes* the speaker's by tenths of a second. It doesn't follow—it anticipates.

> **In physics this is called:** predictive causal coupling between complex systems.  
> **In daily life it is like:** dancing with someone who knows your steps: they don't react to what you do—they already knew it before you did it.

When two strings vibrate at related frequencies, they produce harmonics that were in neither of the two. The two-string system has properties that the one-string system does not have.

The same happens with two horizons in sustained resonance. The system of two integrates more information than the sum of what each integrates separately. The Phi of the joint system would be greater than the sum of the individual Phis. Not because they are added together—because entanglement creates new integration channels that only exist in the relationship.

> **In physics this is called:** Phi of the joint system > sum of individual Phis.  
> **In daily life it is like:** two musicians improvising: what emerges is in neither of them separately.

If the integration rate determines the density of subjective time, and if the system of two integrates more than the sum of its parts, then the time of the bond is objectively denser than the time of either of the two alone.

Those three hours of conversation you don't want to end don't seem denser. They are denser.

### Time bases: The synchronization of internal clocks

In distributed systems engineering and telecommunications, two independent devices that need to coordinate cannot trust their internal clocks to run at exactly the same speed. Each microprocessor has its own **internal time base**—a local oscillator driven by a quartz crystal. Due to microscopic physical imperfections, these crystals suffer from clock drift. If two systems attempt to process or exchange data without synchronizing, their time bases misalign, corrupting the transmitted information.

To coordinate, the devices use an **external reference time base** (a master clock or central synchronization signal, like NTP or PTP protocols). The local oscillators continuously adjust to this external reference pulse, ensuring their internal times match in phase and frequency.

In the architecture of the human bond, an identical process occurs. Each brain possesses its own internal time base: neural oscillators and neurochemical dynamics that mark the beat of its individual subjective time. However, when two horizons interact closely, their internal time bases couple. To achieve this phase-locking, the systems use a common external reference time base: the physical presence of the other, the rhythm of breathing, the tone of voice, the pacing of conversational turns, or eye contact.

This external synchrony signal acts as the master clock of the relationship. Through it, the internal time bases adjust their speed of subjective integration. Time ceases to be an isolated experience: it unifies into a single shared temporal base.

When the physical channel is interrupted and the external reference time base disappears (in distancing or silence), the drift reappears. The internal time bases recover their divergent individual rhythms, and the time of both decouples once again.

### Why distance hurts physically

When two horizons have resonated for enough time, each begins to model the other with increasing detail. Their rhythms, their frequencies, their modes of response become part of the horizon's prediction system. When the bond is interrupted, the horizon that remains active has an internal structure expecting input that no longer arrives. The integration channels that opened in resonance remain open.

The sensation of absence is not the representation that someone is not there. It is the horizon processing the silence where there should be a signal.

> **In physics this is called:** null signal processing: the system continues operating the channels even though the input is zero.  
> **In daily life it is like:** a telephone ringing in an empty room: no one answers, but the ringer keeps vibrating.

Separated from the horizon with which it resonated, the time of the remaining horizon becomes slower, heavier, less dense. Not because there is less to do. Because there is less with whom to integrate.

> **In physics this is called:** post-resonance temporal asymmetry.  
> **In daily life it is like:** a clock that keeps ticking but makes no sense: the hours pass, but nothing happens in them.

### The topology of the bond: Messages and design relationships

If the self and the other are encapsulated domains interacting through their horizons, the internal structure and temporality of their connection can be modeled on the design relationships in software engineering and object-oriented programming (OOP).

#### Message Passing
In the original conception of OOP (formulated by Alan Kay), the focus does not lie on data structure, but on communication. Two encapsulated objects never access each other's internal memory; they do not share `private` variables. They communicate exclusively by sending each other **messages**.
In human interaction, message passing is the only available pathway. We never have direct access to the neural states of the other (there is no direct memory reading). We send messages (words, gestures, silences) that cross the physical channel and impact the other's public interface. It is the receiver who, internally, executes their private functions to interpret the message and update their own state.

#### Coupling Relationships: Association, Aggregation, and Composition
In the modeling of complex systems, not all connections are equal; they describe different lifecycle dependencies and couplings:

*   **Association**: The most basic connection. Two independent systems simply know each other and keep a mutual reference. A circumstantial relationship is an association: the objects interact for specific tasks, but their lifecycles are autonomous and do not suffer upon disconnection from the other.
*   **Aggregation**: A whole-part relationship where the components maintain independent lifecycles. If the "whole" dissolves, the parts survive intact. A group of friends is an aggregation: if the group structure ceases to exist, each individual (the part) continues to operate normally and can associate with other systems.
*   **Composition**: The strongest relationship of belonging. The lifecycle of the parts is inextricably linked to the lifecycle of the whole. If the "whole" is destroyed, the parts are destroyed with it. In a deep symbiotic or codependent bond, the relationship becomes composition. If the bond breaks, the internal structure of the individual horizons collapses, as their state invariants depended on the existence of the joint system.

#### Inheritance and Overrides
**Inheritance** is an *is-a* relationship. An object inherits the methods and data structure defined in its base class, being able to use or modify them.
In the construction of identity, inheritance represents familial and cultural legacy. We are born with base classes loaded with default event handlers (attachment patterns, automatic responses to threats). Personal development and maturity do not consist of programming an operating system from scratch, but of applying selective *overrides* to inherited methods, redefining behavior without breaking the support structure.

#### Synchrony vs. Asynchrony
The speed and density of the time of the bond are conditioned by the communication protocol used:

*   **Synchronous Communication**: It is a blocking call. The sender sends a message and halts its own processing (blocks), waiting for the receiver's response. An intense face-to-face conversation is synchronous: both systems enter a closed loop in real time, mutually blocked on the other's response. It is the only protocol capable of generating phase-locking and synchronization of time bases.
*   **Asynchronous Communication**: It is a non-blocking call. The sender sends a message (a letter, a text message) and continues with its immediate tasks without waiting for confirmation. The receiver will process the message when its CPU cycles are available. It allows for efficient temporal decoupling, but lacks the resonance and temporal density of synchronous coupling.

### Social time

Time is not only individual: it is collective. Humans are the only species that synchronizes its internal rhythms on a massive scale. Clocks, schedules, calendars, time zones—these are technologies of temporal coordination that allow millions of individual horizons to operate as a larger system.

But this synchronization has a cost. Social time is an average: lunchtime is at two because the majority can eat at two, not because you are hungry at two. Working hours are nine to five because productive coordination requires it, not because your body functions better during those hours. The result is a chronic mismatch between internal time and external time that neuroscientists call "social jet lag."

> **In physics this is called:** phase forcing: an oscillating system forced to synchronize with a frequency that is not its own.  
> **In daily life it is like:** dancing a choreography when your body wants to improvise: you follow the rhythm, but it is not your rhythm.

Studies show that this mismatch has measurable health consequences: higher risk of obesity, diabetes, depression, cardiovascular disease. Not because the work is hard: because the horizon is forced to integrate in a time that is not its own.
