## I. Why This Chapter Is Needed

The previous chapter built four bridges with almost notarial care: in each one we pointed out where established physics ends and where the hypothesis begins. That honesty has a price, and it must be paid here. An attentive reader can accept each bridge separately and ask, at the end, something very simple: all right, the nested Shadow and the disjoint Shadow are two different images—but what would I see, in the world, that would be different depending on which of the two were true? If the answer is "nothing, in principle, could distinguish them," then the entire apparatus, however elegant, is decorative: a showy way of saying the same thing we already knew, not a theory that risks anything.

This chapter does not resolve that question once and for all—no theory of consciousness does so yet—but it does do the minimum work that any proposal with scientific pretense must do: propose, for each bridge, a thought experiment that specifies which observation would confirm it and which would refute it. These are not experiments we can run tomorrow. They are, in the oldest sense of the word, *thought* experiments: devices to test an idea against its own consequences, before technology catches up to doing it for real.

The four experiments correspond, one by one, to the four bridges of the previous chapter, and it is worth stating it explicitly rather than letting the reader reconstruct it: condensation is tested with the topological twin experiment; evaporation, with measured evaporation; the filtering of the entanglement between Ego and Shadow, with distinguishable inundation; and the no-hair theorem of the Persona, with the person-without-a-black-hole. They are not of the same type, and it is also worth saying: the first is fundamentally computational—requiring running an already existing method on data that today we are barely beginning to be able to collect—; the second and third are observational, of the type that neuroimaging could in principle address with finer instrumentation than today's; the fourth is, of the four, the most philosophical, closer to a logical existence proof than to a laboratory protocol.

## II. First Experiment: The Topological Twin

**The question it tests:** whether the Shadow is a nested horizon within the Ego or a disjoint complex neighboring it—the problem that Chapter 23 resolved by bet, not by deduction.

**The experiment.** Integrated Information Theory already has an operational method, though computationally expensive, to find the maximum Phi of a system: all possible partitions of that system into subsets are tested, it is measured how much causal information is lost by treating each partition as if its parts were independent, and the partition that loses the least—the "minimum cut"—is identified as the actual boundary of the integrated system. Let us apply that method, at least on paper, to a subject during a process of psychotherapeutic integration of repressed material—a Shadow that begins to manifest more frequently in dreams, slips of the tongue, symptoms.

If the Shadow were genuinely nested within the Ego, the method should find, at all times, a single maximal complex that includes both: any partition trying to separate "Ego" from "Shadow" would lose more information than it would gain, because they would be merged into a single causal structure. If the Shadow is, as Chapter 23 proposes, a disjoint complex, the method should find two local maxima of integration—two complexes, each with its own Phi, each maximal *within its own partition*—joined by a measurable but not dominant channel of information.

It is worth saying, without beating around the bush, that this experiment uses IIT as an operational tool to locate the integration boundary, and not as a closed verdict on the nature of consciousness. The theory itself drags serious debates—the computational viability of calculating Phi with accuracy in large systems, and an unresolved discussion within IIT itself about its exclusion postulate—so any result obtained with this method should be interpreted keeping them in mind, not as if the method were neutral.

**What would distinguish one result from the other.** In the nested case, any therapeutic intervention that increases communication between the conscious and the repressed should, according to the model, *increase* the Phi of a single growing complex—a single rising curve. In the disjoint case, the same intervention should show up as two curves of Phi that remain as separate local maxima while, in parallel, the mutual information *between* them increases—two peaks that approach without merging. The difference is not subtle: one model predicts a single growing crest; the other, two crests that communicate better and better without ceasing to be two. With sufficiently fine neuroimaging instrumentation—we do not have it today, but the criterion can already be written—this would be, in principle, observable.

## III. Second Experiment: Measured Evaporation

**The question it tests:** whether the dissolution of the Ego evaporates towards the Reservoir—towards a common background with which it remains correlated—or towards a vacuum without further ado, leaving no structured trace.

**The experiment.** Let us compare two processes of dissolution of the sense of self: one induced abruptly and traumatically (an acute dissociation), and another cultivated gradually and sustainedly (decades of advanced contemplative practice). The model of "evaporation into the vacuum" predicts that both processes should converge, in their final phase, towards the same state: absence of structure, noise, a flat drop in any measure of integration. The model of "evaporation towards the Reservoir" predicts something different and more specific: in the gradual process, the drop in the Phi of the individual complex should be accompanied by a measurable *increase* in the mutual information between that subject and their environment—greater physiological synchrony with other people present, greater correlation between their neural activity and environmental stimuli that they previously processed as completely external. Not disappearance, but redistribution: what the individual loses as their own Phi should reappear, at least in part, as information shared with the background.

**What would distinguish one result from the other.** If both types of dissolution—the traumatic and the contemplative—show the same flat drop without compensatory gain anywhere else in the system, the Reservoir model loses its distinctive prediction and is reduced, honestly, to poetry. If, on the other hand, the contemplative dissolution shows that transfer—less integration of its own, more correlation with the environment— and the traumatic does not, Chapter 23 would have something that no model of "vacuum without further ado" would predict with the same precision: two ways of losing closure, distinguishable not by how they feel but by where the lost information goes.

For the comparison to be clean, the two groups would have to be matched by age, by initial integration level, and by total duration of the process, so that the only variable left free is the type of dissolution. And the operational definition of the Reservoir as the complement of the maximal closures also imposes where to look, not just what to look for: if R is, by construction, everything that is not already closed in X, the redistribution should appear specifically in the immediate environment of the subject—the physiological synchrony with other people present during the process—, not in any corner of the system chosen at random. The critical variable is not the speed with which the own Phi drops, which can be similar in both cases, but the presence or absence of that specific gain in the immediate environment.

## IV. Third Experiment: Distinguishable Inundation

**The question it tests:** whether psychosis, as we described it at the end of the previous chapter, is a specific failure of filtering towards the Reservoir, and not simply a general deterioration of cognitive function.

**The experiment.** If the model is correct, psychosis should have a different signature than other states of severe cognitive impairment with which it is sometimes superficially confused—an advanced dementia, for example, or severe intoxication. The concrete prediction: in psychosis, the measure of mutual information between the subject and their environment—or between different regions of their own neural activity that normally remain segregated—should *increase* anomalously right before or during episodes of hallucination, even while the Phi of the central complex, the one sustaining the coherent narrative identity, *drops*. That is: less integration of its own, more indiscriminate entanglement with everything else, occurring at the same time. In a dementia due to general deterioration, on the other hand, the model predicts that both quantities should drop together: less Phi of its own, and also less correlation with the environment, because there is no filtering failure but a general loss of processing capacity.

**What would distinguish one result from the other.** This is, of the four experiments, the riskiest prediction, because it goes against the intuition that "more symptoms" should always mean "less of everything." If it were observed that acute psychotic episodes show that specific pattern—a rise in indiscriminate correlation with the environment simultaneous with the drop in central Phi, rather than a general drop in both—it would be a real and surprising confirmation. If, on the other hand, psychosis turns out to be indistinguishable, in this profile, from any other severe cognitive impairment, the model of "inundation from the Reservoir" loses much of its explanatory power and we would have to admit that psychosis, at least in this framework, is nothing but noise.

It is also, for that very reason, the experiment that would best distinguish this model from the simplest of its rivals: no model that treats psychosis as a mere general deterioration of the integrating function would predict that specific signature—Phi dropping while correlation with the environment rises—, because a general deterioration predicts, without further ado, that everything drops together. It is the only one of the four tests where an obvious rival theory makes, on paper, a different and contrary prediction, and not simply a vaguer prediction.

## V. Fourth Experiment: The Person Without a Black Hole

**The question it tests:** whether the public interface of a person—what in previous chapters we called the Jungian Persona—systematically loses information about the internal state, in the same way that a black hole horizon loses all the biography of what fell inside and preserves only three numbers.

**The experiment.** If the analogy with the no-hair theorem is correct, it should be possible to prove, not just intuit, that two genuinely different internal states—two different configurations of Phi, two different histories of integration—can produce exactly the same observable interface: the same behavior, the same discourse, the same responses to a standard questionnaire. Formulated with the notation we already established for the total space X: for any finite set of observables O defined on the public interface—the Persona—, there should exist two states \(S_1, S_2 \in X\) with \(\Phi(S_1) \neq \Phi(S_2)\) but \(O(S_1) = O(S_2)\). Put another way: the projection of X onto the set of public observables is not injective. This formulation is more honest than asking for "indistinguishability in any battery of measurements," a criterion that no finite experiment could ever exhaust; asking that there exist at least one pair of states that collapse onto the same O, on the other hand, is a concrete claim about the structure of X that one can try to refute case by case.

**What would distinguish one result from the other.** This would be tested, in principle, by showing pairs of subjects with measurably different neural integration profiles—different patterns of Phi, different complex architectures—who nevertheless project to the same profile O in any available battery of purely behavioral or self-report measures. If such pairs \(S_1, S_2\) exist and can be documented, the Persona behaves, just as Chapter 23 predicts, as a horizon with its own no-hair theorem. If, on the other hand, every measurable internal difference in Phi always ends up manifesting, sooner or later, in some detectable difference in O, the analogy is weaker than the chapter suggests, and the Persona would be less a surface that forgets and more a surface that, simply, takes time to show everything.

## VI.

None of these four experiments can be run today with the precision they would need. But a thought experiment is not valuable for what it measures now, but for whether it gives a theory something to lose. These four give it that.

### Note to Chapter 24

**What we do know:** that integrated information theory already has an operational method—the search for the minimum partition cut—capable of generating, in principle, the curves of Phi that the first experiment needs; and that mutual information between systems is a measurable quantity with current neuroscience, though not with the resolution these experiments would require in practice.

**What we don't know:** whether any of the four experiments is technically viable with the instrumentation of the coming decades, or if it will remain a criterion of principle with no possible implementation; and whether a negative result in any of them should be taken as a refutation of the specific bridge it tests, or only as evidence that the translation between the physical and psychic formalism still needs more work before being truly put to the test.

**Remaining questions:** does any data already exist in the clinical or neuroimaging literature—even if it was not collected with this in mind—that can be reread under any of these four criteria? And which of the four experiments, if only one had to be chosen to truly try, offers the best relationship between what it would cost to do and what would be learned if it failed?

**If you only take away one idea:** a theory that cannot lose anything cannot teach anything either; these four experiments are the price of this book claiming to teach something.
