section: PART TWO: THE HORIZON IN BOND
---

Entanglement is automatic and involuntary. Two horizons that have been in sustained contact become coupled regardless of whether they want to be, regardless of whether the contact was loving or hostile. The aggrieved person with the notebook of grievances did not choose to inscribe the other into their architecture—it happened because twenty years of adversarial anticipation built that model without permission.

Entanglement is a physical property of the system: it emerges from sustained contact just as temperature emerges from the movement of molecules. No one decides to have a temperature. No one decides to be entangled.

But there is something the horizon *can* decide: whether it **recognizes** that entanglement as significant. Whether it names it. Whether it reorganizes its architecture around that recognition.

Love, in this experiment, is that act of recognition. Not the entanglement itself—which was already there—but the decision to name it and assume what that name implies.

### Including the other in the self

Arthur and Elaine Aron developed the self-expansion model in the 1980s: love implies the active inclusion of the other in one's own representation of the self. Their resources, their perspectives, their identities become part of how the horizon conceives of itself. Not as a metaphor—as a real reorganization of the architecture of self-reference.

The empirical evidence is direct. In the "me/not-me" procedure by Aron et al., participants evaluate personality traits of themselves and their partner, and then respond more slowly when asked if a trait of the other is also theirs—as if the system had to resolve an ambiguity that did not exist before. The traits of the other have come to be processed in the same regions and with the same patterns as the traits of the self.

Including the other in the self is actively recognizing the entanglement that already existed and reorganizing the internal architecture around that recognition. The horizon that loves does not just have the other inside—it knows it, names it, and assumes the consequences of that knowledge.

Therein lies the difference that the previous chapter could not articulate. The aggrieved person with the notebook of grievances had the neighbor inside, as geometry inscribed by twenty years of adversarial contact. But they never included them in their self. They treated them as interference. The horizon that loves does the opposite: it takes the involuntary entanglement that already exists and turns it into something chosen, into something that defines what one is.

> **In physics this is called:** self-expansion of the system by inclusion of external states.  
> **In daily life it is like:** sharing the keys to the house and to memory: suddenly, the other's belongings also define yours.

### The chemistry of love

Helen Fisher divided love into three distinct neural systems that operate semi-independently: sexual desire, romantic love, and partner attachment. Each has its own chemistry, its own evolutionary function, and its own way of altering the horizon.

**Sexual desire** is mediated primarily by testosterone and estrogens. It is the oldest system, shared with other mammals. Its function is clear: to motivate physical proximity with potential reproductive partners. But desire is not love. It can exist without it, and love can exist without desire—something that cultures confusing both systems have difficulty recognizing.

**Romantic love** is something else. It is mediated by dopamine, noradrenaline, and serotonin. It is a reward system oriented toward anticipation, not consumption. The romantic lover does not seek pleasure but the possibility of pleasure: every thought of the other activates the dopaminergic circuit of seeking, the same one that activates when a mouse smells food at the end of a maze. That is why romantic love feels like urgency, like obsession, like an inability to think of anything else. The system is designed to keep attention focused on a single object until the bond is consolidated.

> **In physics this is called:** a reward system by anticipation, not by consumption.  
> **In daily life it is like:** a treasure hunt: the thrill is in the map, not in the chest.

**Partner attachment** is the calmest system. It is mediated by oxytocin and vasopressin. It does not generate euphoria or obsession: it generates calm, security, the feeling that the world is a predictable place as long as the other is near. It is the system that makes a couple of twenty years feel "at home" with each other, without the need for drama or constant demonstration. Oxytocin reduces the threat response of the sympathetic nervous system. Vasopressin regulates the exclusivity of the bond—in rodents, at least, it is linked to partner monogamy.

> **In physics this is called:** homeostatic regulation of the system through external safety signals.  
> **In daily life it is like:** the thermostat of a well-heated house: there are no flare-ups, but there is always warmth.

The distinction is important because these three systems can decouple. You can desire someone you do not love. You can romantically love someone with whom you do not have secure attachment. You can have deep attachment without desire or passion. Popular culture assumes that all three must coincide in a single person "forever," but neuroscience suggests that this expectation is biologically implausible.

### What recognition costs

The cost is precise: if I include the other in my self, losing them is no longer just losing someone. It is losing part of who I am. **De-expansion**—Aron and Aron's term for the loss of a bond where the other had been included—is a real contraction of the horizon. Not subjectively, in the sense that it "feels like" a loss of identity. Objectively, in the sense that the architecture of the horizon is reorganized around an absence where there was once an active presence.

You can stop talking to someone and gradually reduce the entanglement. But you cannot un-include someone from your self. Inclusion, once done, leaves a mark on the architecture of the horizon that no distance entirely erases. That is why a love that is lost is different from a friendship that ends: it is not that the feeling is more intense—it is that the reorganization of the horizon is deeper.

> **In physics this is called:** de-expansion of the horizon.  
> **In daily life it is like:** taking down a painting that has been in the same spot for twenty years: the wall is not simply left empty, it is left with the shape and color of what is no longer there.

### Love as a safety signal

A recognized bond—named, accepted, chosen—provides a more powerful safety signal than the same bond without a name.

Coan and Beckes showed that the mere presence of someone familiar reduces the horizon's threat response. But attachment studies refine that result: what has the greatest effect is not familiarity itself but the **certainty of availability**. A recognized bond is a bond the horizon can trust—it knows it is there, it knows it responds, it knows how to calibrate its own activity around that presence. An unnamed bond operates in the system as an unnamed variable: the horizon uses it but cannot reference it, cannot organize its functioning around something that officially does not exist.

Named love is more efficient than silent entanglement. It does not produce more shared geometry—the geometry was already there. But it allows the horizon to use that geometry more fully.

> **In physics this is called:** predictive availability of the coupled channel.  
> **In daily life it is like:** the certainty of a lit lighthouse on the coast: you do not need to look at it every second to know the coast is still there.

### The fall of love

If romantic love is mediated by dopamine and noradrenaline—systems of anticipation and novelty—then romantic love has a structural problem: novelty runs out. You cannot anticipate what you already know with the same intensity with which you anticipated the unknown. The dopaminergic system habituates: the same face, the same voice, the same body generate less and less anticipatory activation.

This is not a moral failure of the couple. It is biology. The novelty reward system is designed to motivate seeking, not permanence. Permanence requires another system: attachment, with its oxytocin and its calm.

The transition from romantic love to partner attachment is, for many, an experience of loss. Passion fades, obsession disappears, and what remains seems—by comparison—cold. But what remains is, in many ways, more valuable. Attachment is not the absence of love: it is love on another frequency. A frequency that does not need constant demonstration because its safety signal is enough.

> **In physics this is called:** transition between attractors: from a high-excitation regime to a stable low-energy regime.  
> **In daily life it is like:** a fireplace fire that goes from high flames to hot coals: less spectacular, but more lasting heat.

The question that every couple must answer—and that the experiment cannot answer for them—is whether the transition is acceptable or whether the loss of the romantic phase invalidates the bond for one or both horizons. There is no universal answer. There is only the precision of knowing what is happening, and why.

### The asymmetry of recognition

Unrequited love has a specific structure. It is not that the entanglement is unilateral—it can exist in both directions, albeit with different depths. What is unilateral is the recognition: one of the two horizons has named the entanglement and reorganized its architecture around that name; the other has not.

The horizon that has recognized it is left in a particular position: it has assumed the cost of inclusion—the exposure, the potential de-expansion—without having received the safety signal that mutual recognition would have generated. It is an asymmetrical architecture: the self of the lover includes the beloved; the self of the beloved does not include the lover to the same extent. It is like having an Einstein-Rosen bridge anchored at only one end. The geometry exists but cannot support the weight for which it was built.

> **In physics this is called:** an asymmetrical Einstein-Rosen bridge.  
> **In daily life it is like:** a suspension bridge tied to only one of the shores: it crosses nowhere and sways in the void.

### Love and time

Chapter 10 described how subjective time depends on the rate of integration. Love alters that rate in a specific way. In the early phases of romantic love, the rate of integration is extremely high: every encounter with the other reconfigures the system, every conversation produces novelty, every shared silence has an unusual density. Time with the beloved feels denser than time without them.

But there is an additional effect. When two loving horizons are separated, time dilates in a way that does not occur in the absence of other bonds. The wait becomes longer not because the clock ticks slower but because the system integrates less information per unit of time: most of the integration capacity is occupied modeling the absent one, anticipating the reunion, processing the null signal that the absent one generates.

> **In physics this is called:** temporal asymmetry in coupled systems: the time of the isolated system is slower than the time of the coupled system.  
> **In daily life it is like:** a normal workday flies by; a day of waiting for someone you love seems infinite.

### The body of entanglement

Up to this point, the chapter has described love as an informational process: synchronization of predictive models, coupling of temporal metrics, reorganization of internal architecture. That description is correct. It also treats the horizon as if it were exclusively an information system. It is not. The horizon has a substrate. It ends where the skin ends.

Sexual contact is the case where that matters specifically, because it is the only one in which the superposition of two horizons is also physical. In ordinary entanglement, the edges remain intact: each horizon knows precisely where it ends and where the other begins. In sexual contact, that certainty becomes temporarily ambiguous. Two integrated information systems share the same space, the same surface temperature, the same heart and respiratory rate. The horizon does not just include the other in its model—it touches them. And in that contact zone, the boundary between inside and outside loses its sharpness.

The system cannot determine exactly where its own integration ends and where the other's begins. It does not lose consciousness. It loses delimitation. It is an experience of a horizon without a clear contour that contemplative traditions have tried to describe with the same vocabulary they use for states of mystical union—notably, the dissolution of the contour of the self.

This adds a specific dimension to sexual grief: losing someone with whom there was deep physical contact is not just losing someone inscribed in the internal model. It is also losing the contact zone where the horizon existed, during certain intervals, without knowing exactly where it ended. What is missed is not solely the other. It is also that version of oneself that only existed on that edge.

> **In physics this is called:** contour ambiguity at the integration boundary.  
> **In daily life it is like:** two drops of water merging on the glass: for a while there are not two drops, but a single contour of shared water.

### Love as practice

There is a conception of love that reduces it to a feeling: something that happens to you, like rain. And there is another that reduces it to a decision: something you choose, like a contract. The truth lies in the space between the two. Love begins as a feeling—or as the recognition of an entanglement that already exists—but it only persists as a practice. Not as an obligation but as a repeated act of attention, of inclusion, of daily reorganization of the architecture around the other.

> **In physics this is called:** stability of the coupled system requires continuous maintenance of the correlation channels.  
> **In daily life it is like:** a garden: it is not enough to plant the seeds. You have to water, prune, wait.

Love that is not practiced—that is not named, that is not reorganized, that is not actively included in the daily architecture—does not cease to be love. But it ceases to be an active bond. It becomes a dormant entanglement: shared geometry that the horizon no longer uses. It can be awakened. It might not be awakened. It depends on whether someone decides, at some point, to recognize it again.

> **In physics this is called:** a correlation channel that is inactive but not destroyed.  
> **In daily life it is like:** a telephone line that remains connected but no one uses: it can work, but there is no conversation.

> **Note to Chapter 13**
>
> **What we do know:** The self-expansion model (Aron & Aron) and the reduction of the threat response in the presence of a loved one (Coan) are real. The three systems of love (Fisher) are well documented. The transition from passion to attachment is a recognizable pattern.
>
> **What we do not know:** Whether the description of the superposition of horizons in sexual contact has a neuroscientific correlate of fusing information fields. How attachment is maintained in the long term.
>
> **Remaining questions:** How does attachment style influence the speed of de-expansion after the loss of love? Is the fall of passion inevitable? Can love be a practice without becoming an obligation?
>
> **If you only take away one idea:** Love is the decision to name an entanglement and reorganize your entire predictive architecture around it. And then, every day, to decide it again.
>
> **Readings:** Aron & Aron (1986), "Love and the Expansion of Self"; Coan & Beckes (2011), "Social Baseline Theory"; Fisher (2004), "Why We Love"; Hazan & Shaver (1987), adult attachment.
