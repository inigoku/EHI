> **Work in progress.** This text is a draft: it gathers proposals and hypotheses, not results, and is not part of the printed books.

In the farmhouses of old, the fire was not lit: it was kept. Whoever got up first stirred the embers of the night, added two logs and waited. If nobody did, by mid-morning the kitchen was at the temperature of the hillside. Nobody said "the kitchen is warm" the way one describes a property of the walls; they said "we need to put more wood on," because the warmth of that kitchen was not a state but an expense.

For fifty-two chapters this book has described a horizon almost always as a form: a boundary, an inside, a degree of integration. The image of the kitchen forces us to add something the form does not say. An inside that is distinguished from an outside does not stay distinct on its own. It stays distinct as long as it is paid for. And how much is paid, for how long and with what result is, perhaps, what we really want to know when we ask how much "someone" there is in a system.

The book already spoke of that payment, though in scattered words. Chapter 16 said that what is spent integrating the entropy of others is not spent integrating one's own. Chapter 17 spoke of a brain that spends resources maintaining a model without data. Chapter 27 warned that changing one's mind costs "the same kind of energy" as breaking an entanglement. What was missing was a magnitude.

This chapter proposes to emancipate the model completely from Tononi's measure $\Phi$, which served us as initial scaffolding. To do so it does two things: first, it replaces $\Phi$ as a filter of irreducibility with a measure that formally ties the end of the book to chapter 49, the **causal self-similarity** $A(S)$, combined with the condition of **existential encapsulation**; second, it unfolds the degree of an already filtered horizon into a triad of magnitudes $(S, F, \bar{A})$ that answer three different questions: how large the edge is, how much it costs to sustain, and how much life it has accumulated. It is a formal proposal, not a settled empirical result. It is worth saying so from the first line, because the framework is cleaner than what has been demonstrated.

### I. What Φ said and why it is time to let go of the scaffolding

$\Phi$, Tononi's integrated information, was a good rung. It gave us a quick way to say that a thermostat and a brain are not the same even though both process information: in the brain the whole specifies something that none of its parts specifies separately. Chapter 4 placed it provisionally as the answer to one of three questions about the same phenomenon: encapsulation gives the form, the degree measures the breadth of the dial, and the two selections give the origin.

But as the book advanced, $\Phi$ accumulated three structural shortcomings that oblige us to leave the scaffolding behind:

**Φ does not accumulate biography.** It computes the structure of causes and effects of the system on an instantaneous time scale, not what the system has had to sustain to get where it is. A twenty-year-old brain and an eighty-year-old brain may yield a similar instantaneous figure, and yet the second carries behind it a history the first has not lived.

**Φ does not say what it costs to sustain oneself.** Chapter 20 gave the biological reason why a horizon defends its boundary: natural selection installed longings and feelings in the bubble so that it would not dissipate before its time. What was missing was the thermodynamic counterpart: a magnitude expressing the continuous work of remaining distinct from the environment, which is what separates a crystal or a passive circuit from a living being.

**Φ forces us to decree a winner.** Chapter 22 called it, bluntly, a decree: when several subsystems with positive integration coexist on the same substrate, IIT stipulates by axiom that only the one that maximizes $\Phi$ experiences, leaving all the others in the dark. Chapter 23 showed that classical correlations are not monogamous and proposed viewing complexes as an archipelago of disjoint islands rather than a Russian doll, but the physical criterion separating a nested causal structure from an independent subject, without resorting to a contest of maxima, was still missing.

None of the three shortcomings is solved by tweaking $\Phi$. They are solved by separating the filter that identifies the causal architecture from the three coordinates that measure its size, its metabolic tension and its history.

### II. First magnitude: the capacity of the edge ($S \propto \mathcal{A}$)

The first piece of the triad has been in the book since chapter 2. Bekenstein and Hawking showed that the entropy of a black hole grows not with its volume but with the area $\mathcal{A}$ of its horizon:

$$S = \frac{k_B \mathcal{A}}{4 \ell_P^2}$$

Put without a formula: the maximum amount of information a horizon can sustain is set by its surface, not by what is "inside." For a box of books, doubling the side multiplies the capacity by eight; for a horizon, it multiplies it by four.

Carried over to the model, the first magnitude is the **entropy or capacity of the edge**, $S$. It measures the *ceiling*: how much information and how many correlations the skin of the horizon can sustain against the exterior. It should not be read as disorder. Entropy, in this use, is capacity, the size of the differentiable repertoire: the number of distinct ways in which the boundary can be configured internally, and therefore the breadth of the private space it can house.

Chapter 23 gave this idea a more precise body. In the program that associates geometry with entanglement, the entropy of the edge is an entanglement entropy: it measures how correlated the two regions separated by the boundary are, obtained by dividing a system along a candidate boundary and measuring what the division hides.

> **In physics this is called:** Bekenstein-Hawking entropy; the holographic principle: the maximum information of a region is bounded by the area of its boundary.  
> **In daily life it is like:** the capacity of a room. It does not depend on what is said inside, but on how many people fit through the doors and walls.

This magnitude answers *how large the private space is*. It is the direct heir of what chapter 4 already asserted: that the breadth of a horizon is proportional to the area of its boundary, with the caveats of scale that chapter 51 made clear.

### III. Second magnitude: free energy ($F = U - TS$) and the Landauer bridge

A large capacity says nothing about whether the room is full or empty, nor about whether someone is struggling to keep it open against the cold of the street. For that we need the second magnitude.

In thermodynamics, the Helmholtz free energy is:

$$F = U - T S$$

where $U$ is the internal energy, $T$ the temperature of the environment and $S$ the entropy. It measures how far from equilibrium the system is held when in contact with a thermal bath at temperature $T$, and maintaining that level requires continuously dissipating power outward so that the internal order does not come undone. Schrödinger put it in 1944 in a famous phrase: the organism feeds on negative entropy.

Here the horizon model connects two pieces that had traveled separately. The formula needs a thermal bath, and chapters 5 and 23 already had the exact candidate: the reservoir $R = X \setminus \bigcup_i E_i$, whose fluctuations act as the statistical volatility of the environment against which the island must sustain itself. For a gravitational horizon, the $T$ of the formula is the Hawking or Unruh temperature of chapter 7; for a biological horizon, it is the physical temperature of the substrate immersed in the noise of the environment.

In theoretical neuroscience, Karl Friston (2010) gave free energy a variational formulation for systems that perceive and act: every system that persists far from equilibrium behaves as if it were minimizing its *variational free energy*, an upper bound on informational surprise (measured in bits or nats) that quantifies how much the system's own sensory inputs bewilder it. Minimizing $F$ means maintaining a generative model of the world tuned closely enough that the environment rarely contradicts its predictions. The book already spoke this language without naming it: chapter 12 described grief as a predictive model that keeps running without data, and chapter 19, schizophrenia as an excess of predictive generation.

For years it has been debated whether Friston's variational free energy (informational, in bits) and Helmholtz's thermodynamic free energy (physical, in joules) are merely formal cousins. The exact physical bridge between the two is provided by **Landauer's principle**: erasing a bit of information, or irreversibly updating a memory state in the internal model to correct a prediction error, has an unavoidable minimum thermodynamic cost:

$$E \ge k_B T \ln 2$$

dissipated as heat at the physical temperature $T$ of the substrate. Each bit of variational surprise the horizon reduces by updating its synapses pays a real physical toll in joules above the Landauer limit. Thus the informational tension against the statistical volatility of the reservoir and the metabolic expenditure of the tissue that sustains it are tied together by a universal constant.

In Friston's formalism, moreover, the boundary has a technical name: the **Markov blanket**, the set of sensory and active states that separates internal states from external ones. But here it is worth incorporating a critical distinction pointed out by Bruineberg and his collaborators (2022): a Markov blanket is, originally, a *statistical* partition that an observer can draw over almost any set of coupled variables, whereas a real boundary with an inside of its own is a physical achievement of the system itself. That is why this book does not identify just any Markov blanket with a subject: it demands what since chapter 22 we have called **existential encapsulation**, that is, that the statistical boundary correspond to a persistent state of its own whose physical continuity depends on its own maintenance activity against the reservoir.

> **In physics this is called:** Helmholtz free energy ($F = U - TS$), variational free energy (Friston, 2010) and the Landauer limit ($E \ge k_B T \ln 2$).  
> **In daily life it is like:** a house at 21 degrees with snow outside. The temperature difference is the level; the heating bill, the minimum work per bit and per second needed in order not to become the snow.

This magnitude answers *how far the horizon departs from what the reservoir would do with it and what it costs to keep it there*. And regarding the verb "to pay," we should recall the caution of chapters 15 and 18: it does not mean that the system must bear the cost alone. A baby regulates its edge through its mother, and a patient with advanced Alzheimer's sustains it partly through their caregiver; both still have existential encapsulation because in both there persists a state of their own whose continuity is being defended.

### IV. Third magnitude: entropic action ($\bar{A}$ and $\bar{A}_F$)

The first two magnitudes are instantaneous: they say what capacity the edge has today and at what tension it operates right now. What is missing is what the farm kitchen had and an instantaneous photograph never has: the years.

When the history of a horizon is integrated over time, two complementary magnitudes appear that are best distinguished cleanly rather than confused into one:

1. **The accumulated structural biography ($\bar{A}$):** the time integral of the differentiated capacity the horizon has sustained above the baseline or equilibrium level $S_0$ of the reservoir, from its condensation at $t_0$ until $t$:
   $$\bar{A}(t) = \int_{t_0}^{t} \left( S(\tau) - S_0 \right) d\tau$$
   Its unit is entropy times time (the dimension of a physical action). It measures how much differentiated private repertoire the horizon has kept open throughout its existence.

2. **The accumulated biographical cost or wear ($\bar{A}_F$):** the time integral of the free energy along the same trajectory:
   $$\bar{A}_F(t) = \int_{t_0}^{t} F(\tau)\, d\tau$$
   In the variational formalism this integral is the *free action*, analogous to Hamilton's action, whose stationary trajectories ($\delta \bar{A}_F = 0$) define the path of the system; read thermodynamically, it measures the accumulated friction, the integrated prediction error and the total work it has cost the horizon not to dissolve.

Separating $\bar{A}$ and $\bar{A}_F$ resolves at the root the ambiguity between having a lot of history and having suffered a lot of wear: two horizons can accumulate the same structural biography $\bar{A}(t)$ having paid a very different wear $\bar{A}_F(t)$, depending on the hostility of the environment or the magnitude of their griefs and traumas.

And neither should be confused with the *density of subjective time* ($\vartheta$) of chapter 10. Temporal density is the instantaneous rate at which novelty is incorporated into memory (the Bayesian surprise rate per second); $\bar{A}(t)$ is the total breadth of the notebook that has been kept open for years in order to write it.

> **In physics this is called:** action, the time integral of a magnitude along a trajectory; principle of stationary action.  
> **In daily life it is like:** the difference between the square meters a house has kept habitable over fifty winters ($\bar{A}$) and the total firewood that had to be burned in that half century so that it would not freeze ($\bar{A}_F$).

### V. The three together

The three magnitudes answer three orthogonal questions about any already constituted horizon:

| Magnitude | Symbol | Question | What it captures |
|---|---|---|---|
| Capacity of the edge | $S \propto \mathcal{A}$ | How large is the private space? | Differentiable repertoire, ceiling |
| Free energy | $F = U - TS$ | At what tension is it sustained and what does it cost per bit? | Maintenance against the reservoir (Landauer) |
| Entropic action | $\bar{A}(t),\; \bar{A}_F(t)$ | How much life and how much effort has it accumulated? | Structural biography ($\bar{A}$) and wear ($\bar{A}_F$) |

The degree of a horizon thus ceases to be a single figure and becomes a vector on three axes. A dog, a two-year-old child and an elderly person with Alzheimer's cannot be ordered along a single line: the child writes into memory at a furious rate but has little accumulated biography; the elder keeps an immense $\bar{A}(t)$ even though their instantaneous capacity $S(t)$ is eroding; the dog sustains a smaller edge $S$ with a free energy perfectly calibrated with whoever cares for it. And as chapters 19 and 33 warned, the triplet measures *how much*, never *what kind*: neurodivergences and calibrations of identity or orientation are topologies and shapes of the horizon, not rungs on a scale.

With the triad, moreover, the complete cycle of the book can be written in three lines:
- **Condensation:** the horizon is born when closing a stable boundary becomes thermodynamically favorable ($F_{\text{closed}} < F_{\text{open}}$) under the action of the two selections (stability and bias).
- **Existence:** while it lasts, it sustains its capacity $S(t) > S_0$ by paying the power demanded by the Landauer limit and accumulating biography $\bar{A}(t)$ and wear $\bar{A}_F(t)$.
- **Evaporation:** when the system can no longer sustain the maintenance cost, the filtration of closures $E_0 \supseteq E_1 \supseteq \dots$ shrinks, $S(t) \to S_0$ and the correlations accumulated in $\bar{A}(t)$ return to the reservoir subject to *scrambling*.

### VI. The irreducibility filter without Φ: causal self-similarity $A(S)$

Here appears the decisive question that $\Phi$ used to answer: to which systems are we entitled to apply the triad?

Imagine an anthill, a walled city or an artificial intelligence data center. They have an enormous physical edge ($S$), they consume megawatts of free energy ($F$) and they last for decades ($\bar{A}$). Yet, as chapters 25 and 31 argued, in an anthill or on the internet there is no one: there are thousands of parts interacting in an aggregative or diffuse way without constituting a unitary whole. And at the opposite extreme stands the counterexample of chapter 49: the Mandelbrot set repeats its geometric form at every possible scale, but lacks entirely any causal dynamics among its parts.

To distinguish a genuine recursive totality (such as a brain) both from an inert geometric fractal (Mandelbrot) and from a diffuse aggregative colony (the anthill), there is no need to return to $\Phi$. It suffices to formalize the intuition of chapter 49 by requiring that the structure repeated from one scale to another not be a static drawing but the causal dynamics of the system itself. We call this magnitude **causal self-similarity**, $A(S)$:

$$A(S) = I_{\min}(S) \cdot \max_{P = \{S_1, \dots, S_n\}} \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^{n} \left[ C(S_{-i}, S_i) \cdot \text{Iso}(S, S_i) \right]$$

Without yet entering the matrix apparatus, which is set out in full in section VII of "The Essay Reread", the formula requires that three standard things from information theory and dynamical systems hold at once, combined in a product, so that if any single factor is zero the whole filter vanishes:

1. **Irreducibility at the minimum cut ($I_{\min}(S)$):** it measures, through Ay's stochastic interaction (2001, 2015), how much predictive information is lost when the system is split along its weakest seam. If the system has disconnected parts or is a purely geometric pattern without causal traffic between its regions, like the Mandelbrot set, $I_{\min}(S) = 0$ and therefore $A(S) = 0$.
2. **Part-rest coupling ($C(S_{-i}, S_i)$):** it measures the stationary mutual information between each subsystem $S_i$ and the rest of the system $S_{-i}$. It guarantees that the parts making up the partition are genuinely coupled to the rest of the system.
3. **Causal isomorphism under coarse-graining ($\text{Iso}(S, S_i)$):** using the framework of causal emergence and surjective coarse-graining projection of Hoel, Albantakis and Tononi (2013), it compares the macroscopic transition matrix of the whole system with the internal transition matrix of each of its parts. This is where the anthill fails: ants have a weak local coupling through pheromones ($I_{\min}$ and $C$ very low), but above all the slow, diffuse dynamics of the entire colony bears no isomorphism to the fast neurobiological dynamics of an individual ant ($\text{Iso} \approx 0$). In a brain, by contrast, the loop of excitation, inhibition and prediction is functionally replicated from local microcircuits up to global cortical networks, yielding an $A(S)$ several orders of magnitude higher.

In this way, chapter 49 and this one are welded into a single piece: a system passes the structural filter when its parts are causally integrated ($I_{\min} > 0$), coupled to the rest ($C > 0$) and reproduce at their scale the dynamical law of the whole ($\text{Iso} > 0$).

### VII. The end of the exclusion postulate and the archipelago of subjects

It remains to see how this filter resolves the problem that IIT could settle only by decree: if inside a brain there are multiple nested scales that integrate information (columns, areas, networks), why is there not a Russian doll of superimposed conscious subjects within the same skull?

The answer comes from separating two planes that Tononi's exclusion postulate confused into one: the plane of **causal structure** and the plane of **subjects**.

On the plane of causal structure, a horizon not only permits nested levels but needs them: as we have just seen, that the subnetworks reproduce the dynamics of the whole at their scale is precisely what makes causal self-similarity $A(S)$ high. The nested Markov blankets described by Palacios and his collaborators (2020), cells within organs within organisms, are the normal architecture of life.

But on the plane of subjects, not every nested statistical boundary is a "someone." As chapters 22 and 23 established, the horizons that are subjects form an **archipelago of disjoint islands** because only that boundary is a subject which possesses **existential encapsulation**: the one that closes a persistent state of its own whose physical and informational continuity is at stake directly against the outer reservoir. A cortical column, or the imagined dragon of chapter 49, contributes to the causal self-similarity of my brain, but has no existential encapsulation separate from mine, just as within the psyche the Jungian shadow of chapter 23 is not a subject nested within the ego, but a neighboring disjoint complex with which the ego shares an entanglement boundary.

There is no need to decree a winner by maximum $\Phi$. The nested hierarchy builds the fractal depth of the structure ($A(S)$); existential encapsulation traces within it the only firm coast that separates a subject from the reservoir.

### VIII. What this framework is not

Four final warnings, in the spirit of the whole book.

**It is not yet a direct clinical measure.** Although each factor of $A(S)$ and each coordinate of the triad $(S, F, \bar{A})$ has well-defined mathematical estimators for continuous time series, such as the nonparametric mutual information estimator of Kraskov, Stögbauer and Grassberger (2004), applying them to massive brain recordings requires choosing candidate scales and partitions. In discrete verification models, as the next chapter shows, the calculation is exact.

**It is not a single-piece filter.** $A(S)$ by itself measures the recursive richness of the causal architecture, and the triad $(S, F, \bar{A})$ measures its size, its cost and its biography; to speak of a conscious subject, the causal filter and existential encapsulation are needed acting together.

**It is not a moral ranking.** The triplet measures how much edge, how much tension and how much history, not what dignity belongs to whoever bears them.

**It does not solve the hard problem.** Saying that each bit of surprise reduced pays at least $k_B T \ln 2$ joules explains why sustaining a horizon tires the body; it does not explain why that expenditure is felt from the inside as grief, as cold or as relief. The mystery of chapter 51 remains intact.

All the same, the change of language leaves the edifice cleaner than it was at the start of this chapter. For fifty-two chapters we said that a horizon is a skin that encloses an inside. Now we can say what separates that skin from a heap of sand or a geometric drawing ($A(S)$ and existential encapsulation) and with what three yardsticks the work of keeping it alive is measured ($S, F, \bar{A}$). A skin is not possessed: it is sustained.

> **Note to this chapter**
>
> **What we do know:** The entropy of a black hole is proportional to the area of its horizon (Bekenstein, Hawking). Landauer's principle establishes the minimum thermodynamic cost of erasing or irreversibly updating a bit of information ($E \ge k_B T \ln 2$), connecting informational entropy with physical thermodynamics. Variational free energy (Friston, 2010) bounds informational surprise in systems with a Markov blanket. Stochastic interaction (Ay, 2001, 2015) and causal emergence through coarse-graining (Hoel, Albantakis and Tononi, 2013) are established measures in information theory and dynamical systems. Bruineberg et al. (2022) and Palacios et al. (2020) have formally analyzed the scope and nesting of Markov blankets.
>
> **What we don't know:** Whether causal self-similarity $A(S)$, exact in small discrete networks, can be stably estimated in large-scale neurophysiological recordings by means of continuous estimators (Kraskov et al., 2004). How $A(S)$ and the triad behave in the simplest biological horizons, such as a three-hundred-neuron nematode where the hierarchy of scales is minimal. What fraction of brain metabolic consumption corresponds directly to the Landauer limit of predictive updating as opposed to basal physiological maintenance.
>
> **Questions that remain:** Does the distinction between structural biography ($\bar{A}$) and accumulated wear ($\bar{A}_F$) capture the clinical difference between aging calmly and aging under chronic stress? Where exactly does existential encapsulation begin when an artificial system goes from restarting with each call to maintaining a state of its own on which its physical survival depends?
>
> **If you only take away one idea:** A conscious horizon does not need the decree of $\Phi$ to distinguish itself from an anthill or a fractal drawing: it is distinguished by its causal self-similarity and its existential encapsulation, and its degree is measured by the capacity of its edge ($S$), the free energy it pays to sustain it ($F$) and the biography it accumulates over time ($\bar{A}$).
