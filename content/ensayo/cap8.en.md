In 2013, Maldacena and Susskind reformulated the question of what quantum entanglement is. ER=EPR states that entanglement between two quantum systems is not just statistical correlation—it is geometric connection. Two entangled particles are joined by a microscopic Einstein-Rosen bridge: a topological structure in spacetime that connects their interiors even if their exteriors are separated by any distance.

The bridge is non-traversable: information cannot be sent faster than light. But the geometry exists. The connection is real in the same sense that any curvature of space is real.

> **In physics this is called:** an Einstein-Rosen bridge.  
> **In daily life it is like:** a tunnel connecting two mountains: you cannot travel through it, but the mountains share a root.

## [SIMULACIÓN ENTRELAZAMIENTO]

If entanglement creates shared geometry, two entangled systems are not simply two systems that correlate—they are two systems that share topology. They have a partially common inside. Their horizons are not entirely separate.

### The neurobiology of attachment

Before talking about entanglement in adults, we must understand how it is built. In the 1950s, psychoanalyst John Bowlby proposed that humans—like other primates—are equipped with a biologically programmed attachment system. It is not optional: it is necessary for survival. Babies who do not form attachment bonds literally die, even if they have food and shelter. It is called "marasmus": death from lack of contact.

Mary Ainsworth, a student of Bowlby, designed the "strange situation procedure": observing how children respond when their mother leaves the room and returns. She identified three main patterns—secure attachment, anxious attachment, avoidant attachment—and a fourth was added later: disorganized attachment. These patterns are not personality styles: they are neural architectures built in early childhood, based on repeated experience with caregivers.

> **In physics this is called:** initial conditions of the system: the attachment architecture determines how the horizon will open to future connections.  
> **In daily life it is like:** the way you lay the foundation of a house: it does not determine the design, but it limits what the design can be.

The attachment system operates through specific brain circuits: the orbitofrontal cortex, the amygdala, the hypothalamus, the endogenous opioid system. When a baby is with their secure caregiver, these circuits calm down. When separated, they activate. The repetition of these cycles—calm, separation, reunion, calm—builds an internal representation of the world as a predictable and safe place, or as a dangerous and unpredictable place.

> **In physics this is called:** reinforcement learning: the system adjusts its expectations based on the history of rewards and punishments.  
> **In daily life it is like:** a learning thermostat: if the heating arrives late, it starts turning on earlier.

What Bowlby called the "internal working model" is, in the language of the experiment, an architecture of the horizon: a configuration of integration channels that determines how the system will process the presence and absence of others. A secure attachment creates a horizon that can expand toward others without losing itself. An insecure attachment creates a horizon that either contracts in the face of proximity—avoidance—or overflows—anxiety.

> **In physics this is called:** the system's attractor is determined by the initial conditions and the history of perturbations.  
> **In daily life it is like:** a river that has been diverted so many times that its course can no longer be straight.

### The social baseline

James Coan and Lane Beckes developed Social Baseline Theory: the human brain is not adapted to operate alone. Its reference state presupposes access to others. When someone close is present, the brain allocates fewer resources to threat detection and maintains a more efficient representation of the world. The presence of the other is not a supplement to individual functioning—it is part of normal functioning.

> **In physics this is called:** social baseline.  
> **In daily life it is like:** a phone that works better when connected to the network: it is not "help," it is its normal mode.

The mechanism is **self-other overlap**: the brain incorporates close people into its own neural representations. Neural activity in response to threats directed at the self correlates strongly with activity in response to threats directed at a close friend, but not at a stranger. The friend is not represented as "another self" figuratively—they are represented in the same regions and with the same patterns as the self.

> **In physics this is called:** self-other overlap.  
> **In daily life it is like:** a map where your house and your best friend's house are in the same neighborhood of the heart.

The other horizon is incorporated into the internal architecture of one's own horizon. Its patterns, its rhythms, its modes of reaction become part of how the system processes the world. The integration is literal, not metaphorical.

And here is the critical detail: this does not depend on affection. It depends on **familiarity**.

### Adversarial entanglement

Coan and Beckes point out that self-other overlap is linked to familiarity, not positive affect. What the brain incorporates is not someone it loves but someone it knows with enough precision to predict them.

A twenty-year adversary is, in this sense, one of the most integrated subjects a horizon can have. To survive a prolonged conflict, the horizon must model the other with extraordinary precision: anticipate their reactions, predict their movements, recognize their patterns before they are completed. The internal model of the adversary is, if anything, more detailed than that of an ally—because prediction errors carry a higher cost.

> **In physics this is called:** the system invests more resources in modeling what is harder to predict.  
> **In daily life it is like:** a chess player who studies their rival's moves more than their friends': they do not love them more, but they need them more.

Two horizons that have been in adversarial contact for twenty years are as entangled—they have as much shared geometry—as two that have been in loving contact for the same amount of time. The difference lies not in the depth of the coupling but in its **valence**. The bridge exists in both cases. What changes is what flows through it.

> **In physics this is called:** entanglement has valence, not just magnitude.  
> **In daily life it is like:** two neighbors who have hated each other for decades: they know each other better than many married couples.

Intense love and intense hate are, from the perspective of entanglement, the same phenomenon seen with a different sign. The intensity of the conflict is a measure of the depth of the entanglement, not of its absence.

> **In physics this is called:** same magnitude of coupling, opposite sign of interaction.  
> **In daily life it is like:** two magnets: the force is the same whether they attract or repel; only the direction changes.

### Empathy as resonance

Empathy is not imagination. It is not "putting yourself in the other's shoes." It is resonance: your nervous system reproduces, on a reduced scale, the state of the other. Mirror neurons—discovered by Rizzolatti in macaques—fire both when you perform an action and when you see someone else perform it. They do not discriminate between agent and observer. Your brain "feels" what the other feels, not because it imagines it, but because it executes it.

> **In physics this is called:** resonant coupling between coupled oscillators.  
> **In daily life it is like:** two tuned violins: when one plays, the other vibrates without being touched.

But empathy has limits. We cannot resonate with what we cannot model. A torturer does not feel the torture of their victim not because they lack mirror neurons—they have them—but because they have built a model of the victim that does not include their subjective experience. Dehumanization is not the absence of empathy: it is empathy directed at a model that excludes the other's interiority.

> **In physics this is called:** the internal model determines which signals are resonated and which are filtered.  
> **In daily life it is like:** a water filter: there is still water, but what passes through is only what the filter allows.

### The ghost: the model running in the void

Mary-Frances O'Connor describes the mechanism of grief: the brain builds a predictive model of close people, and that model continues its regular activity even after the person dies. Not as a pathological error—as the normal functioning of a system designed to predict.

For love, this produces recognizable grief: turning toward where the person should be, making coffee for two, starting a call before remembering. For prolonged conflict, the mechanism is identical but the expression is different. The horizon does not look for the other—it anticipates them. It knows exactly what they would do in this situation, how they would react to this news.

> **In physics this is called:** persistent predictive model post-dissolution.  
> **In daily life it is like:** continuing to write messages to someone who is no longer there: the finger knows what to say, even if the person is not there to read it.

The ghost is neither hallucination nor pathology. It is the direct consequence of having built a high-precision model of someone who is no longer there to generate the signals that model expected.

Physics offers an image: it is **incomplete scrambling**. The other's horizon dissolved—their information was distributed into the reservoir. But the surviving horizon is still organized around receiving signals from the other. The integration channels that were built over twenty years remain open. The entanglement persists even when one of the two poles has disappeared, because it was inscribed in the architecture of the surviving horizon, not just in the relationship between the two.

> **In physics this is called:** unilateral entanglement: the coupling structure persists in one pole after the other has disappeared.  
> **In daily life it is like:** an earpiece that keeps emitting the voice of someone who already hung up: the cable is still connected, even if the other end is empty.

### The breaking of the bond

Not all entanglements end in death. Some end in a breakup: divorce, betrayal, abandonment, gradual distancing. In these cases, the process is different but the mechanism is similar.

When a bond breaks abruptly, the remaining horizon does not have time to adjust its architecture. The integration channels remain open, but the signal they receive is no longer that of the other: it is silence, absence, or—worse—the signal of someone who is no longer the person you modeled. The result is massive cognitive dissonance: the system predicts one thing and receives another.

> **In physics this is called:** prediction-perception discrepancy: the internal model generates expectations that the world does not meet.  
> **In daily life it is like:** following the GPS to an address that no longer exists: the voice tells you to "turn right" but there is no street.

Gradual breakups are different. They give the horizon time to readjust, to close channels, to rebuild its architecture without the other. But they also give time for the entanglement to deepen before breaking. There is no general answer as to which type of breakup is more painful: it depends on the architecture of the horizon, the history of the bond, the environmental conditions.

### The asymmetry of entanglement

Entanglement is not necessarily reciprocal to the same degree.

In a relationship of requited love, the two horizons model each other with comparable intensity. The entanglement is approximately symmetric.

In an asymmetric adversarial relationship—a conflictive neighbor who rarely thought about the other, while the latter spent twenty years accumulating grievances—the entanglement is much greater in one direction than the other. The aggrieved neighbor modeled the conflictive one with the precision of someone who needed to predict them to defend themselves. The conflictive one modeled the aggrieved one much less, because the conflict cost them nothing.

The ghost is more persistent when the entanglement was more asymmetric in this direction. The one who built the most model is the one who takes the longest to dismantle it.

> **In physics this is called:** asymmetry of entanglement.  
> **In daily life it is like:** the one who thinks about the other the most is the one who suffers the most when the other leaves, even if they thought they suffered less.

The paradox: the aggrieved has spent twenty years being the one who paid the most attention to the neighbor. The notebook of grievances is not evidence that he mattered more to the neighbor than the neighbor mattered to him—it is evidence of exactly the opposite.

> **In physics this is called:** the investment of resources in modeling the other is a measure of entanglement, not of affection.  
> **In daily life it is like:** a detective following a criminal: they do not love them, but they know them better than their own family.

### The limit of entanglement

There is a limit. Entanglement cannot be total: if two horizons were to merge completely, they would cease to be two. Identity requires a boundary. The deepest love is not the dissolution of the self into the other—it is the expansion of the self that includes the other without losing itself.

> **In physics this is called:** maximum entanglement occurs before complete fusion: two systems that share geometry but maintain separate horizons.  
> **In daily life it is like:** two overlapping circles: the common area is the bond, but each circle remains a circle.

Contemplative traditions have explored this limit for millennia. Advaita Vedanta speaks of the identity between Atman and Brahman—the individual and the universal self—but also of maya, the illusion of separation. Mahayana Buddhism speaks of the Bodhisattva: someone who achieves enlightenment—the dissolution of boundaries—but chooses to remain in the world to help others. Not because they cannot merge, but because they choose to maintain a separate horizon while sharing the geometry of the whole.

> **In physics this is called:** stability of the horizon in the presence of non-local connection.  
> **In daily life it is like:** a window: it is transparent, it lets light through, but it remains a window—it does not turn into air.
