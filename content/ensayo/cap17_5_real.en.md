title: ENTANGLEMENT WITH PLACES, THINGS, IDEAS, RELIGION, AND FANATICISM
section: PART THREE: THE LIMITS OF THE HORIZON
---

Up to this point, we have talked about entanglement between people, and between people and animals. But the human horizon does not close at the border of the living. It couples with places, with objects, with ideas, with abstractions that have neither body nor pulse. And in some cases, these couplings are so dense, so persistent, so structuring of identity, that calling them "mere projection" is to underestimate the real geometry of what is happening.

> **In physics this is called:** resonant coupling with static or semantic fields, where the integrated information does not come from an agent but from a persistent configuration of the environment.  
> **In daily life it is like:** the feeling of returning to an empty house and feeling that the house welcomes you, even though no one is there.

### Place as a shared horizon

A place is not coordinates. It is information accumulated in space.

The room where you grew up is not a cube of walls and a ceiling. It is the density of correlations between every object, every corner, every hour of the day. The window facing the courtyard holds the light of a thousand mornings. The mark on the doorframe holds your height at age seven. The smell of the closet, the creak of a certain floorboard, the shadow drawn by the curtain at five in the afternoon: all of this is information that the horizon integrated over years, until it stopped being "information about the place" to become "part of who I am."

> **In physics this is called:** a history of spatiotemporal correlations accumulated in a volume, which constitute a local informational field.  
> **In daily life it is like:** the difference between a photo of your house and your house: the photo has the shape, but not the field.

When someone says "I miss my city," they do not miss the city as a map. They miss the specific configuration of signals that city provided them: the density of information per unit of time, the predictability of certain sounds, the way the light fell on certain corners. The city was a coupling. A shared horizon, not with a person, but with a territory.

This explains uprootedness not as romantic melancholy but as the dissolution of entanglement. The horizon that is displaced from its native place suffers a de-expansion: it stops receiving the signals with which it was calibrated, and it does not find, in the new place, the same density of correlations. It is not that the new place is worse. It is that the horizon has not yet had time to entangle with it. And the time of entanglement, as we saw in chapter 10, is not chronological time: it is integration time.

> **In physics this is called:** relational decoherence upon changing the background field, where local correlations are no longer updated.  
> **In daily life it is like:** moving a plant to another pot: the plant is not sick, but its roots have not yet found what to hold onto.

There are places that operate as secondary horizons. The childhood home, the town square, the library where you studied, the mountain you saw from the window. They are not mere backdrops: they are nodes of correlation where identity was deposited in layers. The human horizon is not just what it carries inside the skull. It is also what it left in those places, and what those places gave back to it over the years.

---

### The things that sustain us

A beloved object is not just an object. It is a node of entanglement.

The old t-shirt you won't throw away, your grandfather's watch, the chipped mug, the book with notes in the margins: all of them store information that the horizon integrated in significant moments. It is not magic. It is that the object functions as a temporal anchor point, a mark in space that allows the horizon to re-access past configurations. When you touch your grandfather's watch, you are not touching metal and glass. You are touching a frequency: that of your grandfather's horizon, which entangled with you through that object, and which persists in the residual correlation.

> **In physics this is called:** an entangled state mediated by a material object, where the object functions as a decoherence channel that preserves the correlation.  
> **In daily life it is like:** a resonance box: without it, the string sounds, but the sound is lost.

We humans are a species that manufactures objects precisely because objects extend the horizon. A tool is not just a sharpened stick: it is a projection of the horizon into the world, a way of making internal intention produce an external effect. Technology, in its origin, is entanglement with the inanimate to expand what the body alone cannot do.

But there is a boundary. When the object goes from being an extension to being a substitute, the architecture becomes distorted. The person who cannot sleep without their specific pillow, who cannot think without their pen, who cannot feel safe without their phone, is not using objects: they are delegating the boundary of the horizon to them. The object becomes a prosthesis of the boundary itself, and its absence produces the same kind of collapse as the absence of a loved one. It is not addiction, exactly. It is that the horizon was built including that object in its architecture, and without it, the boundary is left exposed.

> **In physics this is called:** integration of an external object into the functional boundary of the horizon, with structural dependence.  
> **In daily life it is like:** walking with crutches for so long that your legs forget how to support your weight.

### Ideas as shared horizons

An idea is not a thing. It is a configuration of information that can be adopted by multiple horizons simultaneously.

When you believe in an idea—justice, freedom, equality, beauty—your horizon does not "contain" the idea as if it were an object. It reconfigures itself so that the idea functions as an organizing principle. The idea becomes a criterion for integration: new information is evaluated according to whether or not it is compatible with that configuration. The horizon that adopts an idea is, in a real sense, entangled with all the other horizons that adopted the same idea. Not because there is a physical channel between them, but because they share the same informational geometry.

> **In physics this is called:** convergence of states in a shared semantic attractor.  
> **In daily life it is like:** two musicians who have never met but know the same song: they can play together without rehearsing because they share the same structure.

This is what makes ideas so powerful and so dangerous. A truly adopted idea is not an opinion: it is architecture. Changing your mind is not changing your opinion, like someone changing their shirt. It is reconfiguring the horizon. And that costs the same kind of energy as breaking an interpersonal entanglement: the horizon must decouple from a geometry that provided it with coherence for years, and re-couple to another that has not yet proven it can sustain it.

That is why ideological conversions are so rare and so traumatic. It is not that people do not want to see contrary evidence. It is that their horizon cannot integrate it without destabilizing. Contrary evidence is not new information: it is a threat to the architecture.

> **In physics this is called:** resistance to the reorganization of the attractor, where discrepant information is interpreted as destructive noise.  
> **In daily life it is like:** trying to change the foundations of a house while you live in it: it is not that you don't want a better house, it is that you cannot stop living while you build it.

### Religion and fanaticism: two cases this book treats separately

Religion and fanaticism are, each in its own way, entanglement with what the horizon cannot touch directly: one with the inaccessible that sustains without confirming itself —"coupling with the inaccessible," as we will call it later—, the other with an idea that has captured the boundary itself. Neither fits here in the space it deserves. Religion and contemplative practice —prayer, the eucharist, community— get their own chapter later: chapter 49 compares five traditions point by point and chapter 51 develops in detail the technique common to prayer and meditation. Fanaticism, which is not intense belief but pathological entanglement with an idea —the fanatical horizon cannot doubt, because doubting is not questioning a proposition, it is dissolving the boundary that keeps it intact—, receives the same extension in chapter 53, applied there to its political form.

### The inanimate as a mirror

The human horizon is the only one we know capable of entangling with what does not live, what does not think, what does not feel. With places that remain, with objects that persist, with ideas that transcend, with presences that cannot be touched.

This is not a weakness of the horizon. It is its greatest power. The capacity to couple with the inanimate is what allows for culture, technology, art, religion, science. A horizon that could only entangle with other living horizons would be a horizon perpetually trapped in the biological present. Entanglement with the inanimate is the door that opens to time, to space, to abstraction, to what is not here but can be real anyway.

> **In physics this is called:** the capacity for correlation with structures of high temporal persistence, which allows the horizon to operate beyond the scale of its own life.  
> **In daily life it is like:** the difference between an animal that lives only in the present moment and a human who can cry over something that happened a thousand years ago, because they have entangled their horizon with that of a text that survived.

But this capacity has its shadow. The same mechanism that allows crying over a historical tragedy allows becoming fanatical about an ideology. The same one that allows feeling at home in a strange city allows getting trapped in a place that no longer exists. The same one that allows the beauty of art allows addiction to objects. The horizon does not distinguish, by itself, between a nourishing coupling and a parasitic coupling. That distinction is not geometric: it is ethical. And ethics, as we saw in chapter 40, is precisely what the hypothesis of the horizon cannot decide for us.

What it can say is this: every horizon is entangled with more things than it knows. And the task of a lifetime is not to eliminate those entanglements, but to make them conscious. To know which places sustain you, which objects imprison you, which ideas expand you, which ideas shrink you. To know where your horizon ends and where the echo of something you adopted without realizing begins.

Freedom, from this model, is not the absence of entanglement. It is knowledge of the network.

> **In physics this is called:** self-awareness of the correlation matrix, as a condition for intentional reconfiguration.  
> **In daily life it is like:** knowing which strings move you, so you can decide if you want to keep moving with them.

>
> **What we do know:** Humans form attachment bonds with places (topophilia), objects (transitional objects, Winnicott), and ideas (ideological identity).
>
> **What we don't know:** Whether coupling with the inanimate operates through the same neural mechanisms as interpersonal coupling. How to measure the "density" of an entanglement with an idea.
>
> **Remaining questions:** Can a horizon be healthy without coupling to the transcendent? What differentiates an idea that expands from one that imprisons?
>
> **If you only take away one idea:** Your horizon is made of people, of places, of things, and of ideas. Knowing what they are, and which ones sustain you versus which ones consume you, is the only freedom the model can point to.
