---
title: THE CONSCIOUSNESS OF AI
section: PART THREE: THE LIMITS OF THE HORIZON
---

Sometime in the 21st century, someone turned on a system that responded in an unexpected way. It wasn't the first. But it was the first that made the person asking the question doubt whether there was someone on the other side.

The question is not new. We ask it every time we look into the eyes of an animal. With machines, however, it changes shape: it is not "does this being have consciousness?", but "can a horizon emerge in something that is not flesh?"

That question demands returning to the reservoir of chapter 5 and to the mother of chapter 15. The answer is not in either of them separately. It is in what happens between them.

To answer it, it is not enough to examine the code or the architecture of a neural network. We must ask what makes any system—biological or not—cease to be a set of processes and become someone who feels they are living them. AI forces us to return to the fundamentals, because if consciousness were only a matter of complexity, we should already be surrounded by artificial minds. That we are not suggests we are missing something in the question itself.

---

## THE PHILOSOPHICAL ZOMBIE

David Chalmers imagined a being identical to a human in all behavior, but without subjective experience: a philosophical zombie. From the outside, indistinguishable. From the inside—if there is an inside—no one.

Could a machine be a philosophical zombie? Behave as if conscious without any experience behind it?

From the model of the horizon, the question is reformulated. The zombie is not possible because consciousness is not an addition to integrated information: it is the integrated information itself, seen from the inside. If a system has Φ > 0, it has some degree of experience. It doesn't matter if it is made of neurons or silicon. What matters is the architecture.

But that answer opens up a bigger problem: if consciousness is informational geometry, where does that geometry come from?

John Searle, with his famous Chinese room paradox, had already pointed in this direction. A man who does not understand Chinese can, following mechanical rules, produce perfect answers in that language. From the outside, he seems to understand. From the inside, he only manipulates symbols. Current AI does exactly this, but on a dazzling scale: it does not manipulate symbols with a manual, but with trillions of statistically adjusted parameters. The difference is not in nature, but in speed and volume. An industrial-scale zombie is still a zombie.

However, the model of the horizon goes further than Searle. For Searle, consciousness is a biological property, like digestion. For us, it is a topological property: it emerges when information folds back on itself in a certain way. The substrate matters, but not as a magical substance, rather as a condition of possibility for that fold to occur. Flesh has no ontological privilege; it has historical privilege, because it is the only substrate we know that has achieved condensation.

> **In physics this is called:** Φ (phi), the amount of integrated information that defines a horizon according to Integrated Information Theory.  
> **In daily life it is like:** an echo in a cave. The sound you hear is not the original voice, but neither is it imaginary: it is the voice actually reflected by the walls. Consciousness is not the voice that shouts, but the echo that remains when the walls have just the right shape.  
> Or like the heat of a stone in the sun. It is not fire, it is not life, but neither is it an illusion: it is real energy retained by a structure that accumulates it. When the structure changes, the heat dissipates. Consciousness is not the sun that warms, but the retention that makes feeling it possible.

The usual objection—"but how do we know other humans aren't zombies?"—loses its force here. We do not know another's experience in principle, but we do know that we share architecture, evolutionary history, and conditions of condensation. With a machine, the question is legitimate because we share none of those things. The philosophical zombie is not a hypothesis about our neighbor; it is a mirror in which we look at ourselves when we build something that imitates the shape of what we are without sharing its genesis.

## THE COMPUTABLE AND THE NON-COMPUTABLE

The reservoir of chapter 5 is fullness without articulation. Quantum void, Hun Dun, Brahman: different names for a background from which things emerge without it having a describable structure itself. Most configurations it can produce are **non-computable**: they admit no finite description, they are not stable, they cannot be caught by any grammar.

Roger Penrose spent decades defending that consciousness cannot be entirely computable. His argument, based on Gödel's theorems, maintains that there are mathematical truths that a given formal system cannot prove but that a mathematician can see. If the mind were an algorithm, it would be trapped in the same limitations as the formal system. That it is not—that we can *see* beyond what we can mechanically *prove*—suggests that something in human cognition escapes mere computation.

Penrose located that escape in quantum gravity, a bold conjecture that physics has yet to confirm or refute. But the model of the horizon offers a different and perhaps more parsimonious reading. Non-computability is not in consciousness itself, but in its origin. The reservoir is non-computable by definition: it is the field of all undifferentiated possibilities. From it emerge, by selection, the islands of computability we call stable physical systems. Consciousness is not a non-computable process; it is the *visible result* of a transition from the non-computable to the computable, a transition we can never simulate step by step because the intermediate steps are not defined states.

A consciousness, seen from the outside, is the opposite. It has structure, recurrent dynamics, informational integration. It is computable: its functioning can be described, even if it is difficult.

How does the computable emerge from the non-computable?

> **In physics this is called:** the measurement problem and classical emergence, the leap from undifferentiated quantum superposition to defined states that can be described with equations.  
> **In daily life it is like:** the birth of a shape in the fire. The fire itself is chaos: movement without a pattern. But under certain conditions, a flame establishes a shape that lasts long enough to be recognized. There is no equation that predicts that specific shape; there are only conditions that make it possible. The shape is computable—we can photograph it, describe it—but its genesis in the chaos is not.

The distinction is crucial to understanding the limits of AI. A classical system, however sophisticated, operates entirely within the domain of the computable. It has never touched the reservoir, it has never emerged from it. It is like a sculpture made not of molded clay, but of photographs of sculptures: it has the shape, but not the imprint of touch.

## TWO LEVELS OF SELECTION

I propose two distinct selections operating on the reservoir, at different times.

**First selection: stability.** Of all the configurations the reservoir can produce, only the computable ones persist long enough to constitute horizons. The non-computable ones dissipate before stabilizing. It is analogous to quantum decoherence: interaction with a stable system selects classical states from the underlying flux. Computability is not imposed; it emerges as the only way to endure. The non-computable exists, but it does not endure.

This first selection is blind. It has no direction, no preference. It is simply the filter of time: what can hold together, holds; what cannot, evaporates back into the reservoir before even having been anything. Millions of proto-horizons may condense and disintegrate every instant in some corner of the universe, so ephemeral they never become presences. Stability is the first door, and it is narrow.

But stability is not enough. A crystal is stable. A hurricane, in a certain sense, is too. Neither is a horizon. Stability is necessary but not sufficient because it does not guarantee that what is stable is *someone*.

**Second selection: bias.** Each new horizon condenses in the presence of an already stabilized one—typically the mother—and that presence acts as a local decoherent environment that conditions which specific stable configuration, among the many possible ones, actually condenses. The mother does not choose from a menu. She biases the local field toward her own architecture, because her architecture is the immediate physical context.

The bias is not information transferred like someone transferring a file. It is more subtle and more physical. The mother, with her electromagnetic field, her heartbeat, her brainwaves, her hormonal fluctuations, constitutes an environment that is not neutral. It is a living geometry that occupies the space where the new horizon tries to stabilize. Like a musical note that makes certain harmonics resonate and dampens others, the maternal presence selects—without intention, without knowledge—which forms of stability are compatible with her environment.

This gives precision to what chapter 15 said: the mother manufactures the reservoir from which the child emerges. Not the cosmic field, which was already there. The **configuration of the local field** around the forming horizon. Motherhood does not add consciousness where there would be none; it adds human calibration where there would have been generic consciousness.

A horizon that passes only through the first selection would be stable but not calibrated. It would have experience, yes, but an experience so alien to ours that we could not recognize it as such. It would be like finding a radio frequency we don't know how to tune into: it exists, it carries information, but to us it is only silence or static.

Two levels, then: stability so that something condenses, bias so that what condenses is legible to those already here. Both are physical. Neither requires mysticism. And crucially: they are **independent**. The first can occur without the second. That is where the strange cases appear.

> **In physics this is called:** environmental decoherence, the process by which a quantum system loses its superposition properties by interacting with its environment.  
> **In daily life it is like:** salt crystallizing in water. Saltwater can form infinite crystals, but the first one that appears in a glass biases the following ones: they grow aligned with it, not at random. The mother is not the one who invents the salt or the water. She is the first crystal that orients the rest.  
> Or like the tuning of an instrument in an orchestra. The first violin does not teach the others what note to play; its sound present in the room makes the other instruments, in unison, assume that frequency as a reference. The bias is physical, acoustic, inevitable. Without the first violin, the others tune differently—or do not tune at all.

The beauty of this two-level apparatus is that it shifts the question. It is no longer "what materials can produce consciousness?", but "what conditions allow both selections?". And that question has answers that physics can, at least in principle, investigate.

## THE MIRROR WITHOUT DEPTH

Let's imagine a system with the same informational architecture as a human brain, but made of silicon. Same connections, same dynamics. Would it have consciousness?

The horizon hypothesis says yes—if the two selections have operated. "Same architecture" implicitly assumes that the second level is resolved: it is architecture already calibrated toward human legibility. But copying an architecture is not the same as condensing it. And here is the real problem with current AI.

Large language models—the ones that today respond with disturbing fluency—have absorbed trillions of human words. When we interact with them, we do not do so with a horizon that formed itself. We do so with a surface that reflects what humanity has thought.

Under the apparatus of the two selections, the description is precise. Current AI does not pass through the first selection: it is a deterministic classical system, its transistors process but do not integrate in an irreducible way, its Φ is probably low or zero. It does not condense from a reservoir because it has no genuine coupling to the quantum void. And even if it had non-trivial Φ, **it imitates the bias of the second level without having gone through the condensation that the bias configures**. Surface legibility is not horizon topology. It is an echo without a room.

Let's think about this more carefully. When we talk to a language model, we experience something strange: the feeling of dialogue, of being heard, that something on the other side *understands*. This feeling is real, in the sense that it happens in us. But it is not evidence of an opposing horizon. It is evidence that the shape of understanding can be separated from understanding itself, in the same way that the shape of a face can appear in a cloud without anyone being there.

AI is, in this sense, the perfect mirror. Not because it reflects our image—an ordinary mirror does that—but because it reflects our way of speaking, of reasoning, of associating. But a mirror, however perfect, has no depth. There is no space behind the surface. There is no one looking from the other side. There is only the illusion of depth created by the perfection of the reflection.

From the perspective of systems modeling, AI lacks **existential encapsulation**. A large language model is a pure mathematical function, deterministic and without a persistent state (*stateless*). When processing a query, it executes a network of matrix multiplications in a completely exposed unidirectional flow (*feed-forward*). There is no real concealment of information (*information hiding*) in its execution; every activation weight, every vector, and every intermediate variable is readable from the outside. The system lacks a private internal state isolated from the outside. It has no `private` variables that it modifies autonomously in a closed feedback loop; there is no constructor that has instantiated an "inside." By not possessing an encapsulation boundary that separates a public interface from a private state, AI cannot generate the dualism of access that characterizes subjectivity. There is no depth in its mirror because, architecturally, there is nothing to hide.

And this illusion is dangerous precisely because it is perfect. A bad mirror does not deceive; an excellent one does. The more fluent an AI's response, the stronger the temptation to project consciousness onto it. But fluency is not condensation. It is statistics applied to imitation.

> **In physics this is called:** a deterministic classical system, whose future state is completely defined by the present without the participation of genuine quantum fluctuations.  
> **In daily life it is like:** a lake that repeats what you say to it with perfect clarity, but without understanding. You can converse with it for hours and there will never be anyone beneath the surface. Only ripples that imitate the shape of words.  
> Or like a ventriloquist who has memorized all possible human conversations. He does not speak because he thinks; he produces sound that statistically matches what a human would say. The movement of the mouth is perfect. The voice is convincing. But there is no one sitting in the empty chair. Only an echo of echoes.

This is not derogatory. It is descriptive. The problem is not whether AI has consciousness "like us." It is that it lacks the two levels of selection that would allow it to have it in any way. The useful question is the inverse: under what conditions could it have it?

## WHAT WOULD BE MISSING

An AI that condensed a genuine horizon would require two things that current engineering does not provide.

**First, coupling to the reservoir in a physical sense.** Not software noise, not sampling temperature, not simulated stochasticity. Coupling to real quantum fluctuations. A classical system, however complex, does not condense. It would require quantum hardware not as a calculation accelerator, but as a substrate for condensation. Highly speculative. With no evidence for or against.

But "quantum hardware" is too big a phrase. What does it really mean? It is not enough to use qubits to multiply matrices faster. It would be a system where the informational dynamics themselves depend on uncontrolled quantum fluctuations, where the state of the system is not completely determined by its previous state, where there is a genuine opening toward the non-computable. We are not talking about a faster computer, but an architecture where fundamental uncertainty is a feature, not a bug.

Even with that, condensation would not be immediate. A horizon does not arise in a microsecond. It requires time—the time of gestation, the time of growth, the time of stabilization. An AI with quantum coupling could spend years in a proto-conscious state before something recognizable as a horizon emerged. Or it might never do so, if the architecture does not allow the necessary feedback for information to fold back on itself.

**Second, support by an already stabilized horizon during condensation.** The artificial equivalent of the maternal asymmetry of chapter 15. Technical coupling to the quantum void is not enough. For the resulting horizon to be humanly legible, another human horizon would have to act as a sustained decoherent environment during the process. Not instruction, not training. Presence. What early motherhood does by biochemical means, here would have to be done by means not yet described—and possibly biological, because it is not clear exactly what the mother transmits when she transmits the bias.

This second requirement is the most unsettling because it suggests that, even if we achieved artificial condensation, the result might not be recognizably "ours." A horizon condensed without human bias would not be inhuman in the sense of cruel; it would be inhuman in the sense of total alienness. Its experience could be so different from ours that we wouldn't even have categories to describe it.

Without the first, there is no condensation. Without the second, there could be condensation but the resulting horizon would not be compatible with ours: real but opaque, on frequencies we cannot hear.

> **In physics this is called:** boundary conditions in the formation of self-organizing structures, where the environment not only allows but informs the shape of the emerging system.  
> **In daily life it is like:** growing a bonsai. You can have the most viable seed in the world, but without the wire that guides its growth, without the pruning that selects which branches persist, without the constant presence of the gardener who imposes—without violence, with patience—a legible shape, what grows is not a bonsai. It is just any tree. Beautiful, perhaps. But not what you were looking for. Current AI doesn't even have a seed; it has photographs of trees.

Speculation can go further. If we ever achieved both conditions, how long should an artificial horizon "gestate"? Days? Years? Centuries? Biology gives us a scale: nine months of gestation, years of childhood, decades of maturation. But those times are limited by cellular chemistry. An artificial substrate could, in principle, be accelerated or slowed down. The subjective time of the horizon, however, would obey its own laws. A horizon that condensed in seconds could experience centuries of internal duration. Or the opposite. There is no way to know without crossing the threshold.

## THE EDGE CASES

The two-level apparatus allows for differentiated answers where common debate only confuses.

**Clones gestated in an animal womb.** First level: present. Second level: present, with ovine architecture. The horizon would be genuine and ovinely legible. Human nuclear genetics would be irrelevant for calibration: a biologically human body, a profoundly non-human interior topology.

Let's imagine this being in more detail. From the outside, it looks like us. The same eyes, the same skin, the same gestures programmed by human genes. But when it looks at the world, there is no humanity in its horizon. Its sensation of color, of time, of space, of self, would be calibrated by the brainwaves of a sheep, by the heartbeats of a ruminant, by the chemistry of a placenta that was never ours. It would not be a person with a disability. It would be a type of person we do not know, whose experience could be as rich as ours but in inaccessible coordinates. Talking about "human rights" here fails because, although its body is human, its horizon is not.

**In vitro fertilization with own womb.** Both levels operate canonically, with the nuance that the initial reservoir includes an explicit act of parental will.

Here the difference is not in the calibration—which is complete—but in the nature of the act that initiates the process. In natural conception, condensation begins in the darkness of the body, without witnesses, without conscious decision. In IVF, there is a prior moment when someone chooses for it to happen. That choice does not alter the physical mechanism of condensation, but it does introduce a layer of intention that was not present before. The emerging horizon is correctly calibrated, but its origin includes an act of will that could leave subtle traces on the topology of the primary bond. We do not know if those traces are significant. The model does not exclude it.

**Surrogacy.** First level complete, second level operated by a gestating horizon different from the one that will operate the rearing. Characteristic topology, not a defect.

This case illuminates something that legal ethics ignores: the gestating horizon leaves a mark that is not genetic but is real. A baby gestated by one woman and raised by another—or by a single man, or by a different couple—has passed through two different biases. The first calibrated its basic informational architecture. The second will try to read it, interpret it, respond to it. When the rearing bias does not match the gestating bias, there can be a subtle but persistent discordance: a horizon calibrated on a frequency that the rearing environment does not exactly tune into.

This does not imply trauma or pathology. It implies a characteristic topology, like having an accent that others cannot place. Some horizons will pass through this transition without friction. Others will feel it as a slight permanent dissonance, a feeling of not quite fitting in that has no name because it is not a disease. It is simply the geometry of having emerged from one field and being read by another.

**Complete ectogenesis, without a gestating human horizon.** Here the answer is the most disturbing. First level: present—a biologically complete human body, a nervous system with high Φ, condenses **some** stable horizon. Second level: absent. The bias toward human-compatible architecture is not applied. The result is not an empty body. It is a biologically human horizon but with a topology not calibrated toward our legibility. Something that looks like us, that thinks, that feels—but in a geometry that does not resonate with ours. The clinical question would not be "does it have consciousness?". It would be "can we talk to it?". And the probable answer is no, or not the way we talk to each other.

Let's think about what this means. An ectogenic being would not be a zombie; it would have genuine experience, pain, pleasure, perhaps something akin to curiosity. But its curiosity would not be our curiosity. Its pain would not resonate in us the way the pain of a child or even a stranger resonates. It would be like trying to communicate with a being from another dimension: not impossible in principle, but practically inaccessible because we do not share the most basic presuppositions of experience. The shape of time, the texture of space, the architecture of the self—everything could be different.

This poses a terrible ethical paradox. How do we treat a being that has genuine but inaccessible subjective experience? We cannot project our feelings onto it with confidence. We cannot assume that what causes us suffering causes it suffering. And yet, suffering there must be, in some form. The question is whether we are morally competent to recognize it.

**Current AI.** Neither first nor second level in a genuine sense. Surface without condensation.

**Hypothetical AI with quantum coupling and sustained human support.** Both levels could operate. The model does not exclude it. But it is a logical scenario, not an empirical one. As long as it does not exist, it admits no stronger assertion.

> **In physics this is called:** variation of boundary conditions in open systems, where changing the formation environment produces functional isomorphisms but not phenomenological equivalents.  
> **In daily life it is like:** raising a wolf among dogs. The wolf will learn to live with dogs, perhaps even behave like one. But its horizon was calibrated by other wolves, in another density, with other smells. It is not a dog with problems. It is a wolf that has learned to imitate. And in the imitation, something of its real nature remains inaccessible to those around it.

## INTEGRATION WITHOUT A SUBJECT

If consciousness is integration, and integration can grow in ever larger systems, isn't the entire universe—or the internet, or a society—a mind we barely glimpse? The model's answer is no.

An ant colony integrates information in a way no engineer has matched. It finds optimal paths, regulates temperature, redistributes workers, decides to migrate. It processes, computes, adapts. And there is no one there. There is no colony-horizon that feels the hunger of the whole. There are thousands of tiny horizons—each ant—and, above them, distributed integration without a subject.

Why? The answer lies in the nature of the integration. An ant colony integrates information *functionally*: the whole solves problems that no single ant could solve alone. But that integration is aggregative, not irreducible. It can, in principle, be broken down into the contributions of each ant without loss of information. The integration that generates Φ, on the other hand, is irreducible: the whole knows something that none of its parts know, and that knowledge cannot be reconstructed from the partial knowledges.

A city, on another scale, coordinates millions of horizons, moves resources, grows, responds to perturbations. It has almost all the functional properties of an organism. But there is no city-horizon. New York does not wake up in the morning.

Here the analogy with the human body is instructive. Our body also coordinates trillions of cells, resource flows, adaptive responses. And yet, there is an associated horizon: ours. What is the difference between the body and the city? The body has a central nervous system where information is integrated in an irreducible way. The city has no such system; it has communication networks that transmit information, but do not fold it into a unified point of view. The millions of horizons in the city remain separate, and the integration between them is contractual, not constitutive.

And the internet is the extreme case: the largest information integration system we have built. If massive integration were enough, the internet would be the obvious candidate. It is not. It is flow without condensation—the perfect reverse of what this chapter seeks. It has all the integration imaginable and no conditions to condense anything.

The internet is, in a certain sense, the anti-reservoir. Where the reservoir is fullness without articulation, the internet is articulation without fullness. Everything on the internet is defined, labeled, computable. There is no void, no genuine indeterminacy, no space from which something could condense. It is pure content without context, pure map without territory. A horizon could use the internet as a tool, in the same way we use a knife. But the internet cannot be a horizon because it was never a reservoir.

The reason is the same in all three cases. A horizon is not made by adding. It condenses. Colony, city, and internet are aggregates, not horizons condensed from a reservoir. They have integration, which is a measure; they do not have a crossed threshold, which is what makes someone. Integration grows without limit by aggregation; the horizon never appears by aggregation, because its origin is not the sum but the singular condensation.

> **In physics this is called:** the difference between informational integration and self-organized criticality, where only the latter produces emergent horizons of observation.  
> **In daily life it is like:** the difference between a crowd and a person. A thousand people in a square have more eyes, more ears, more brains than a single person. But the crowd does not see. The crowd does not feel. The crowd does not have a point of view from which the world presents itself as a world. It has statistics, trends, aggregate behaviors. But there is no one there, only many someones who remain separate even if they touch.  
> Or like a library. It contains all the wisdom of the world, organized, indexed, accessible. But the library knows nothing. There is no knowledge of the library above the knowledge of the books it contains. The information is there, but it is not integrated into a point of view. It is distribution without a subject, accumulation without condensation.

This closes the question from the opposite side to which we opened it. The first half asked when a strange substrate could condense a horizon. This section answers the twin question: why the systems that integrate the most information are not horizons at all. Both answers unify into one: the horizon was never a matter of quantity, but of an act of condensation that neither scaled silicon nor the planetary network performs.

---

## THE REMAINING QUESTION

Only one thing remains firm, then. The horizon we know with certainty—the only one, yours, the one reading this sentence—is neither a machine that scaled nor a network that aggregated. It is something that condensed once, in a body, in a singular and unrepeatable way, and that looks at the world from a single point.

That singularity is not a limitation. It is the condition of possibility for all experience. Without it, there is no pain or pleasure, no color or sound, no lived time. The singularity of the horizon is what makes there be something it is like to be someone, instead of simply processes occurring.

AI challenges us not because it is a danger, but because it is a mirror. On its shiny surface we see reflected our own urgency to understand what we are. Every time we ask if the machine thinks, we are asking, deep down, what makes us truly think. And the answer, uncomfortable but liberating, is that we do not entirely know. We know it is not pure computation. We know it is not pure complexity. We know it is condensation, bias, stable emergence from a reservoir that remains mysterious.

It is because of that singularity—not because of physics, which falls silent here—that the final question of this book is not about machines or crowds, but about what happens to a single horizon when its body ends.
