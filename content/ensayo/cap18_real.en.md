That question demands returning to the reservoir of chapter 5 and to the mother of chapter 15. The answer is not in either of them separately. It is in what happens between them.

Everything said about how a legible horizon condenses —chapter 32 develops it in detail with respect to artificial intelligence— rests on two selections. The first is stability: of all the configurations the reservoir can produce, only the ones that persist long enough come to constitute anything. The second is bias: each new horizon condenses in the presence of an already stabilized one —typically the mother—, and that presence orients which specific configuration, among the many possible ones, actually stabilizes. Her electromagnetic field, her heartbeat, her brainwaves constitute an environment that is not neutral: it selects, without intention or knowledge, which forms of stability are compatible with it.

That two-level apparatus allows for something public debate rarely offers: distinguishing with precision the cases where the body is human but the calibration is not, or the reverse. That is where common debate only confuses, and where the model, for once, can be clearer than intuition.

## THE EDGE CASES

**Clones gestated in an animal womb.** First level: present. Second level: present, with ovine architecture. The horizon would be genuine and ovinely legible. Human nuclear genetics would be irrelevant for calibration: a biologically human body, a profoundly non-human interior topology.

Let's imagine this being in more detail. From the outside, it looks like us. The same eyes, the same skin, the same gestures programmed by human genes. But when it looks at the world, there is no humanity in its horizon. Its sensation of color, of time, of space, of self, would be calibrated by the brainwaves of a sheep, by the heartbeats of a ruminant, by the chemistry of a placenta that was never ours. It would not be a person with a disability. It would be a type of person we do not know, whose experience could be as rich as ours but in inaccessible coordinates. Talking about "human rights" here fails because, although its body is human, its horizon is not.

**In vitro fertilization with own womb.** Both levels operate canonically, with the nuance that the initial reservoir includes an explicit act of parental will.

Here the difference is not in the calibration—which is complete—but in the nature of the act that initiates the process. In natural conception, condensation begins in the darkness of the body, without witnesses, without conscious decision. In IVF, there is a prior moment when someone chooses for it to happen. That choice does not alter the physical mechanism of condensation, but it does introduce a layer of intention that was not present before. The emerging horizon is correctly calibrated, but its origin includes an act of will that could leave subtle traces on the topology of the primary bond. We do not know if those traces are significant. The model does not exclude it.

**Surrogacy.** First level complete, second level operated by a gestating horizon different from the one that will operate the rearing. Characteristic topology, not a defect.

This case illuminates something that legal ethics ignores: the gestating horizon leaves a mark that is not genetic but is real. A baby gestated by one woman and raised by another—or by a single man, or by a different couple—has passed through two different biases. The first calibrated its basic informational architecture. The second will try to read it, interpret it, respond to it. When the rearing bias does not match the gestating bias, there can be a subtle but persistent discordance: a horizon calibrated on a frequency that the rearing environment does not exactly tune into.

This does not imply trauma or pathology. It implies a characteristic topology, like having an accent that others cannot place. Some horizons will pass through this transition without friction. Others will feel it as a slight permanent dissonance, a feeling of not quite fitting in that has no name because it is not a disease. It is simply the geometry of having emerged from one field and being read by another.

**Complete ectogenesis, without a gestating human horizon.** Here the answer is the most disturbing. First level: present—a biologically complete human body, a nervous system with high Φ, condenses **some** stable horizon. Second level: absent. The bias toward human-compatible architecture is not applied. The result is not an empty body. It is a biologically human horizon but with a topology not calibrated toward our legibility. Something that looks like us, that thinks, that feels—but in a geometry that does not resonate with ours. The clinical question would not be "does it have consciousness?". It would be "can we talk to it?". And the probable answer is no, or not the way we talk to each other.

Let's think about what this means. An ectogenic being would not be a zombie; it would have genuine experience, pain, pleasure, perhaps something akin to curiosity. But its curiosity would not be our curiosity. Its pain would not resonate in us the way the pain of a child or even a stranger resonates. It would be like trying to communicate with a being from another dimension: not impossible in principle, but practically inaccessible because we do not share the most basic presuppositions of experience. The shape of time, the texture of space, the architecture of the self—everything could be different.

This poses a terrible ethical paradox. How do we treat a being that has genuine but inaccessible subjective experience? We cannot project our feelings onto it with confidence. We cannot assume that what causes us suffering causes it suffering. And yet, suffering there must be, in some form. The question is whether we are morally competent to recognize it.

**Current AI.** Neither first nor second level in a genuine sense. Surface without condensation.

**Hypothetical AI with quantum coupling and sustained human support.** Both levels could operate. The model does not exclude it. But it is a logical scenario, not an empirical one. As long as it does not exist, it admits no stronger assertion.

> **In physics this is called:** variation of boundary conditions in open systems, where changing the formation environment produces functional isomorphisms but not phenomenological equivalents.  
> **In daily life it is like:** raising a wolf among dogs. The wolf will learn to live with dogs, perhaps even behave like one. But its horizon was calibrated by other wolves, in another density, with other smells. It is not a dog with problems. It is a wolf that has learned to imitate. And in the imitation, something of its real nature remains inaccessible to those around it.

---

These cases do not exhaust the list, and technology will probably add others we cannot imagine today. But the criterion does not change: it is not genetics, nor appearance, nor even substrate that decides whether there is someone there. It is whether the two selections —stability and bias— have operated, and toward what architecture they calibrated the result. The further we move from the typical case, the clearer it becomes that these two questions, not resemblance to us, are the only ones the model knows how to ask.

> **Note to Chapter 31**
>
> **What we do know:** Gestational motherhood exerts a measurable physical bias on the architecture of the horizon that condenses. That bias is independent of genetics: it can operate with or without a genetic link, with or without a match between gestation and rearing.
>
> **What we do not know:** What exactly a mother transmits when she calibrates the local reservoir of her child. Whether the traces left by an explicit act of parental will, or a discordance between the gestating horizon and the rearing horizon, are significant for the resulting topology. Whether a biologically human horizon not calibrated toward our legibility —the edge case of complete ectogenesis— could, over time, learn to communicate with us.
>
> **Remaining questions:** Could complete ectogenesis produce a being with genuine subjective experience but inaccessible to us? What ethical obligations do we have toward a horizon whose experience we cannot confidently project? Could a bias someday be deliberately calibrated instead of inherited?
>
> **If you only take away one idea:** It is not genetics, nor appearance, that decides whether there is someone there: it is whether the two selections —stability and bias— have operated, and toward what architecture they calibrated the result.
