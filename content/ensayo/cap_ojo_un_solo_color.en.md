---
title: THE COLLECTIVE HORIZON
subtitle: (The Eye of a Single Color: on political belonging and fanaticism)
section: PART FOUR: THE LIMIT OF THE EXPERIMENT
---

An idea is not a thing. It is a configuration of information that can be adopted by many horizons at once, and the horizon that adopts it does not contain it the way one keeps an object: it reconfigures itself so that the idea works as an organizing principle, as the criterion with which to evaluate any new information. What this chapter asks is what happens when the idea that organizes the horizon points not upward—toward a reservoir or an origin horizon—but sideways: toward thousands of horizons unknown to one another that share, without ever having met, the same configuration.

That is a political movement before it has a name, a leader or a flag. Before the condensation there is only scattered unease: thousands of grievances with no common boundary, a reservoir of unorganized indignation. Condensation happens when something—an image, a phrase, an injustice caught on film—suddenly draws an edge: this is what we demand, this is who we are, this is the line that separates us from those who do not share the demand. It is not a gradual process. It is a discontinuous phase transition, of the same kind that separates liquid water from ice: the unease was dissolved, "liquid", and suddenly it freezes into a shape with edges.

> **In physics this is called:** a first-order phase transition: the system jumps at once from a disordered state to one with structure, with no stable intermediate states.  
> **In daily life it's like:** supercooled water. It stays liquid below zero until a knock freezes all of it in a second.

A newly condensed collective horizon is, like any newborn horizon, fragile. It may dissolve back into the reservoir of diffuse unease it came from, if the edge that defined it fails to hold. Or it may consolidate, entangle itself with other horizons—parties, unions, existing institutions—and survive far beyond the generation that condensed it.

Software design has a vocabulary for distinguishing kinds of bond, and it serves here with surprising precision. An association is the most basic relation: two horizons know each other, cooperate on something specific, and their life cycles are autonomous—voting for a party once, without one's own identity depending on that vote, is an association. An aggregation is a whole-part relation in which the parts survive the dissolution of the whole: belonging to a party, a union, a movement, and remaining entirely oneself if that structure disappears tomorrow. A composition is the strongest relation of belonging: the life cycle of the part is tied, indissolubly, to the life cycle of the whole. When the collective horizon becomes a composition, the boundary between "I" and "we" stops being a metaphor. If the party loses, something inside me loses. If the movement dissolves, something inside me dissolves with it. No individual with a political opinion remains: what remains is a part that cannot survive the destruction of the whole on its own.

> **In software this is called:** association, aggregation and composition: three relations between objects, distinguished by whether the part's life cycle depends on the life cycle of the whole.  
> **In daily life it's like:** the neighbor you share an elevator with, the club you belong to, and an organ of your body. You can lose the first two and still be you.

Here appears the distinction that truly matters, and it has nothing to do with which side one chooses. A healthy political horizon can be an aggregation for its entire life: it defines you in part, organizes you, gives you company and meaning, and yet you survive—entirely you—if that belonging changes. A fanatical political horizon is a composition disguised as an aggregation: it presents itself as one more among your many belongings, but in reality it has captured the very boundary that separates your inside from your outside. It is no longer an idea you hold. It is the idea that holds you, and losing it does not feel like changing your mind: it feels like dying a little.

This is the exact architecture of fanaticism, applied here to its most contagious form. The fanatic cannot doubt, because to doubt is not to question a proposition: it is to dissolve the boundary that keeps their horizon intact. And it is no accident that fanatics of every ideology—right, left, religious, scientific—share the same violence toward disagreement: it is not that they hate the dissenter, it is that the dissenter emits a frequency their horizon cannot integrate without collapsing, and the horizon defends its integrity with the same ferocity with which a body defends its temperature.

> **In physics this is called:** boundary homeostasis: the system spends energy expelling any signal that threatens to break its membrane, just as a body defends its temperature.  
> **In daily life it's like:** a fever that rises not against a virus, but against a friend's question.

There is, however, a structural difference between political and religious fanaticism that deserves to be named separately. Vertical entanglement points at something that, by definition, lies beyond the reach of human revision: an impersonal reservoir or an origin horizon that no election can depose. Political entanglement points sideways: toward other horizons as finite, fallible and revisable as one's own. A party loses elections. A leader ages, errs, dies. An ideology corrects itself, or should be able to, because nothing in its origin protects it from being simply wrong. And yet political fanaticism treats that horizontal horizon—human, constructed, correctable—with the same intransigence that religious fanaticism reserves for what declares itself, from the outset, beyond question. It is borrowing the architecture of the vertical to sustain something that should never have stopped being horizontal: sacralizing the neighbor.

> **In physics this is called:** mistaking an open system for a boundary condition: treating a finite, revisable horizon as if it were a fixed parameter of the universe.  
> **In daily life it's like:** hanging on the wall, in an altar frame, the photo of someone who ages, makes mistakes, and may change their mind tomorrow.

Ethics exceeds the vocabulary of this experiment: no "is" produces an "ought" on its own, and politics is, almost by definition, the territory where that gap is crossed without cease. The model cannot say which political vision is correct—it has no tools for that, and it would be an imposture to pretend it does. What it can describe is the geometry, regardless of content: whether a collective horizon integrates disagreement or collapses against it. That question has no side. It can be put to any movement, of any color, in any century.

> **Note to Chapter 53**
>
> **What we do know:** Social movements show patterns of sudden condensation that are well documented in the sociology of crowds (Le Bon, McAdam). Group psychology describes deindividuation in crowds (Zimbardo, Festinger), and confirmation bias measurably worsens inside ideological echo chambers. Ron Jones's historical experiment in 1967, which inspired The Wave, is a documented case, not just fiction.
>
> **What we don't know:** Whether there is any neural or behavioral marker that distinguishes, from inside the experience, a healthy belonging from a fanatical one before it is too late to reverse it. Whether the architecture of political composition is avoidable at the scale of an entire society, or whether it is, simply, the price paid for the strength of any movement capable of changing something real.
>
> **Remaining questions:** Can an entire society avoid composition without losing the strength of its movements? Is there a marker that warns, from within, of the moment when belonging begins to replace? What does it take for a full square to keep a question?
>
> **If you only take away one idea:** A healthy collective horizon defines you without replacing you; the fanatical one captures your boundary. The test is not the side, but whether a reasonable question from an ally feels like information or like a threat.
>
> **Readings:** Le Bon, G., *The Crowd* (1895); McAdam, D., *Political Process and the Development of Black Insurgency* (1982); Zimbardo, P.G. (1969), on deindividuation; Festinger, L. et al. (1952), on deindividuation in groups; Orwell, G., *1984* (1949); Golding, W., *Lord of the Flies* (1954); Koestler, A., *Darkness at Noon* (1940); Strasser, T., *The Wave* (1981); Le Guin, U.K., *The Dispossessed* (1974).
