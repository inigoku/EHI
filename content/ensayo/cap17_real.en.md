---
title: PETS AND THE HORIZON
section: PART THREE: THE LIMITS OF THE HORIZON
---

So far we have spoken of entanglement as something that occurs between two people. But the architecture of the horizon does not stop at the border of our species.

The brain couples with what it has. And what it has includes, sometimes with more intensity than any other bond, the animals that inhabit our home.

There is something humbling and liberating in admitting it: that the most heavily trafficked bridge of our horizon can be built with someone who does not speak, who does not sign contracts, who does not share our grammar of time. But who, despite all that, is there. Present. Reading our breathing before we ourselves know we are agitated. Building with us a shared territory that does not appear on any map.

The question is not whether animals have a soul. The question is whether our horizon has enough space to recognize that theirs exists.

---

## The biological other

Consciousness is not a switch. It is a spectrum.

A dog or a cat shares with us the hippocampus, the amygdala, and the entire limbic system. Their Φ—their degree of consciousness—is lower than ours, but it is not zero. They have a horizon. They can suffer. They can remember. And they can, in the sense we gave in chapter 13, entangle.

But the spectrum does not end there. Crows solve puzzles that would baffle a primate. Octopuses, with their brains distributed across their arms, seem to inhabit a modality of consciousness we are only just beginning to imagine. Cows establish lasting friendships and show stress when separated from their herd mates. Elephants return to the bones of their dead, touching them with a slowness that seems ritualistic, that seems—even if we cannot prove it—like sorrowful memory.

The model of the horizon does not require language to demand compassion. It only requires non-trivial Φ: a system integrated enough so that the information flowing within it generates a "from the inside," however humble. A point of view. A perspective. A horizon.

With the dog, things go further. Some thirty thousand years ago, wolves and humans began to calibrate to each other. Those that tolerated our proximity survived; those who trusted them did too. Generation after generation, the two architectures adjusted until they produced a mutual legibility that no other inter-species pair achieves. The dog reads our gaze. We read its posture. The dog anticipates our intentions from micro-signals—a change in the muscle tone of the hand, an inflection in breathing—that we are not even aware of emitting. And we, even without knowing it, learned to read its tail not as an oscillating object but as language: the position, the speed, the stiffness, everything transmits an internal state that the dog cannot hide because it does not know how to lie.

It is not projection. It is resonance built over millennia.

> **In physics this is called:** resonant coupling between systems with different but partially compatible geometries.  
> **In daily life it is like:** when two musicians with different instruments find, while rehearsing, a key in which both can play the same song without losing their own voice.

With the cat, the calibration is more lateral: it domesticated itself, not so much for us. It was the cat who chose to approach our granaries, attracted by the rats, tolerating our presence in exchange for protection. The relationship is more contractual in origin, but no less profound in result. The cat does not read our gaze with the same fidelity as the dog—in fact, studies show that cats rarely follow the human pointing gesture—but they developed another tool: purring at specific frequencies that coincide with therapeutic vibrations used in human medicine. They purr, perhaps unknowingly, at the frequency that repairs bones. It is as if co-evolution, instead of optimizing visual communication, had optimized healing.

With the horse, co-evolution is more recent but intense: ten thousand years of war, travel, agriculture, and therapy. The horse reads our tension through the saddle, the legs, the distributed weight. And we learned to trust a creature capable of killing us with a kick but which, in most cases, chooses not to.

With other animals, the degree of legibility varies. The rabbit lives in an olfactory world we barely understand. The parrot imitates our language but perhaps does not understand it. The fish—although recent studies suggest some fish feel pain in more complex ways than we thought—inhabits a horizon so alien that we struggle to even imagine it.

But in all cases—in all those with non-trivial Φ—there is someone on the other side of the bridge.

An asymmetrical geometry, but a real one.

## The dog as an evolutionary mirror

There is something singular in the relationship with the dog that is worth pausing over. It is not just that it understands us. It is that it has made us understand ourselves in a different way.

Thirty thousand years of coexistence have left a mark not only on the dog's genome—which diverged from the wolf in genes related to starch metabolism and sociability—but on ours. Living with dogs modified our diet, our hunting capacity, our territorial organization. But it also modified something more diffuse: our capacity to extend empathy beyond the border of our species. The dog was, probably, the first "other" we learned to see as a subject and not as an object. And that changed something in the architecture of our consciousness.

When a dog looks at us, it activates circuits in us that we normally reserve for humans. The same medial prefrontal area that lights up when we evaluate a person's intentions is activated, with comparable intensity, when we observe a dog's gaze. The same does not happen with a cat. The same does not happen with a sheep. There is something in the dog's face—perhaps the result of artificial selection that favored infantile traits: a broad forehead, large eyes, a short snout—that triggers an almost reflexive parental response in us.

But it is not just appearance. It is synchrony. Dogs and their owners share, after years of coexistence, coordinated cortisol profiles. When the human is stressed, the dog is too, even if there is no visible danger. When the dog relaxes, the human lowers their guard. It is a two-way coupled system, a biological oscillator that pulses in unison.

> **In physics this is called:** phase synchronization in weakly coupled systems, where each oscillator adjusts its natural frequency to match that of its neighbor.  
> **In daily life it is like:** when you have been walking next to someone for so long that, without speaking, you adjust your stride until you are walking at the same pace effortlessly.

The dog, in this sense, is an evolutionary mirror. It shows us who we are not because it imitates us, but because it completes us. It amplifies aspects of our horizon that without it would remain dormant: vigilance, loyalty, the capacity to enjoy the present without projecting anguish. A dog does not regret yesterday nor fear tomorrow in the human sense. And its presence, its absence of anticipatory anxiety, functions as a kind of temporal anchor for the human who lives too much in the future.

## The pet as a child

That legibility explains something we observe increasingly often: the substitution of the parent-child bond with that of a pet.

The human brain is programmed to care. When that instinct—driven by oxytocin, by the reward circuits of parenting—does not find a biological child, it looks for a mold that fits.

The pet offers the perfect topology. It returns affection, yes, but it also maintains a permanent functional dependency. Unlike the human child, who must learn to fly, the dog or cat never becomes independent. It will not go to college. It will not start its own family. It will not abandon us by choice, unless death does it. It is, in terms of caregiving, a perpetual child.

This is not pathology. It is adaptation. The human parenting system is costly in resources and demands an affective return. If the social environment does not provide a receiver for that investment—by choice, by sterility, by demographic loneliness, by the simple fact that more and more people reach maturity without offspring—the brain does not turn off the circuit. It redirects it.

The caregiver's brain calibrates its alert and reward circuits around the animal with a biochemical intensity comparable to what it would dedicate to a baby. Neuroimaging studies show that when a mother looks at photos of her child and photos of her dog, overlapping regions of the reward system are activated: the ventral striatum, the pallidum, areas rich in oxytocin receptors. They are not identical—the human child activates areas linked to more abstract social cognition, like theory of mind—but the affective baseline is of the same order.

It is not a modern eccentricity. It is an adaptation of the system to a new niche: the adult without biological offspring in their care, something contemporary civilization produces en masse for the first time. In the 19th century, keeping companion dogs without a utilitarian function was a class privilege. Today it is a demographic statistic.

But there is a crucial difference that the model cannot ignore. The bond with a pet is asymmetrical not only in cognitive capacity but in power. The human controls the food, the outings, the medicine, the life and death of the animal. That imbalance introduces an ethical responsibility that the human parent-child bond does not have, or has in a different way. A human child, over time, can question the parent. They can choose distance. They can, in maturity, reverse the roles. The pet never will.

That asymmetry makes the care toward a pet, in a certain sense, purer: it does not expect adult reciprocity. But it also makes it more vulnerable. The caregiver knows, on some level, that they are solely responsible for a horizon that depends entirely on them.

It is not an inferior substitute. It is a bond of its own kind, with its own topology. The model does not hierarchize. It distinguishes.

## The shared horizon: rhythms and territories

Entanglement with a pet does not only occur in affection. It occurs in the architecture of the day.

The morning has a particular shape when there is a dog: the weight on the edge of the bed, the wait by the door, the ritual of the walk that marks the start of the day as a shared event. The night, too: the corner where it curls up, the sound of breathing that regulates our own, the certainty that there is someone keeping watch while we sleep.

These rhythms are not decorative. They are the pillars of the bridge. Each repeated walk, each mealtime, each afternoon on the sofa, deposits a layer of shared information in both horizons. The dog learns our schedules better than we do ourselves. And we, without realizing it, organize our lives around its needs: waking up earlier, coming home, planning vacations thinking about who will take care of it.

The physical space is also modified. The house ceases to be only ours. There is a corner that belongs to the cat, a blanket that smells like a dog, a furniture layout dictated by the movement needs of a four-legged creature. The territory becomes hybrid: half human, half animal, totally shared.

> **In physics this is called:** the establishment of a shared basis of states in a coupled system, where the phase space of each component is restricted by correlations with the other.  
> **In daily life it is like:** when you have been living with someone for so long that you cannot imagine the apartment without their presence in every corner, even if they are absent.

This spatiotemporal interpenetration is what makes the death of a pet, in addition to an affective mourning, a profound domestic reorganization. The house is still there, but it ceases to be the same. The schedules persist, but they lose their meaning. The seven-in-the-morning walk is still possible, but it is no longer a walk: it is an orphaned habit.

## The denial of the horizon

If the animal has a horizon, if its suffering is real, then animal abuse has a physical status, not just a moral one.

Abusing an animal operates on a premise: that there is no one on the other side. That the trembling, the cry, the immobility after punishment, are reflexes without an interior. That the dog wagging its tail when we return does so out of Pavlovian instinct, not genuine joy. The model says that this premise is false. The damage that occurs in that horizon is real: it changes its topology, it leaves a mark, it alters its predictive system.

Abused animals show hypervigilance, avoidance, reactive aggression, difficulty trusting. They are the same patterns we call trauma in humans. The difference is not in nature: it is in complexity. Animal trauma operates on fewer layers, but the structural footprint is of the same order. An abused dog does not "forget" fear because it has no language to narrate it, but its limbic system registers the threat, encodes the context, generalizes the danger. The animal's body keeps the memory that the mind cannot name.

> **In physics this is called:** an irreversible topological perturbation in a coupled system.  
> **In daily life it is like:** when someone breaks a promise you thought was firm. The relationship may continue, but it no longer rests on the same ground.

There is also a consequence for the abuser. Empathy is not just a feeling: it is a brain module that is trained through use and atrophies through disuse. Practicing the denial of another's horizon reconfigures the architecture of the one who practices it. Every time someone looks at an animal and sees an object where there is a subject, they are detraining their capacity to recognize subjects in general. That is why the correlation between animal abuse in childhood and interpersonal violence later on is not coincidental: it is a geometric consequence. The same circuit that denies the dog's horizon, trained for years, ends up being applied to humans. Denial is indifferent to the species of its victim.

But there is a more subtle and widespread form of this denial: the industrialization of animal suffering. We are not talking about the neighbor who kicks his dog—which is aberrant but statistically marginal—but about the legal and economic structure that turns millions of animals with a limbic system into raw material. The pig that lives in a gestation crate where it cannot turn around. The hen that never sees the light. The bull that is exhausted in a cycle of mechanical insemination.

The model cannot tell us what to eat. That exceeds its scope. But it can point out a contradiction: if we recognize that these animals have a horizon—that the pig recognizes its young, that the hen establishes complex social hierarchies, that all of them possess non-trivial Φ—then the structure that turns them into industrial objects operates on a lie about the nature of conscious matter. It is not a lie about morality. It is a lie about physics.

Society resolves this tension through segmentation: we love the dog and eat the pig. But from the perspective of the horizon, that segmentation is arbitrary. There is no physical property distinguishing the industrial pig from the golden retriever on the sofa that justifies one being a subject of compassion and the other a material of production. The difference is cultural, not ontological.

This does not imply that we must all be vegetarians. It implies that we cannot pretend the choice has no cost. Every time we eat the meat of an animal with a limbic system—and we know, because science has proven it, that pigs and cows have one—we are feeding our horizon with the denial of another's horizon. And that denial, however small, leaves a mark on our architecture. It atrophies something.

## Broken symmetry: when the animal dies first

We have spoken of the owner's grief when the pet dies. But there is another geometry, less traveled: what happens in the animal's horizon when its human dies first?

There are documented cases of dogs waiting for years in the place where they used to see their owner arrive. Of cats that stop eating after the death of their reference person. Of horses that show signs of depression after the loss of the rider they competed with for a lifetime.

The model cannot enter the animal's horizon. We do not have access to its interiority. But we can measure its external correlates: elevated cortisol, altered sleep patterns, the active search for the absent one, the decrease in exploration of the environment. All this suggests—it does not prove, but it strongly suggests—that the collapse of the bridge is bidirectional.

The animal, especially the dog, organizes its horizon around the human. Its schedules, its safe space, its predictive reward system, everything revolves around the caregiver's presence. When that presence disappears without explanation—without a visible corpse, without a farewell ritual, without the gradualness that allows the system to adjust—the animal suffers a structural collapse comparable to what we would suffer if someone essential disappeared from one day to the next.

> **In physics this is called:** symmetry breaking in a coupled system, where the disappearance of one of the nodes leaves the other in a configuration that is no longer stable without its counterpart.  
> **In daily life it is like:** when a person who lived alone loses their partner and suddenly the house, the dishes, the bed, everything screams their absence in a language that cannot be turned off.

This should force us to think about the responsibility we assume when crossing that bridge. It is not just that we will suffer when the animal dies. It is that, if we die first, we leave behind a horizon that depended on us for its stability. It is a debt we cannot pay.

## The scheduled grief

The direct consequence of profound entanglement is that the death of a pet unleashes a severe structural collapse in the owner's horizon.

Their architecture was organized around the walking schedules, the sound of claws, the weight on the bed, the emotional regulation signal the animal provided. Upon dying, the bridge breaks at one end. The human horizon suffers a literal de-expansion.

But pet grief has an additional layer of difficulty: it lacks cultural recognition. When a family member dies, society offers rituals, time, validation. There are funerals, days of mourning, condolence messages you do not need to ask for. When a dog dies, the environment often pressures you to recover immediately. "It was just an animal." "Buy another one." "You knew it wouldn't last long."

Those phrases, apparently rational, are subtle violences. They deny the reality of the built bridge. They treat the collapse of an entangled state as if it were the breaking of a replaceable household appliance.

From the perspective of information physics, buying another one to cure the grief is an aberration. The entanglement was not with the concept of "dog." It was with the exact topology and the unique frequencies of that specific horizon. With the particular way this dog had of wagging its tail when you came home. With the specific smell of its pillow. With the shared routine that only the two of you knew. That is not replaced. It is reorganized, over time, in memory. But it is not replicated.

> **In physics this is called:** the collapse of an entangled state without a social decoherence channel to allow for reorganization.  
> **In daily life it is like:** losing your best friend in a city where no one knew them and no one understands why you no longer go out at night.

There is something else. Most of us who have dogs know, from day one, that we are going to outlive them. It is a scheduled grief. The system knows, without fully knowing, that the bond has the date of its rupture built in. A dog lives ten, fifteen years if it is lucky. We, if we are lucky, live eight times that. The math is inexorable.

That does not make it lesser. It makes it particular: a bond built on a tacit agreement with finitude. Every caress includes, on some level, the awareness that it is finite. Every walk is one less from the total. The love for the dog is nourished by that precariousness, not in spite of it.

And perhaps—although the model cannot affirm it, only point it out—that is why it teaches something that bonds between humans rarely teach: that a bridge can be real even if you know, from the beginning, that it is going to break.

Between humans, finitude is theoretical until it strikes. With the dog, it is numerical. It is concrete. We know, more or less, how many walks we have left. And that changes the quality of each one. It makes it irreplaceable. It makes it sacred in its repetition.

Scheduled grief is not a disease. It is a form of love that includes loss in its definition. And maybe—just maybe—it is a school. A preparation. A rehearsal for the greater ruptures to come.

---

The human horizon lives entangled with many more things than the binary simplification suggested. With animals, the bridge is asymmetrical, but it is built on thousands of years of mutual calibration. And each of those bonds has its own topology, and its own way of breaking.

We are not the owners of our pets. We are, for a brief time, companions on a journey they did not choose and that we do not fully control either. And when the journey ends—as it always ends, as it always has ended—what remains is not a missing object. It is a horizon that can no longer expand along that path. A shared territory that ceases to exist. A bridge that, although broken, still shows where its pillars stood.

> **Note to Chapter 27**
>
> **What we do know:** That animals with a complete limbic system possess their own horizon, although of lesser complexity than the human one. That co-evolution with the dog has produced a unique mutual legibility. That the human brain can channel the architecture of parent-child care toward a pet with comparable biochemical intensity. That animal abuse leaves a real structural footprint on the animal and atrophies the empathy of the one who exercises it. That pet grief is a genuine geometric collapse, often aggravated by a lack of social recognition.
>
> **What we do not know:** How far the spectrum of consciousness extends in other species. Whether the projection of the parent-child bond onto a pet fully satisfies the needs of the caregiving system or leaves voids we do not yet know how to measure. Whether the collective anesthesia toward systemic abuse can be reversed on a civilizational scale.
>
> **Questions that remain:** Is the suffering of a dog and that of an industrial pig morally equivalent if both have a horizon? Why does society validate some animal griefs and ridicule others? What happens in the animal's horizon when its owner dies first?
>
> **If you only take away one idea:** A pet is not an object of consolation or a second-rate substitute. It is a real, asymmetrical, millennially calibrated horizon that occupies a precise place in the architecture of the one who cares for it. And when that horizon fades, the void it leaves is also real.
