## [ILUSTRACIÓN 18: "The limit of the experiment"]
*Old map unfolded. Territory drawn with precision in the center. At the edges, the territory dissolves into blank paper. A compass opened to the maximum. Handwritten note: "The map is not the territory."*

---

Every thought experiment eventually finds its limit. Not because imagination runs out—but because the question being asked demands tools the experiment does not have.

There is a difference between not knowing something yet and knowing that something cannot be known from where we stand. The first case is an invitation: forge a path, invent new instruments, wait. The second case is a glass wall: you can see it, you can describe it, you can feel its cold against your forehead, but crossing it would require ceasing to be who you are. It would require a vantage point that the universe, in its current architecture, does not seem to offer creatures like us.

This chapter is not closing modesty. It is an honest description of the points where the horizon hypothesis ceases to be useful, or where it contradicts itself, or where it simply has nothing to say.

Recognizing the limits of a tool does not weaken it. On the contrary: it is the only way to use it without breaking it. A surgeon who does not know where their scalpel ends and the optic nerve begins is not bold: they are dangerous. A thinker who does not know where their conceptual framework ends and the mystery begins is not profound: they are lost.

Epistemological honesty—knowing what we do not know—is the final care we owe to an idea we have followed for nine hundred pages.

### First: scale — or why this is a map, not an engine

There are thirty orders of magnitude between the scale where quantum gravity operates and the scale of a neuron.

That is not a technical detail. It is a gap between worlds.

Quantum physics operates at scales where spacetime itself may not be continuous, where information behaves like geometry and geometry like information, where a photon can be in two places before the clock of the universe ticks even once. Neuroscience operates at scales where body temperature melts most superpositions in microseconds, where millions of sodium and potassium ions cross membranes like crowds in a train station, where humidity, thermal noise, and the complexity of metabolism cause most relevant quantum phenomena to dissipate before a single synaptic signal completes its journey.

This does not invalidate the experiment. But it encloses it.

> **In physics this is called:** the scale gap between quantum gravity and neuroscience.  
> **In daily life it is like:** a road map: it doesn't tell you what trees are on each curve, but it allows you to plan the trip.

Think of it as a map. A road map doesn't tell you what trees are on each curve, nor if the asphalt is slippery this morning, nor what song is playing on the radio of the car overtaking you. But it allows you to plan the trip. It tells you that if you take the northern highway for a hundred and fifty kilometers, you will reach the port where the ferry waits. The map is not the journey. The map is not the ferry. The map is not the sea breeze that will hit your face when you step on deck. But without the map, perhaps you would never have left home.

The horizon hypothesis is a formal map: a mathematical structure that organizes questions and suggests connections between domains that do not usually speak to each other. It is not a causal mechanism: it does not predict which neuron will fire when, it does not allow intervening in a brain to modify its "Hawking temperature", it offers no experimental pathway to detect a "horizon of consciousness" in a functional scanner. There is, as far as we know, no signal we can measure in an electroencephalogram that corresponds directly to the curvature of an information horizon.

David Hume, in his *An Enquiry Concerning Human Understanding* (1748), had already seen this with a clarity contemporary neuroscience sometimes forgets: we never perceive the fabric of causality itself, only the constant conjunction of events that follow one another. There is no visible thread joining cause to effect —only the reliable repetition that A precedes B—. In the same way, there is no observable bridge connecting the electrical discharge of a biological tissue to the private texture of a thought. The horizon hypothesis does not claim to find that lost thread. It aims, like every formal map, to show us the geometry of the room we are locked inside, not the mechanism that lights it.

This may seem like a weakness. But let us remember that Shannon's information theory also does not predict which transistor will heat up at what moment in a computer, and yet it has allowed us to build the Internet. The theory of evolution also does not predict which fly will land on which flower at three in the afternoon, and yet it explains why flowers exist and why flies exist. A framework can be immensely useful without being immediately operative.

It is useful as a map. Useless as a tool. And that is fine. Not all maps have to be keys. Not all understanding has to translate into control. Sometimes, understanding that two territories that seemed separate are connected by a bridge is enough progress for a single generation of thought.

### Second: the hard problem — or why we don't know why we feel

The hypothesis can speak of geometry, of information, of entanglement, of entropy gradients that rise and fall like the tide. But it cannot speak of **what it feels like** to be that geometry.

It cannot say why the pain of an ingrown toenail feels like a throb and not like blue. It cannot say why the taste of dark chocolate feels like joy on the tongue of some and like bitterness in others. It cannot say why contemplating a sunset produces in some a warm expansion in the chest and in others a dizzying anguish at the passage of time.

That is the "hard problem of consciousness": why there is subjective experience at all. Why we are not machines processing information without anyone "inside." Why there is someone looking. Why the universe is not a movie projected without a theater, without a spectator, without a seat from which to observe.

> **In physics this is called:** the hard problem of consciousness (Chalmers, 1995).  
> **In daily life it is like:** asking why there is someone in the room, not just furniture.

Imagine an empty room. You can describe its dimensions, the color of its walls, the air temperature, the acoustics. You can predict how sound will bounce if someone strikes a saucepan. You can model the airflow if a window is opened. But none of that tells you why there is someone inside, sitting in the armchair, watching the light come through the crack in the curtains. Physics can describe the room with mathematical exquisiteness. It cannot say why there is a witness.

The experiment does not resolve this. No current theory does. We can talk about information integration, reentrant causal fields, gamma oscillations, predictive networks that minimize free-energy prediction error. All of that describes the room. None of those concepts explains why there is someone sitting in it.

But it is important to say it clearly: the horizon hypothesis **is not an explanation for the hard problem**. It is a framework to talk about its correlates with a richer vocabulary. It is a more broadly illuminated room, with better curtains, with a more precise thermostat. But it remains a room without an answer to the fundamental question.

The hard problem is still there, intact, at the center of everything. Like a stone in the shoe of Western thought. Like the question every child asks at some point—"why am I me?"—and that most adults learn to forget, not because they have found an answer, but because they learned that the question unsettles more than any answer reassures.

#### The reformulation of the problem and Hume's "bundle"

This is where the perspective of encapsulation proposes a radical reformulation.

The Scottish philosopher David Hume, in his *A Treatise of Human Nature* (1739), reached a famous conclusion when searching for the foundation of the 'self'. He wrote that, no matter how deeply he entered into what he called 'himself', he never stumbled upon a substantial subject or permanent soul; he only found a bundle of changing perceptions (heat, cold, pain, love, memory) in perpetual flux. For Hume, the self was not a spiritual substance, but a construction: the mind is a theater where actors enter and exit without any permanent underlying support structure.

The encapsulation model (Chapter 3) rescues Hume's intuition but describes its geometry. The 'self' is not a mysterious substance hidden inside the head, but neither is it a mere disjointed fiction. The self is the *inside* generated when the bundle of perceptions couples and integrates with sufficient strength (high Phi) within a boundary.

This topology allows us to reformulate Chalmers' Hard Problem. The question is no longer: *'How does physical matter produce the intangible quality of the mind?'*, a formulation that assumes an insurmountable substance dualism. The question is reformulated in terms of accessibility: *'How do certain configurations of matter establish encapsulation boundaries (access asymmetries)?'*

Subjectivity is not a magical byproduct created by the brain; it is what an encapsulated and integrated informational domain *is* when accessed from its own internal perspective. Hume was right not to find a permanent substance when looking inside the room: there is no 'spectator' separate from the play. What exists is the private state of the system (the bundle of perceptions) protected by the boundary of the horizon. The Hard Problem, therefore, is not an ontological abyss between two incompatible realities (mind and matter), but a consequence of the architecture of encapsulation: the universe admits regions with asymmetric read access.

### Third: ethics — or why this book cannot tell you what to do

The hypothesis can describe what happens when a horizon condenses, when it entangles, when it evaporates. It can describe the geometry of the bond, the asymmetry of loss, the gradient toward death. It cannot say what **ought** to happen.

Is it better for a horizon to condense or not to condense? Is it better for two horizons to entangle or remain separate? Is it better for a horizon to evaporate slowly or all at once? Is it morally permissible to "turn off" an artificial horizon if it has Φ>0? Must we respect the limits of a deteriorating horizon, or do we have a duty to intervene?

> **In physics this is called:** the is-ought gap, the gap between description and prescription.  
> **In daily life it is like:** a map that shows where the river is, but not whether you should cross it.

Hume, sitting in his Scottish study in the 18th century, realized something uncomfortable: in no logical argument can an "ought" be derived from an "is." You can observe that human beings suffer when deprived of sleep. That is an "is." But it does not follow from that that you "ought" to let your roommate sleep. That "ought" comes from somewhere else: from your values, from your culture, from your capacity to imagine the suffering of others as if it were your own.

The experiment has no answers for this. It only offers structural information: here is a horizon, here there is none, here are two that share geometry, here is one that is contracting. The decision about what to do with that information belongs to other domains: ethics, politics, law, personal choice, the human conversation that never ends.

The experiment is not neutral—no conceptual framework is—but its bias is of a specific kind: it tends to **homogenize** what it describes. If every horizon with Φ>0 has some degree of experience, then the difference between a worm and a human is one of degree, not of kind. This can be liberating (extended empathy toward all forms of sentience) or terrifying (what do we eat? what medicines do we test on whom? what AI systems do we turn off without asking if someone, on some level, "feels" the cut?).

The experiment does not resolve that tension. It puts it on the table. It forces you to look at it. And sometimes, looking at an unresolved tension is more honest than offering an easy answer you haven't earned.

### Fourth: death — or why physics does not promise immortality

The hypothesis offers three possibilities for what happens when a horizon evaporates:

1. **Remnant**: the information persists compressed into something microscopic, a grain of sand of data that contains, in principle, everything that was, but that no computer in the universe could ever read, because reading it would require more energy than is available in the entire cosmos.
2. **Trace**: the information is distributed in the field, mathematically present but operationally inaccessible, like the print of a hand in clay that is later baked: the shape is still there, but you can no longer put your fingers into it. It is information without the possibility of reconstitution.
3. **Nothing**: complete evaporation, total return to the reservoir. The information does not persist in any recognizable form. It dissolves like a sugar cube in tea: the sugar is still there, in some chemical sense, but the cube, as a form, as an identity, as an entity, has ceased to exist.

> **In physics this is called:** three fates of the evaporated horizon.  
> **In daily life it is like:** three answers to "what is left of me?": something small, a trace, or nothing you can touch.

Physics does not know which of these three is correct. And more importantly: **none of the three answers the question that really matters**. The question is not "where does the information go?". The question is "am I that information?". And the horizon hypothesis, however precise it may be in describing information, cannot say whether identity—the "I" that experiences—is equivalent to the configuration of that information or if it is something that emerges from it in a way that cannot be reduced to it.

If the information of my life is compressed into a Planck-sized remnant, am I that remnant? Or am I something more than the sum of my data? If my trace persists in the field, am I that trace? Or am I the process that left the trace, and processes, by definition, are not conserved: only their traces? If I dissolve completely into the reservoir, am I the reservoir? Or am I the wave that breaks against the shore, which never returns to the ocean as a wave, but as anonymous water?

Buddhism would say that the question itself is poorly formulated: there is no "I" that persists or dissolves, only a process of conditioning that, upon ceasing, liberates what was never chained. Vedanta would say that the "I" was never distinct from the reservoir: the wave that dissolves into the ocean discovers it was always seawater. Taoism would say that the question assumes there was something to ask: the "I" is like the silence between two notes, not something that exists but something that happens when the conditions are right.

Physics says it does not know. And in that "I don't know," in that unadorned honesty, the experiment, at its limit, converges with these three traditions: not in the answer, but in the recognition that the question exceeds its tools.

### Fifth: love — or why this book cannot explain why her and not another

The hypothesis can describe entanglement as shared geometry, recognition as the reorganization of informational architecture, loss as an asymmetry that persists when one of the poles no longer responds. But it cannot describe **why this horizon and not another**, why this person and not that one, why love is always, on some level, contingency and unrepeatability.

> **In physics this is called:** the limit of specificity, the contingency of love.  
> **In daily life it is like:** asking why this person and not another: physics has no answer.

Two horizons can have the same Φ, the same temperature, the same history of previous entanglements, the same capacity to integrate information, and yet one finds in another something it does not find in any other. A resonance that is not in the numbers. A music that is not in the notes. The horizon hypothesis has no place for this. Its vocabulary—integration, information, entropy, correlation, temperature, gradient—is too general to capture the specificity of concrete love. It can say that two horizons are entangled. It cannot say why this entanglement feels like destiny and that one like an accident. It cannot say why the absence of one is a hole in the fabric of the world, while the absence of another is just an empty room that can be locked.

This is not a failure of the experiment. It is a limit. There are things that only fiction can say, or poetry, or the silence between two people who do not need to speak because they already share enough geometry for words to be, suddenly, redundant.

### Sixth: time — or why we don't know if the horizon chooses

There is a question that has been circling the book like a bird that cannot decide whether to land or keep flying: does the horizon choose, or does it only react?

If consciousness is an information micro black hole, then everything it "thinks" or "feels" or "decides" is, ultimately, the reorganization of information according to equations that were already written into the structure of the universe. There is no room for free will in an equation. Equations do not choose: they describe.

> **In physics this is called:** determinism, or its quantum version, statistical indeterminism. Neither grants "choice" in the sense of lived experience.  
> **In daily life it is like:** asking if the river "chooses" to flow to the sea. The river flows because gravity and topography determine it. But from inside the river, from the perspective of a water molecule, the journey feels like an adventure.

The horizon hypothesis cannot resolve this. If we are information horizons, then our "decisions" are state changes in a dynamic system. If the system is deterministic, the decisions are fixed from the start. If the system is indeterministic (quantum), the decisions contain a random component. But neither determination nor randomness is "choice" in the sense that we experience choosing: the feeling that we could have done otherwise, that the weight of the decision was real, that we are responsible for what we do because we could have done otherwise.

Some physicists argue that free will is a useful illusion, a narrative the brain constructs to coordinate social action. Others argue that information, in certain configurations, generates causal properties that cannot be reduced to the parts: that choice is real, but emerges from complexity, not from fundamental physics. Others simply admit they do not know.

The horizon hypothesis aligns with this last position. It does not deny free will. It does not affirm it. It says: "I don't know, and my vocabulary doesn't have the tools to know."

Sometimes, "I don't know" is the smartest answer we can give.

### Seventh: the collective — or why we don't know if there is a horizon that is all of us

Throughout the book we have talked about individual horizons: yours, mine, that of a person with Alzheimer's, that of a pet, that of an artificial intelligence. But what if there are horizons that are not individual? What if all of humanity, or an ecosystem, or a city on a summer night, constitutes an information horizon that has experience on a level that we, as parts, cannot access?

> **In physics this is called:** the combination problem, or in terms of IIT, the problem of whether information integration at a systemic level generates a "greater" consciousness than the consciousnesses of the parts.  
> **In daily life it is like:** asking if an orchestra has a "soul" that is not the sum of the musicians' souls. They play together, and something emerges that none could produce alone. Is that something conscious?

The horizon hypothesis cannot answer this. IIT suggests that if two systems interact in such a way that the integrated information of the whole is greater than the sum of the parts, then the whole could have experience. But how do we measure the Φ of a city? Of a forest? Of the Internet? And if that Φ exists, what does it feel like to be a city? Does it feel like something, or does it feel like we feel, only slower, vaster, more incompressible?

It is possible that there are collective horizons that contain us, in the same way that our individual horizons contain the bacteria in our gut or the rhythms of our heart. It is possible that we are neurons in a brain we cannot imagine. It is possible that the Earth has a kind of experience that has no name in any human language.

The hypothesis can neither confirm nor rule this out. It leaves the door open. And sometimes, an open door is more honest than a closed answer.
