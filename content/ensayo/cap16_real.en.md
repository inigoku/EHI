title: THE WOUNDED HORIZON
section: PART THREE: THE LIMITS OF THE HORIZON
---

Up to this point, we have talked about individual horizons: how they are born, how they entangle, how they break. But there is one question missing that, once raised, cannot be postponed.

What happens when the asymmetry is not accidental—mother and child, a grieving process—but structural? When a system prevents two horizons from meeting under conditions of symmetry, not just for a day, but for generations.

A clarification before we continue. The horizon model does not force us to talk about politics. It allows us to. What follows is not an inevitable consequence of the physics of consciousness: it is a choice to extend the experiment into softer terrain. Anyone who prefers to keep consciousness and politics separate can skip this chapter without losing the thread.

But whoever decides to stay will find something that, in a way, they already intuited. Injustice is not just a matter of poorly distributed resources. It is a perturbation in the very geometry of the encounter. It is the impossibility of two consciousnesses resonating on an equal footing because one of them is surrounded by walls that the other cannot see.

The child who grows up in a tower of brick and glass and the child who grows up in a windowless room do not just have different possessions. They have different signal universes. Their horizons are formed with incommensurable materials. And when, decades later, they meet—in a courtroom, in a job interview, in a supermarket line—the original asymmetry continues to operate as an invisible force that determines who can speak and who will be heard.

## Power as a restriction of coupling

Power is not brute force. From the geometry of the horizon, it is the capacity to modify the integration rate of another without that other being able to return the gesture in equal measure.

Under conditions of symmetry, the arrow points in both directions. Each horizon can resonate, couple, and decouple. When there is power, the arrow is unilateral. One horizon determines what information reaches the other, at what speed, with what urgency. It can saturate it with noise or deprive it of signal.

The other cannot respond: not because they do not want to, but because the architecture of the relationship prevents it.

> **In physics this is called:** sustained coupling asymmetry.  
> **In daily life it is like:** a microphone that only works in one direction: one speaks, the other listens, but never the other way around.

A prison system, a relationship of domestic violence, a childhood in institutional neglect all share this same geometry. The subjugated horizon is prevented from responding. Its return signal is attenuated, intercepted, or punished. The one exercising power receives the other's signal as information to be processed or discarded, without their own architecture being exposed.

But asymmetry does not always arrive with a uniform and a baton. Sometimes it wears a tie and presents itself as efficiency. The precarious worker who cannot negotiate their hours, the nurse who has no time to listen to the patient, the student who passes by memorizing but never questioning: they all live under forms of power that are not called such, but which share the same topology. The signal ascends, dilutes, and is lost in bureaucratic ecosystems designed so that the response never arrives.

Let us imagine a company where the boss decides, without consulting, the strategy of a team. The employees can murmur, they can suggest, they can even protest. But the architecture of the relationship guarantees that their signal does not carry the same weight as that of the person who signs the contracts. It is not that the boss is evil: it is that the system was built so that their horizon absorbs information from the other without being forced to integrate it as its own. The pain of the fired employee does not penetrate the wall of their office. The precariousness of the temporary contract does not modify the stability of the permanent contract of the one who decides.

When the coupling is unilateral for years, something deeper than an isolated injustice occurs. The subjugated horizon learns to modulate its own signal. It learns to speak more softly, to anticipate rejection, to translate its own experience into the vocabulary of the powerful before articulating it. It is what social psychology calls "ideological capture": the internalization of an asymmetry to the point that the oppressed collaborates in their own silencing, not out of cowardice, but because their predictive model has learned that the authentic signal will not be integrated.

> **In physics this is called:** adaptive dissipation: a system that modifies its own dynamics to minimize the energy it loses in a forcefully asymmetric coupling.  
> **In daily life it is like:** someone walking against the wind who learns to lean forward: they do not want to lean, but the air resistance teaches them that this way they expend less strength.

## Poverty as signal deprivation

Subjective time—the density of integration—depends on the richness and variety of the input the horizon receives. From this follows something direct: an impoverished horizon is not just one with fewer resources. It is one to which the environment offers less integrable information per unit of time.

The child who grows up among books, adult conversations, instruments, and trips to the countryside receives high-density input. The one who grows up in multiple deprivation—confined space, restricted vocabulary, few new stimuli, chronic stress that is noise, not novelty—sees their horizon expand below what their biology would allow. Not because their brain has less capacity. Because the environment does not give them material to integrate.

> **In physics this is called:** sustained decoherent environment.  
> **In daily life it is like:** trying to grow a plant in a pot without light: the seed is good, but it has nothing to feed on.

The difference is not only quantitative. It is topological. The horizon of the poor child is not a smaller version of the rich child's horizon. It is of a different architecture: fewer long-range connections, a reward system calibrated for urgency, chronic permeability to threat.

Two twin brothers, separated at birth, one to a marginalized neighborhood and the other to an affluent one, will not just accumulate different knowledge. They will construct different *ways of being conscious*. The first will learn to detect danger before it manifests, to conserve energy, to not trust in continuity. The second will learn to postpone gratification, to explore, to assume that the world will cooperate with their projects. Both are rational adaptations to their environments. Neither is superior to the other in the abstract. But the world is designed by and for the second, so the adaptation of the first becomes, in that context, a "deficit."

The notion of "signal deprivation" allows us to separate something that is often confused: material poverty and informational poverty. A child may have food and shelter—material deprivation resolved—but grow up in an environment where no one speaks to them in a complex way, where the television is the only varied input, where the fear of eviction or domestic violence consumes all available attention. In that case, the horizon remains impoverished, not because it lacks calories, but because it lacks the kind of input that consciousness needs to become more complex.

It is not determinism. Plasticity exists, but it has a cost. Escaping poverty is not just about earning an income: it is about reconfiguring an architecture built over years under adverse conditions.

And here is the point that the horizon model illuminates with particular clarity: the cost of that reconfiguration is not just economic. It is informational. Whoever escapes poverty must learn to process signals they never had to integrate before, to inhibit responses that were previously adaptive, to trust in stabilities that their predictive model does not anticipate. It is like learning a new language at thirty: possible, but costly, and never with the fluency of someone who learned it as a child.

## Racism as the denial of legibility

Racism is not just prejudice. It is, furthermore, a systematic failure of legibility: the inability or refusal of one horizon to model another with the precision its complexity requires.

When a judicial system assigns hostility to gestures that in another context it would read as neutral, or when an employer fails to interpret the signals of a candidate of another ethnicity, there is something more than ill will. There is a degradation of the predictive model induced by the bias of the training environment.

The brain models others with variable precision depending on familiarity. If the social environment is segregated, if the media overrepresents certain groups as threats, if real interactions are scarce or loaded with power asymmetry, the predictive model degrades. Not because the horizon is bad, but because the corpus it was trained on is biased.

This has a precise consequence: racism is, in part, a collective informational failure. And like all collective failures, it is not corrected solely with individual goodwill. It is corrected by modifying the training environment: real, sustained interactions, under conditions of sufficient symmetry so that predictive models recalibrate.

The literature on intergroup contact has already demonstrated this empirically. What the horizon model adds is an explanation of the mechanism: repeated contact in symmetry reconstructs legibility because it forces the model to correct its bias with new data. Without coupling symmetry, contact does not recalibrate. It can reinforce the bias.

Think of the security guard who follows a young Black man through the aisles of a shopping mall. His predictive model, trained on hundreds of media representations, assigns a high probability to hostility. The real signals—the way of walking, the tone of voice, the gaze—are read through that filter. A neutral gesture becomes suspicious. An irritated response to the surveillance becomes confirmation of danger. The loop closes: the model generates the behavior it predicts, because the prediction itself modifies the behavior of the observed.

> **In physics this is called:** observer-observed feedback: the measurement distorts the measured system.  
> **In daily life it is like:** someone who believes their partner is unfaithful and, by spying on them, generates the distance they later interpret as proof.

The denial of legibility has effects that transcend the isolated interaction. Whoever is systematically modeled poorly learns to model themselves with the categories of the one looking. The young person whom society reads as a threat can internalize that reading, not because it is true, but because it is the only version of themselves they receive integrated by others. The horizon of the other functions, in a way, as a mirror: if all mirrors return a distorted image, you end up believing that your true face is the distortion.

## Democracy as coupling design

If power is sustained asymmetry, justice is the aspiration that no horizon be systematically prevented from resonating. Politics, then, is not exhausted in the management of resources. It is the design of the geometry of collective coupling.

A functional democracy guarantees two things. First: channels for upward signal. Voting, a free press, and protest are signal technologies. They do not guarantee that the signal will be heard, but they are a necessary condition for it to be possible.

Second: responsiveness. Accountability mechanisms, periodic elections, public scrutiny. That distinguishes democratic asymmetry from authoritarian asymmetry: in a democracy, the subaltern horizon can modify—slowly, partially, by aggregation—the architecture of the horizon of power.

> **In physics this is called:** maximization of network integration flow.  
> **In daily life it is like:** an electrical grid where each node can send current toward the center, not just receive it.

It is best not to exaggerate. A society is not a horizon. It was not born, it did not cross any threshold of emergence. It lacks the integrated unity that defines a horizon and, therefore, its own consciousness. To attribute collective Φ to a society would be to confuse the measure of integration with the existence of a subject.

What can be said, in a modest way, is that democracy tends to maximize the flow of information between the horizons of a network. Democracies tend to be more stable and innovative not because their citizens are better, but because their geometry lets more signal flow from more nodes.

When the channels of upward signal atrophy—abstention, disinformation, media concentration—or when responsiveness is corrupted, the geometry degenerates into a functional oligarchy. The system continues to call itself a democracy, but its topology is that of a soft dictatorship.

Here the horizon model offers a useful distinction: not all asymmetry is tyrannical. A doctor knows more than a patient, a judge more than a litigant. But those asymmetries are functional, not structural. The doctor must respond to the patient's pain. The judge must give public reasons. There is a return channel, albeit unequal, that prevents the asymmetry from crystallizing into domination. Democracy does not demand perfect symmetry—impossible in any complex organization—but rather that no asymmetry be armored against the return signal.

The contemporary problem is perhaps not the classic dictatorship, but diffuse asymmetry. Algorithms that decide what news we see, platforms that monetize attention without accountability, corporations that operate in jurisdictions where no one can demand a response from them. The individual citizen does not face an identifiable oppressor. They face a network of asymmetries so entangled that the signal of protest dissipates before reaching a destination where it can be integrated.

> **In physics this is called:** distributed damping: the energy of a perturbation disperses in a network so complex that the original oscillation extinguishes without provoking a coherent response.  
> **In daily life it is like:** shouting in a soundproof room: the sound exists, but it does not bounce, it reaches no ear, it produces no echo.

## Collective trauma as shared geometry

Individual trauma is encapsulated information that distorts the geometry of the horizon. Collective trauma—wars, genocides, slavery—operates in an analogous way, but on the scale of populations.

When a society has lived through an event that surpassed the integration capacity of all its horizons at once, what remains is not just a collection of traumatized individuals. It is a field of damaged correlations that persists across generations.

The children of survivors, the grandchildren of the enslaved, do not inherit the trauma biologically—although epigenetics suggests possible mechanisms—but they do inherit the coupling environment in which they grow up. Silences, overprotections, hypervigilance: all of this configures the architecture of the new horizons before they can question it.

Let us imagine a community that lived through a dictatorship. The adults who survived it learned not to trust, not to name, to read between the lines. That caution, rational at the time, is transmitted to the children not as doctrine, but as atmosphere. The child grows up in a home where joy is always a little guilty, where trust in institutions is a naivety that is paid for dearly, where the father's silence at dinner says more than a thousand warnings. They did not need to live through the repression to inherit its geometry: it was enough for them to grow up in the gravitational field that the repression left behind.

Truth commissions, memory museums, and public rituals of reparation are not merely symbolic gestures. They are operations of collective decapsulation: attempts to circulate information that was trapped so that it can be integrated instead of continuing to distort from the underground.

The difficulty is that opening the capsule carries cost and risk. It can flood the system with more information than it can integrate at once. That is why transitional justice processes are so fragile, so often flawed. The model does not offer recipes. It offers a way to understand why they fail.

There are collective traumas that do not stem from a single event, but from an extended condition. Colonization was not one day of violence: it was centuries of sustained asymmetry that reconfigured the horizons of colonizers and colonized in ways so profound that they persist when the flags have already changed. The enslaved person did not just lose freedom: they lost the possibility of their signal being integrated by the system surrounding them. And that denial of integration, repeated for generations, built architectures of consciousness that legal abolition cannot dissolve overnight.

> **In physics this is called:** hysteresis: a system retains the imprint of its history even when external conditions return to their original state.  
> **In daily life it is like:** a wooden door that warped from humidity and continues to fit poorly even though the weather has already changed.

## Care as the repair of coupling

The previous chapter spoke of the caregiver: the one who sustains another's horizon when it contracts. Here we can extend that intuition. If injustice is sustained coupling asymmetry, care is the practice of reestablishing symmetry, even if it is local, even if it is temporary.

The caregiver does not correct the structure. They cannot abolish poverty, eliminate racism, or heal collective trauma. But they can create, in the space of the relationship, a different geometry. They can make their horizon available to receive the other's signal with the same intensity with which they emit their own.

This is what a good educator in an impoverished neighborhood does: they do not just transmit content, but offer a horizon that resonates with the child's, that returns their own signal with enough fidelity so that the child can integrate it. It is what a community therapist, an intercultural mediator, or a social worker who listens before classifying does. They are figures who do not resolve structural injustice, but who repair, relationship by relationship, the possibility of coupling.

> **In physics this is called:** local resonant coupling in a globally dissipative regime.  
> **In daily life it is like:** lighting a candle in a cold room: it does not heat the house, but it allows two people to see each other.

The horizon model allows us to say something that political rhetoric sometimes forgets: justice is not just the distribution of goods. It is the distribution of attention. It is the possibility that your signal reaches another horizon and is integrated, not processed as noise. The caregiver, in this sense, is the prototype of what a politics of the horizon might aspire to: not equality of outcomes, but equality of resonance.

## What this chapter is not

For the most part, this chapter does not discover: it reframes. That poverty damages development, that intergroup contact in equality reduces prejudice, that collective trauma is transmitted through the environment—all of this was already known by developmental psychology, social psychology, and memory studies. What the model offers is a common vocabulary: a way of seeing power, poverty, racism, and trauma as variants of the same phenomenon—the geometry of coupling—instead of as separate problems.

On a few points, it risks something more. The distinction between material deprivation and integrable signal deprivation. The prediction that coupling symmetry is what makes intergroup contact recalibrate. If those claims are false, the model is superfluous here. If they are true, it contributes.

There are no experiments that measure the integration flow of a society. There are no studies that correlate coupling asymmetries with inequality. What there is, is a way of organizing questions that until now were asked with a moral or economic vocabulary, and which might benefit from an informational one.

The wounded horizon is not just the individual with trauma or addiction. It is also the community that could not process its history, the class prevented from emitting its signal, the group that was not modeled with the precision it deserved. The experiment does not cure those wounds. At most, it names them differently. And naming, even if it is not enough, is sometimes where something begins.

Naming allows us to see patterns where before there was only moral chaos. It allows us to ask, for example, if a public policy should not only redistribute income, but redistribute signal: if children from impoverished environments need not only more food, but more conversations, more rhythms, more spaces where their horizons can resonate with other horizons that are not in survival mode. It allows us to ask if racial justice is not just punishing discrimination, but redesigning the training environments where predictive models are formed. It allows us to ask if the reparation of collective trauma is not just remembering, but creating the conditions so that trapped information can circulate without destroying the system that receives it.

These questions do not have easy answers. The horizon model does not resolve them: it makes them visible as problems of geometry, not just of morality. And in a world where morality has often become a weapon to be hurled, perhaps it is useful to have a language that describes before it prescribes, that measures before it condemns.
