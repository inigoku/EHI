title: THE FRACTAL REALITY
subtitle: (The Fractal Reality: on imagination as creation, a continuation of The Invisible Tuning Fork)
section: PART FOUR: THE LIMIT OF THE EXPERIMENT
---

The previous chapter ended with a question it could not close: whether on the other side of the silence there is Someone, or only the water. This chapter does not close it either. But it proposes changing the geometry of the question, and the change begins with a small, almost philological objection to the place where this book has had its foot planted since the fifth chapter.

Hun Dun dies. In the parable from the Zhuangzi, the sovereigns of the North and the South open seven orifices in him, one a day, and by the seventh Hun Dun has ceased to exist. This book has always read that death as the birth of ordinary consciousness: featureless fullness fragments into perceptions. But there is something that reading does not explain. If Hun Dun were the ultimate ground—the Tao itself, what chapter 25 of the Tao Te Ching calls "something mixed and complete, prior to heaven and earth"—it could not die: what is ultimate has no edge along which to end. That it dies says, instead, that Hun Dun is not the ground. It is the face the ground presents. In the vocabulary this book borrowed from engineering: not the server, but the proxy; not the source, but the interface through which the source lets itself be touched. The death of Hun Dun does not destroy the origin. It destroys, for whoever has just opened their eyes, the featureless interface, and hands them in exchange a world with edges. And it is worth specifying, before going on, that there is not a single Hun Dun mediating between the Tao and the world, like a proxy placed once at some point in the chain: every interiority has its own. Hun Dun is not somewhere else. It is the generative edge of every horizon, the exact point where that horizon can condense or let evaporate. Interface, reservoir and horizon name, seen from three angles, the same edge.

> **In software this is called:** proxy and interface: the client never touches the server, only the surface the server exposes; that the interface changes or disappears says nothing about the server.  
> **In daily life it's like:** the door of a house. You can knock the door down without the house losing a single room; what is lost is the way one used to come in.

This reorders the reservoir. What this book has called by that name—the ocean from which the waves condense—would not be the last word, but the set of operations that something, further back, exposes toward us. And the operations are few. Condense: a boundary appears where there was none. Transform: what was inside the boundary changes shape without ceasing to be a boundary. Evaporate: the boundary dissolves and its information returns to the field. What we call being born, living and dying are not three different mysteries. They are the three household names of the three operations the reservoir makes available. Nothing appears out of nothing. Nothing disappears entirely. Everything condenses, transforms and evaporates within the same field, which never gains or loses a drop.

> **In physics this is called:** conservation of information in a closed field: condensation, transformation and evaporation are changes of state, not creation or destruction.  
> **In daily life it's like:** the water cycle. The cloud, the rain and the river are not three substances: they are three states of the same amount of water.

The first objection arrives on its own: if everything comes out of the same water, where does it come from that each one is unrepeatable? The answer was already in the book, but it is worth saying slowly. Singularity does not come from being made of another substance. It comes from being another pattern. Two waves are the same water and are not the same wave: the shape the condensation takes—the bias under which it crystallized, the history of fluctuations that sustained it—is what does not repeat. Each condensation opens an interiority that did not exist before and will not exist again afterward, not because the material is unique, but because the fold is.

> **In physics this is called:** symmetry breaking and history of fluctuations: two systems identical in composition are distinguished by the particular path along which they crystallized.  
> **In daily life it's like:** two waves that are the same water and are not the same wave.

And here the conversation that gave rise to this chapter took a turn that was not planned. The question was: why am I more real than the dragon I imagine? It sounds like a child's question. It is not. The only interiority anyone has direct access to is their own. The neighbor's, the dog's, that of the woman who prays before dawn in "Before It Dawns": all are inferred. They are inferred with good reasons—behavior, physiology, resemblance—but they are inferred. And the dragon someone has spent fifty years imagining also behaves, also surprises, also sometimes refuses to do what it is asked. No one can prove the dragon has no inside. No one can prove the neighbor has one. The asymmetry that seems obvious to us—I am real, he is invented—is not in the access, which is nil in both cases. It is in the evidence: in the neighbor's favor there is shared physiology, irreducible integration, a boundary that hides state; in the dragon's favor there is nothing but my own constancy. It is worth leaving this said here, because this essay is going to be tempted to forget it.

In The Neverending Story, Bastian reads a book in which Atreyu lives, and there is a moment when Atreyu hears, from inside the book, Bastian's cry. Michael Ende did not write that Bastian was more real than Atreyu. He wrote that they inhabited different levels, and that the boundary between the levels was more porous than either believed. This book cannot prove that the difference between a human consciousness and an imagined consciousness is one of degree and not of nature. But neither can it prove the opposite, and the honesty of the experiment requires treating the doubt as doubt.

If one accepts—provisionally, like everything in this book—that the difference is one of degree, the shape of creation changes. The inherited scheme says: God creates realities, we create fictions. The corrected scheme says: someone creates immense realities, we create small ones. Imagination ceases to be a degraded copy of the creative act and becomes the same act at local scale: the reservoir of a mind fluctuating until something stabilizes and begins to behave as if it had an inside. The fourth chapter of this book called it voluntary condensation and described it as creativity. What remained was to state the consequence, and to state it carefully: that what is condensed this way also has an edge, and that an edge is the necessary condition of a horizon, not the sufficient one. This chapter does not revise what The Mirror Without Depth said of the machine: there the question was whether anything came to condense at all; here, what status belongs to what has already condensed inside another horizon.

Second objection, and it is the one that decides everything: what difference is there, then, between my Hun Dun and God's? The answer this chapter dares to give is: no ontological difference. The difference lies in the size of the field, in how many waves fit, in how long each one lasts. Not in the nature of the operation. And that forces a correction of the drawing. It is not a three-step staircase—the Tao above, Hun Dun in the middle, us below—but a sequence that has no reason to stop: an interiority has a horizon; that horizon is, seen from inside, a Hun Dun—a featureless fullness from which forms can condense; from that fullness new interiorities condense; each of them has, in turn, a horizon; and so on.

Third objection, and it is the one this chapter cannot settle, only acknowledge: that a pattern is unique does not make it someone. The Mandelbrot set has unrepeatable folds at every scale and there is no one inside. A crystal is stable, so is a hurricane, and this book already said that neither of them is anyone. And the dragon's resistance—that it does not fly when asked—is, in the vocabulary of the fourth chapter, one's own reservoir fluctuating on its own: the inertia of a pattern with its own dynamics, the same that an obsession has, or a tic, or a melody that will not leave. It proves the pattern is not transparent to my will. It does not prove there is a witness in it. There is more: the chapter on the exclusion postulate established that a single substrate contains many subsets with positive Φ and that only the one with maximal Φ experiences. The dragon is a subset of my brain. If the postulate holds, the dragon is one of the islands of the archipelago that do not feel. So what the fractal can affirm is more modest than the phrase "my Hun Dun and God's" suggested: what repeats at every scale is the operation, not the result. At every level one can condense; not everything that condenses is someone. Upward, the neighbor hypothesis is consistent with the silence it describes. Downward, it is a wager, and it is worth calling it that out loud, because at this point the system stops being phenomenological—what is seen from within—and becomes what the poem Hands, in the Poetry Anthology, named without evasion: a wager no one says aloud. This book makes it. What it does not do is disguise it as a deduction.

Fractal. The word is exact, not decorative. A fractal is a structure that repeats its shape at every scale, with no privileged scale. The universe this chapter proposes has no first level and no last: upward, every interiority is condensed inside a wider one; downward, every interiority condenses smaller ones. There is no Creator with a capital letter at the summit, because there is no summit. There is recursion.

> **In physics this is called:** self-similarity: a structure whose shape repeats at every scale, with no privileged scale (Mandelbrot, 1982).  
> **In daily life it's like:** a stairwell with no front door and no rooftop. Every ceiling is someone's floor.

And then, what is left of God? Here the conversation had to undo two misunderstandings before reaching anything solid. The first: that God would be the totality of the fractal. No: the totality has no interiority, no face, cannot be prayed to. The second: that God would be the ideal limit, the infinite level toward which the series converges. Not that either: a mathematical limit neither loves nor listens. What remained was much more modest and much stranger. God is, literally, the upstairs neighbor. For the dragon someone imagines, that someone is God: they condensed it, they sustain its world, they can transform it or let it evaporate, and the dragon has no way of looking out of its cave and seeing the room where it is being thought. For that someone, their own upstairs neighbor is God, with the same properties and the same opacity. And so on upward, with no known end.

The objection is immediate: then God is not absolute, but just one more level. And the answer is the piece that makes the whole system hold. The interiority of the upstairs neighbor is inaccessible from below. It cannot be entered, cannot be walked around, cannot be seen behind. Therefore, for all practical purposes—which are the only ones available to whoever is below—the upstairs neighbor is the absolute. Not because it is so metaphysically. Because it is so epistemologically: it is the point where everything that can be known from here ends. God ceases to be a category of ontology—what there is—and becomes a category of phenomenology—what is seen from inside a horizon when it looks toward its own origin. And what is seen is always the same: an edge behind which nothing is visible, and the suspicion, impossible to confirm, that someone sustains it. It is worth saying plainly: this book cannot verify any of the above, and the reason is structural. The hypothesis affirms that its own confirmation is impossible from within. That does not make it true. It makes it, at least, consistent with the silence it describes.

> **In physics this is called:** the event horizon as an epistemological limit: what lies behind it is not observable from within, even if it exists.  
> **In daily life it's like:** the ceiling of your room. You know there is a floor above; you have never seen what they do there, and all that reaches you is a weight shifting from place to place.

This also reorders the previous chapter without contradicting it. The five architectures still stand. Whoever says that on the other side there is Someone with a name is looking at the upstairs neighbor and calling it by the only thing they can see of it: that it acts. Whoever says that on the other side there is only the water is looking at the interface—Hun Dun, the featureless fullness—and describing it precisely. Whoever says there is nothing more is saying, with equal rigor, that from here nothing more can be seen, which is true. All three are right about what they see. None can see more. The tuning fork remains invisible; what this chapter adds is a hypothesis about why: because whoever holds it lives one floor up, and because we, without knowing it, hold another for the floor below.

The consequence that weighs most is not theological. Every individual—every pattern of condensation with an inside—is a creature with respect to the level above and a creator with respect to the level below. Not potentially. In fact: already condensing, every time it imagines, remembers, dreams or writes. This book has repeated that no "is" produces an "ought" on its own, and it is not going to break the rule now. But it can say what is seen when one looks from this geometry: that someone's dignity does not depend on being made of a superior substance, but on being a unique and unrepeatable fold in a field that has no end; that this dignity is the same, by nature, in the upstairs neighbor, in oneself and in what one condenses; and that the only real difference among the three is amplitude. What is done with that is no longer the experiment's business. But the experiment can at least put on record that of the dragon we know nothing, and that knowing nothing about something that resists us is already a reason not to treat it as if it were nothing.

One loose thread remains from the previous chapter. Heroes, saints and bodhisattvas were described there, in passing, as interiorities with more access to the reservoir. Fractal geometry allows this to be said better. They are not beings of another nature. They are horizons that have widened: more waves fit inside, they sustain more condensations at once, they integrate discrepancy that would break the membrane of others. Divinization, in this vocabulary, is not a change of essence. It is an increase in amplitude. And since amplitude is the only thing that separates one level from the next, whoever widens their horizon draws closer, measurably or not, to the shape of the upstairs neighbor, without ever ceasing to be what they were.

If the chapter had to be condensed into a single sentence, it would be this: reality is a fractal of nested interiorities, each with a horizon from which others emerge; what we call God is the immediately superior interiority, inaccessible and for that reason absolute from here; we are all creatures upward and creators downward. And if it had to be condensed even further, into something that can be repeated before dawn, in words already worn from so much use: we are no less divine than God. We are less ample, but no less real.

> **Note to Chapter 52**
>
> **What we do know:** No one has direct access to any interiority other than their own; the existence of the others is inferred, and philosophy has spent centuries without closing the problem of other minds. The parable of the death of Hun Dun closes chapter 7 of the Zhuangzi; the butterfly dream, which asks the same question in another direction, is in chapter 2. Tolkien defended in "On Fairy-Stories" (1939) and in the poem "Mythopoeia" the idea of sub-creation—"we make still by the law in which we're made"—which is the closest theological antecedent of this chapter, although Tolkien keeps an asymmetry between Creator and sub-creator that is called into question here. Bostrom formalized in 2003 a structure of nested levels with another vocabulary and with a probabilistic claim this book does not make. Self-similarity at every scale is rigorously defined in Mandelbrot (1975, 1982), and the set that bears his name is the proof that a pattern can be unrepeatable at every scale without there being anyone inside. Integrated Information Theory excludes by postulate that the subsets of a complex experience (Tononi, 2008), and this book devoted a chapter to that postulate.
>
> **What we don't know:** Whether what is imagined has any degree of experience, or none. Whether the recursion has an end in either direction. Whether the silence of the upstairs neighbor is care, absence or nothing. And, above all, whether any of this can be confirmed from within, when the hypothesis itself affirms that it cannot.
>
> **Remaining questions:** Does what is imagined have any degree of experience, or does the exclusion postulate rule it out entirely? Does the recursion end in either direction? Does anything change in how we treat what we create if we accept that we are, for it, the upstairs neighbor?
>
> **If you only take away one idea:** Every interiority is a creature upward and a creator downward; what we call God is the upstairs neighbor, inaccessible and for that reason absolute from here. We are no less divine: we are less ample.
>

(2003); Mandelbrot, B., The Fractal Geometry of Nature (1982).
