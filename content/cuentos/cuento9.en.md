---
title: THE EDGE WE CROSS EVERY NIGHT
---

---

There is a researcher who has spent years gathering testimonies about the edge.

Not the edge in a metaphorical sense. The literal edge, the one of every night: the seconds or minutes that mediate between being awake and being asleep. The transition. The moment when the system that runs the day lets go and something else takes over, or does not take over exactly, but rather taking over is no longer necessary because the one who was taking over has ceased to be there.

She calls it the edge because no other name seems sufficiently exact to her. *The transition* is too clinical. *The threshold* has architectural connotations that do not quite fit. *The edge* describes what it is: a boundary that is crossed, every night, in both directions. Without anyone quite knowing how.

---

What she has gathered, in years of asking, is this:

**Mireia, 34 years old**: *I feel like I am falling. Not toward any specific place. Just downward, toward myself, and at some point in the falling there is no longer anyone who continues to fall.*

**Tomàs, 67 years old**: *I see things. Images I haven't asked for and that make no sense together. A hand on a table. A bird I have never seen. A number. The images do not frighten me but I do not understand them either. I let them pass.*

**Noa, 8 years old**: *It gives me a fright and I wake up. Then I go back to sleep. That happens several times. In the end I fall asleep for real.*

**Àngels, 51 years old**: *I don't notice anything. I am awake and then I am asleep and between the two things there is nothing I can remember.*

**Jorge, 29 years old**: *It is the best part of the day. The moment when what I thought before stops mattering.*

---

The researcher has a hypothesis that she has not published because it is not the kind of hypothesis that gets published.

Her hypothesis is that the edge is the practice.

Not the practice of anything in particular. The practice of the edge itself: the repeated experience of letting go. Of letting the system that maintains the continuity of the self—tomorrow's plans, one's own name, the list of what remains to be done, the thread that connects the self of ten minutes ago with the self of now—relax enough for something else to happen. Something else that has no precise name because the one experiencing it is in no condition to take notes at that moment.

We do it every night. We have been doing it all our lives. Most of the time we do not notice it.

---

She notices it because she studies it. And studying it has changed what she experiences at the edge.

She lies down, closes her eyes, waits. The moment arrives when the images begin—hers are landscapes that fold in on themselves, or the invented memory of a house with too many doors—and then she knows she is close. That the edge is only a few meters away.

What she does is remain still. Not resisting, not rushing. Just being in the moment when the two things coexist: the thought still connected and the images that no longer belong to thought. Both at the same time, briefly.

She does not always succeed. Sometimes thought wins and she returns to the day, to the list, to the thread. Sometimes the images win and she is already asleep before she notices it.

When she succeeds—that second of both at the same time—she describes it as the stillest moment of the day. Not empty. Still in a different way: as if everything she is were there, complete, gathered, before reorganizing into something more diffuse.

---

What she does not know, and which is the question that keeps her on the project, is what exactly it is that returns in the morning.

Not in a philosophical sense. In a practical sense: the person who wakes up at a quarter past seven has the same memories, the same name, the same incomplete plans as the one who fell asleep at half past eleven. The continuity seems intact.

But between half past eleven and a quarter past seven there was something that was not that person, or that was that person in a completely different way, and of that there is no record. Not because it did not happen. But because the one who could record it was not available to do so.

Every morning we return from a place of which we can recount nothing. And we do so with the naturalness of things that have always been this way.

That they are does not make them any less strange.

---

The last testimony the researcher gathered for her archive was from an eighty-two-year-old woman who did not want to give her name.

*Why does that moment interest you?* the researcher asked her.

*Because it is the only moment of the day when I do not have to be me,* the woman said. *The rest of the day I have to be me. That moment, no.*

The researcher wrote it down. Then she thought about it for a while without writing it down.

Then she wrote that down too: that some answers are too exact to keep asking questions on top of them.
