---
title: THE HOUSE THAT BREATHED
---

---

The house found them first.

It wasn't that they were looking for it. Or rather, they were, but in the vague way one looks for things one doesn't yet know one needs: looking at photos on websites at eleven at night, calculating mortgages on napkins, telling themselves it wasn't the right time yet and returning to the listings anyway.

The house was at the end of a street that terminated in a park. First floor. Thick walls, the kind of walls that are cool in summer and hold the heat in winter. The agency broker talked about the year of construction, the quiet community, the orientation. Marta and Jordi nodded. They weren't really listening. They were looking.

They couldn't say what convinced them. That was also part of it.

---

The first months were the usual chaos of moving: boxes that never found their final place, furniture bought in moments of optimism that later didn't fit, the simultaneous sensation that there was too much space and not enough. They cooked on the floor the first few days because the kitchen didn't have a table yet. They argued about where to put the bookshelf. They reconciled over the bookshelf issue in the same way seven years of cohabitation reconcile: with the efficiency of someone who already knows the way back.

The bookshelf went on the north wall. It probably wasn't the best place. It looked fine.

---

The first thing they noticed was the steam.

The bathroom window—the small one, the one facing the interior courtyard—would fog up in a way that didn't entirely correspond to the shower. Marta discovered it one Sunday morning: they had been talking in bed for hours, in that way Sunday mornings have when there is no rush, and when she went to the bathroom the window was covered in steam even though no one had showered yet.

She told Jordi. He went to look. He agreed: the window was fogged up.

—It must be the heating —he said—. Or the humidity from the courtyard.

—Of course —Marta said.

Neither of them mentioned it again.

---

It took them time to realize the house had rhythms.

Not schedules, not exactly. Rather, states. There were moments when the apartment seemed larger than usual: the hallway, which was narrow, felt comfortable; the light in the living room reached corners that normally remained in shadow. And there were moments—not many, but enough to notice them—when the house shrank a little. Not physically: the walls didn't move. But the ceilings seemed lower and the hallway harder to navigate.

Marta had a theory she never fully formulated: that the states of the house corresponded to their own. Or more exactly, to those of the two of them together. That when they were fine, the house was fine. That when there was tension—the days leading up to arguments, that accumulation of silences that precedes an outburst—the house gathered it somewhere in its walls.

She didn't say it because it sounded strange. And because saying it out loud would have required also saying when they were in tension, which was information that was sometimes easier not to name.

Other times, however, the condensation appeared on the bathroom window on a Tuesday afternoon when absolutely nothing had happened. Marta would look at it, wipe her hand across to erase it, and not understand what it meant. It probably didn't mean anything. That also disconcerted her.

---

The first real argument—not the disagreement about the bookshelf, which was just a breaking-in, but the first one that had something underneath it—was in the second winter.

It was about work. Marta had made a decision that affected them both without fully consulting him. Or she had consulted him, but in that way sometimes used to consult when in reality it has already been decided. Jordi knew it. He said so. The conversation escalated.

It was long. It had three or four phases. The last was in the kitchen, at eleven at night, with the lights on and the January cold on the windowpanes.

And at some point in that last phase, Marta realized she was cold. Not from the January cold—the radiator was working—but a specific coldness in the kitchen air, as if the heating had been off for a while even though it wasn't.

She didn't mention it. It wasn't the time.

That night they slept in the same bed but apart. The next day Jordi brought flowers, which was a habit of his that irritated Marta a little because it was such a predictable gesture that it lost half its meaning, and yet she liked it. They talked. They arrived at the place they usually arrived at, which was an imperfect but familiar place.

That night the kitchen was warm.

Marta didn't write it down anywhere. But she kept it.

---

Jordi traveled for work for three weeks in the spring. It wasn't unusual; his job had those periods. Marta stayed in the house.

The house was different alone.

Not unsettling. Not empty. Different in a way that was hard to describe with precision, like when you change the arrangement of the furniture and for days your body keeps expecting to bump into the chair that is no longer there.

The bathroom window didn't fog up.

Marta noticed it the second morning and didn't know what to do with that information. The showers were just as long. The interior courtyard was the same. What had changed was what filled the rooms when she wasn't looking.

Jordi called every night. They talked about the usual: his work, her work, whether the neighbor on the third floor had left his bicycle in the entryway again. Small things that served as a thread. Marta didn't tell him about the window. She didn't know how to formulate it without it sounding like a complaint, or like something it wasn't.

---

The night Jordi returned, he arrived late. Marta was already in bed but not sleeping. She heard the key in the lock, the footsteps in the hallway, the sound of him leaving his suitcase where he always left it. Then the silence of someone trying not to make noise because they think the other is asleep.

—I'm awake —Marta said.

Jordi entered the room. He sat on the edge of the bed. He smelled of airplanes and exhaustion. Marta didn't turn on the light.

They didn't say anything for a while. He put his hand on her shoulder. She let it be.

It was late and they were both tired and there was nothing urgent to say. The walls of the room were warm. Through the window came the noise of the park at the end of the street, which was a noise of trees moved by the wind and nothing else.

The bathroom window, Marta checked in the morning, was fogged up.

---

They lived in that house for twelve years.

They left it for ordinary reasons: a job opportunity in another city, the logic of the changes that arrive when you are no longer who you were when you made the previous decision. They packed boxes with the efficiency that practice gives. The bookshelf was the last to be dismantled.

On the last day, alone now in the empty rooms, Marta walked through the apartment one last time. Not to check that they hadn't forgotten anything—that was already done. For something else she couldn't entirely name.

The walls were cold. It made sense: the heating had been off for three days.

In the bathroom, the small window was clean and dry.

Marta looked at it for a moment. Then she went out and closed the door.

They never talked about it, she and Jordi. About the house. Or perhaps they tried once, one night of the many they had afterward, and couldn't find the right words, which sometimes happens. Or perhaps there were things that lived better without being named, which is something twelve years teach you if you listen to them.

They kept the key longer than necessary. They both remembered that.
