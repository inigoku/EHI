---
title: THE TUNERS
---

*It wasn't in the archive. It arrived loose, without an exact date, wedged between two chapters that do not touch: a chronicle of the dry lagoon of Tarel and a police report from Barcelona with the letterhead crossed out. I don't know who stitched them together. I leave them united because, read separately, they mean nothing; read together, they beat.*

---

### I. The one who closes the circuit
*Tarel, 1620*

Mateu was nine years old the first time he felt a dead man breathe underwater.

It wasn't a ghost. It was a texture: a fine vibration on the surface of the lagoon, as if someone had plucked a string buried in the mud. The body had been under the silt for three days—an old fisherman, drowned in his own boat, drunk on wine and exhaustion—and Mateu, who had only gone looking for tadpoles, stood with his hand submerged in the cold water until his mother dragged him out, trembling, with a phrase in his mouth that was not his own: *“Tell my daughter the knot is on the third post.”*

The knot was on the third post. No one knew how he knew.

The Council of Tarel called this *tuning*, and treated it with the same sullen respect given to midwives and tide-readers. Mateu was raised for the trade just as others were raised for the net or the oar. They taught him that a person's horizon—that which sustained them inside while they lived—does not disappear all at once when the body stops. It dissolves slowly, like salt in water, leaving a trace on the surface for a time: a drawing that is no longer anyone but still holds the shape of someone. Tuning was reading that drawing before the mixture erased it completely. And then—this was the important part—letting it go. Helping it mix. Closing the circuit that death had left open, so that the one who left could truly leave.

—We are not thieves of what people leave behind —old Bernat, who had trained him, used to say—. We are the ones who sweep the doorway when the guest has already gone.

Mateu was twenty-six when the ships from the Continent arrived, and with them, a man who swept in a different way.

His name was Gaff. He didn't speak of tuning; he spoke of *extracting*. He dressed like the delegates—clothes that never learned to get wet—but his hands, when he finally showed them to Mateu in a forced aside by the delegation, had the same fine tremor, the same sensitivity to water. A tuner. The first Mateu had ever met who was not from Tarel.

—You read to say goodbye —Gaff told him, the first night, with the delegation's wine open between them like an uncomfortable truce—. We read to collect. Every dead man is a file someone closed ahead of time. I reopen it.

—A dead man is not a file.

—A dead man is exactly a file. —Gaff smiled without malice, which was worse than if he had any—. And files, my friend, have owners.

Gaff did not wait for the time of dissolution. He forced the reading before the dead man's horizon finished mixing, plunging his hand—Mateu could find no other word—into a wound that still bled information. And wherever Gaff read, the water was left wrong: a scar in the geometry of the place, a whirlpool that shouldn't be there.

The delegation was looking for a name: that of the wise man who, according to the oldest archives of Tarel, had known how to *sustain the water*—to halt, even for a time, the retreat that was emptying the lagoon. He had died two winters ago, and his horizon had spent that time dissolving slowly on the northern shore, where no one fished anymore.

Gaff went for him with open hands and without waiting. Mateu arrived when he was already inside.

There is no clean way to tell what he saw. Let us say that Gaff had his arm submerged in the water up to the shoulder, and that the water, around his arm, boiled without being hot. The wise man had not finished dissolving. And in the center of that half-made mixture there was only one recognizable thing, clenched like a fist: not a treasure, not a map. A phrase. A single phrase that the wise man had taken with him whole, without distributing it to anyone, precisely so that no one could tear it from him like this.

Mateu did not get to hear it. That was what he decided.

He did the only thing he knew how to do and that Gaff did not know or did not want to do: instead of pulling toward himself, he pushed inward. An asymmetrical, unilateral coupling, with no turning back: he thrust his own horizon—alive, whole—against the wise man's residue, and wrapped it, and took it into his own structure before Gaff could close his hand.

It cost what Bernat had always said it would cost to do that alive and not dead: a tear that does not close. Mateu came out of the water with the wise man's phrase hidden in some fold of himself that he himself could not read, and with a new and permanent tension behind his eyes, like a string pulled taut for life between two posts that no longer align.

—You have hidden what we were looking for inside your own head —Gaff said, without anger, almost with professional curiosity—. That is not reading. That is burying yourself alive with the dead.

—Someone had to play the tomb —Mateu said—. Better me, who knows how to close circuits, than you, who only knows how to open them.

Gaff left with the delegation when the Council, ashamed, stopped renewing his permit. Mateu lived another thirty-one years, increasingly quiet, with the wise man's phrase beating inside him like an ember that neither fully extinguishes nor fully ignites. He never told it to anyone. He died without distributing it, exactly like the wise man, and in the instant of his own dissolution he did the same as him: he clenched it in a fist and did not let it go.

The Council recorded his death without an exact date. Someone added a phrase in the margin that explained nothing either:

*“What Mateu hid has not mixed. It beats in its corner as if waiting.”*

### II. The echo that does not mix

Four hundred years is a long time for a man and nothing for a reservoir.

Somewhere beneath what would later be a city, a knot of information refused to dissolve completely. Not because it was indestructible—nothing is—but because it was made of two horizons entangled at the exact moment of death, and entanglement, when it is this strong, takes longer to mix than an entire lifetime.

It beat slowly. It beat alone. It beat without anyone, in three hundred and ninety-six years, having the necessary sensitivity to notice it.

Until someone built a machine that did.

### III. The one who sinks into the tank
*Barcelona, 2026*

Marcos had spent six years inside a sensory deprivation tank, listening to the dead scream their last instant into the walls where they had died. He had been out for three, and he still woke up at night with his skin expecting the weightlessness of the liquid.

Post-Crime did not predict murders. It reconstructed them. When someone killed someone and did it well—no witnesses, no cameras—the metropolitan police sent a tuner submerged in a tank, isolated from their own pulse so that no internal noise would drown out the signal, and that tuner read the Hawking temperature that the victim's panic had left imprinted on the cement, on the metal, in the still air of the room. They did not see the future. They read the exact instant when someone stopped being a closed horizon and began to evaporate by force.

Marcos had been the best. And he had ceased to be the day when, inside the tank, he had confused a victim's fear with his own, and had never been able to separate them again. *Wood too thin*, the medical reports said. Now he was chief inspector. He directed the three remaining tuners from outside the liquid, and tried not to look at them too much when they came out of the tank with their eyes still fixed somewhere else.

Elena directed Post-Crime. She had arrived with a doctorate and a conviction: that the past, like any other resource, was being squandered by scruples that were no longer necessary.

—Every tuner is a biological bottleneck —she told Marcos the first week—. An algorithm doesn't burn out. An algorithm doesn't confuse the victim's fear with its own.

The algorithm was called, in internal documents, the *Absolute Tuner*. It promised to read the past at will, without a tank, without wear, without the human limit of having to rest between readings. Elena didn't want to solve homicides with it. She wanted, though it took her a year to say it in those exact words, to apply it retroactively: to disputed inheritances, to reclaimed properties, to the political loyalty of anyone whose past was worth rereading.

—The dead don't have lawyers —she told Marcos, the night she explained it to him completely—. We are the first to arrive in an ownerless territory.

—They have something better than a lawyer —Marcos said—. They have time. They are mixing. The more we force the reading, the longer it takes to dissolve, and something that doesn't dissolve...

—What?

Marcos didn't know how to finish the sentence. For weeks he had been hearing, in the margins of his own silence, a melody he didn't remember ever learning, hummed by a voice that wasn't his own, in a language that resembled the old Catalan of the archives he sometimes read out of professional curiosity.

The Post-Crime system found the anomaly on a Tuesday, during a routine sweep of the subsoil before a subway construction project. A hot spot of information that hadn't mixed in four hundred years: a frozen singularity, beating at a frequency that no living tuner should recognize.

Marcos recognized it. The melody he had been unintentionally humming for weeks was, note for note, the same frequency.

Elena, when she saw the data, didn't say *how strange*. She said: *there it is*.

### IV. The fog that is not fog
*La Mussara*

She chose La Mussara because there, according to the ancient reports her team had unearthed alongside the anomaly, space was already warped by something similar, an old pocket of poorly dissolved horizon that the local fog had spent centuries failing to digest. A place where extracting would be easier, Elena said. A place where the ground already knew how to yield.

Marcos arrived two hours early, alone, and got into the field tank that Post-Crime used for emergency operations, without authorization, his skin already remembering the weightlessness of the liquid before the liquid even touched him. He didn't go to stop Elena with his hands. He went to read what she was about to tear out before she tore it out.

What he felt, upon submerging, was not the future. It was distance collapsing: a bridge that crossed not space but time, entanglement pulling on entanglement, the old intuition that two horizons correlated at the exact moment of death remain united even if four centuries separate them, like two ends of the same bridge that no one has finished building because it doesn't need to be finished: it is already there, waiting for someone with just the right sensitivity to lean over and look.

He found himself face to face with Mateu at the exact instant of his death.

It wasn't an apparition. It was a correlation so ancient that, read from the inside, it felt like being in the same room. Mateu—old now, with the taut string behind his eyes that Marcos recognized because it was, spanning four centuries, the same tension he had carried since the tank—did not speak to him with words. He showed him, in the languageless language of two horizons touching, what he had carried inside since he pulled his arm out of the water on the northern shore.

It wasn't a secret to sustain the water. Marcos understood it with an aching clarity: the wise man of Tarel had never known how to halt anything. The only thing he knew—the only thing worth hiding so deeply—was this: that what leaves is not lost, it mixes; and that the mixture, to complete itself, needs to be left in peace. The treasure was not inside Mateu's clenched fist. The treasure was the fist itself: the decision not to distribute what was not his to distribute.

On the surface, Elena was forcing the extraction. The prototype, fed with too much information resisting the mixture, began to generate a feedback loop its engineers hadn't foreseen: the more it pulled on the singularity, the more entropy tried to enter through the same channel, like water rising up a drain that has been turned inside out.

Marcos had a fraction of a second to choose. He did what Mateu had done four centuries earlier, on the same different shore: instead of pulling, he pushed inward. He voluntarily mixed his own horizon—his, Mateu's, the distant echo of the wise man that he already carried inside them both—in a massive and deliberate *scrambling*, untying the knot that had gone four hundred years without untying, not to guard it better but to let it, finally, mix completely.

The channel Elena had opened to extract a concentrated singularity found, on the other side, nothing to extract: only reservoir, only mixture, information returned to its most democratic and truest state, the one that belongs to no one because it no longer has the shape of a fist. The Absolute Tuner, designed to pull on something dense, burned out pulling on the void.

Elena said nothing as the prototype shut itself down, smoking in the fog of La Mussara. There was no confession, no arrest, no courtroom scene: only an internal report describing the events as a technical failure, and a director of Post-Crime who, months later, left her post without giving explanations that completely convinced anyone.

---

Marcos returned to Barcelona and never entered a tank again. He walks, say those who know him, with his eyes fixed on a point that is not on any specific street, and sometimes he hums, without realizing it, a melody in a language no one fully recognizes, though to some it sounds, vaguely, like a very old Catalan.

He doesn't know if what Mateu hummed now lives a little in him, or if he simply learned, in the instant the two horizons touched, a song that no longer has an owner because it finally mixed as it should. He doesn't look for it either. He has learned, in the most expensive way possible, that not everything that dissolves needs to be reread.

---

> **If you only take away one idea:** Mateu, Gaff, Marcos, and Elena are not four people in two different centuries. They are the same experiment told from three shores: that of the one who guards what dissolves, that of the one who extracts it as if it had an owner, and that of the one who, in the end, must choose between the two ways of touching what can no longer defend itself. The past, just like the future, is not a free territory just because no one lives in it anymore.
