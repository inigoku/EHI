---
title: THE FOG OF LA MUSSARA
---

*The archivist did not write this. It is a found text: notes the professor typed for himself and never sent to any journal, recovered from a drawer long after he left the faculty. I leave the vocabulary intact —the entorhinal cortex, the hyperbolic metric, the phenomenological overflow— because that vocabulary was the only thing he had left to hold onto. A man who can name everything and suddenly cannot name what happened to him names the usual things harder than necessary. That, too, is a symptom.*

---

### I. The Marinada

There is, in the moorlands of the Baix Camp, rising above the limestone crests of the Prades mountains, a place where the red sandstone and the sea wind weave a conspiracy against the stability of understanding. Cadastral maps name it La Mussara, a hamlet abandoned in 1959 whose ruined profile—crowned by the remains of the church of San Salvador—stands out against the gorges like a crown of decayed teeth exposed to the elements.

I have dedicated my career at the University of Barcelona to the study of predictive processing disorders and schizophrenia. To me, the mind is not a passive receptacle of light, but an engine of Bayesian inference: a bubble that projects spatial hypotheses to protect itself from the infinite entropy of the universe. When the brain works, it translates sensory data into a stable Euclidean space. When it fails, the hypotheses collapse.

I arrived at La Mussara chasing the diary of Albert Martínez Ortiz, a hiker who in October 1991 vanished on the moor leaving no trace other than his wicker basket. His diary, recovered from his apartment in Reus, did not record the ordinary notes of a nature lover, but a growing mathematical obsession with the Prades fog—the marinada—which ascends from the Tarragona coast and swallows the ruins in a matter of seconds.

In his entry for September 12, Albert had written:

> *"The fog does not hide the landscape. It deactivates the rendering matrix. The trees that emerge from it are not made of ordinary wood: they are low-resolution geometric approximations. If I walk a hundred paces in a straight line through the ruins of San Salvador with the fog upon me, the sensors in my middle ear do not register ninety degrees in the turns. Space is not flat up here. It folds inward like a hyperbolic sphere."*

I decided to ascend to the plateau on a Thursday in October, when the weather reports predicted the arrival of a dense maritime front. At an altitude of a thousand meters, the silence was absolute, broken only by the crunch of sandstone beneath my boots. The ruins of La Mussara stood inert under the leaden gray sky. I found the collapsed hamlet, the blind well, and the famous "vuitena pedra" (the eighth stone), a half-buried limestone block about which local folklore claims that whoever steps on it is projected into another existential plane.

### II. The Bayesian Stack Overflow

From the perspective of systems neurology, myths about dimensional portals are rationalizations of massive failures in the network cells (*grid cells*) of the entorhinal cortex. If the brain loses its orientation coordinates in an environment where the visual horizon has been erased, the spacetime interface begins to oscillate. But what I experienced at five in the afternoon, when the boira marinada ascended like a wall of freezing vapor, exceeded any clinical hypothesis.

The fog was not merely suspended moisture. It possessed a density that absorbed not only light, but time. Looking at my wristwatch, the second hand seemed to stop, oscillating in an infinitesimal interval before ticking backward a full second. Three-dimensional space began to lose coherence. The parallel lines of the ruined church walls seemed to converge in my visual field, not due to an optical effect of perspective, but because the Euclidean metric of the environment was dissolving.

The panic I felt was not the biological fear of physical danger. It was the Lovecraftian dread before the revelation that our mind is a program running on an evolutionarily limited hardware substrate. I was trying to compile a hyperbolic (Anti-de Sitter) geometry for which my neural networks had no available API.

I walked toward the church of San Salvador. With every step, the physical distance seemed to dilate exponentially. The light poles appeared to duplicate at impossible, non-Euclidean angles, curving into a hyperbolic geometry where the volume of the room expanded to infinity while the surfaces remained finite. My eyes sent information that my brain tried to fit into its three-dimensional Euclidean model, provoking an unbearable memory overflow. It was a phenomenological overflow, a collapse in the resolution of the horizon.

### III. The Eighth Stone

I arrived before the eighth stone. The stone was not a physical object. It was a metric singularity: a point where space suffered a topological torsion. Brushing it with the toe of my boot, I felt the boundary of my own consciousness—the horizon of the self that isolates my private state from the universe—collapse.

There was no physical transport. I did not appear in another dimension populated by anatomical nightmares. What occurred was the absolute silence of a yielding boundary. The "inside" and the "outside" ceased to be incompatible directions. I felt my own information processing—the fragile bubble of my mind that sustains the simulacrum of distance and identity—overflow before a presence that did not occupy space, but constituted it. It was as if the walls of the horizon dissolved into an immensity that admitted no exterior, a conformal metric that integrated my consciousness into its nonlocal fabric, stripping me of the privilege of separation.

I woke at seven in the morning the next day in the mud, next to the cemetery of La Mussara. The autumn sun warmed the red stones and the fog had retreated, revealing a perfectly flat, Euclidean, and reconciled landscape.

I have returned to Barcelona. I continue with my classes at the faculty, my Wednesday consultations, and the drafting of an article on latency in predictive processing. Ordinary life demands that simulacrum of order, and I lend myself to it with an almost automatic precision, like a software executing its background routine. Yet, there are anomalies. In the clinic, when a patient describes their detachment from reality, I no longer seek the diagnosis in the manuals; I merely listen to the frequency of their fall. And on my office desk, the wristwatch I wore on the moor still marks 17:12 of that afternoon. The hands vibrate faintly, without advancing, as if waiting for time to find its usual metric once again. I have not tried to adjust it again. It is, I suppose, my only clinical note on the abyss.
