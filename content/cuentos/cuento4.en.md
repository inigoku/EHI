---
title: THE WEB OF NAMES
---

There exists in the Library—of which I shall give no further details because its details are, like those of every true library, imprecise—a section that the catalogers call the Knot.

It is not an official name. The official names of the sections are alphanumeric codes that no one uses. The Knot is so named because its catalog is not linear but reticular: the elements are not ordered by author or by subject, but by their connections to one another. In the Knot, one book does not lead to another by its theme, but by the names they contain. And the names—this is what makes the Knot a place that demands caution—are knots.

Every person that someone has named in any book of this Library exists as a knot in the web. The knot remains active as long as someone preserves the memory of the name. When the name disappears from all living memory, the knot goes dark. The connections remain. The absence has a shape.

---

I have spent sixteen years working in the Knot. I am a cataloger of absences: my work consists of tracing the shape of the dark knots through the connections they leave behind, just as one traces the shape of a lost continent by the sediments it left on neighboring continents. The inference is imperfect. But the shape exists.

The case I explain when someone wishes to understand the method is that of Marta Albó.

Marta Albó existed at some point between the 16th and 17th centuries. There is no greater certainty: the exact dates belong to the dark knot. What I can trace is this:

There is in the Library an account book from a cobbler's shop in Barcelona, year 1603, which mentions a *husband of the Albó woman* as a regular customer. The husband has a name, three further mentions in other documents, and a will dated 1619. The Albó woman has none. But the husband of the Albó woman appears in a parish book connected to a neighborhood that includes, in a tithe register, *the baker woman from the alley*, unnamed. A letter from 1621 mentions *the cobbler's wife who sent the bread with her boy on Fridays*. The will of a neighbor, year 1638, leaves a minor bequest to *the daughter of Marta, may God rest her soul*.

Marta. No surname: I added that as a working convention.

From this trail—the husband's shoe, the Friday bread, the daughter in the neighbor's will—I can trace a presence that was never directly named in any document in the Library. The shape of Marta Albó is not in any archive. It is between the archives, in the pattern of what they set in motion without knowing they were doing so.

That is the principle of the method: the shape of the one who is not there, made of what remains when they are gone.

---

What I did not foresee, when I began, was that the Knot has a structural limit that cannot be resolved from within.

The catalogers are also knots.

I am an active knot in the web. There are documents that mention me, there are people who preserve the memory of my name, there are connections between my knot and those of others. From the Knot—from outside of me—there exists a shape that is my shape, a pattern of connections that defines me with more precision than any description I could make of myself.

I cannot see it.

This is the irreducible limitation of the method: one cannot trace the shape of a knot from within the knot. I can read the shape of Marta Albó because I am outside of it. I cannot read mine because I am inside. The cataloger and the catalog cannot occupy the same place.

What I can do is infer. From the books I have written, from the names that appear near mine in the documents, from the connections I notice when others mention things that bear some relation to me without knowing it. I obtain a blurred shape. Never the exact shape.

I have come to think that this is the condition of every active knot, not only of the catalogers: that the exact shape of oneself is precisely what oneself cannot see, because seeing it would require being outside, and being outside would require having ceased to be.




I have been in the Knot for sixteen years and I have not yet finished cataloging Marta Albó.

The daughter, whose name I found five years ago in a parish book: Joana, mentioned in 1641. Joana had four children, two of whom have documented descendants. One of those descendants—in the seventh generation, at the end of the 19th century—appears in a personal diary that forms part of the Library's collection. The diary does not mention Marta Albó. It could not: for the author of the diary, the name of Marta Albó was as dark as it was for anyone else.

But I can trace it. The line of connections that goes from the cobbler of 1603 to the diary of 1887 is long and has gaps, but it exists. The shape of Marta Albó includes, forward in time, the shape of people who lived two hundred years after her and who did not know that the shape of their lives had that knot at its center.

The web does not end. The dark knots remain knots.

Every morning I open the catalog and continue. The shape of the one who is not there, built from what remains. That is what the Knot does: it gives an exact shape to absence, which is the only shape that cannot contradict you, because it is no longer here to do so.
