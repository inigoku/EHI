---
title: THE MUSIC LEFT IN THE ROOM
chapterNumber: 13
---

---

You don't know when you started humming it. One December Thursday it catches you with the coffee pot in your hand: four notes, the one that rises, the one that falls, the silence, again. You turn off the tap and stand still, listening to yourself, trying to work out where this thing came from that comes out of you without your having called it.

It comes from Clara. That much you know, though you never knew the name of what she hummed while she cooked. Twenty-two years of marriage and you never asked her—not because you didn't care, but for the same reason you don't ask the name of a bird that's been singing in your garden for a long time: because it's already yours in another way.

It was a short melody. Four notes, maybe five, something you'd have placed in F minor if you knew how to read music, which you don't. More a color than a scale.

---

Now that she's gone, you hear it. Not always. Only when the afternoon falls a certain way, or when the water on the stove sounds the way it's supposed to sound. The first few months you'd turn off the extractor fan and listen, sure it was the neighbor or a radio. Nothing. Just the traffic below.

You left Clara's things where they were: the coat by the door, the book with the bookmark on the page she never finished, the hand cream, nearly empty, that you haven't touched again. Not because you didn't know what to do with them. Because it still wasn't the time.

The kitchen drawer only she could open, because she knew the exact force it took. The bathroom window you leave just as ajar as she used to, without meaning to. The mark on the sofa where she sat, which you avoid—not out of superstition, but because the dip doesn't match your weight.

---

You searched for the melody the way you search for problems actually worth solving: hummed it into music-recognition apps, described it on a forum—something from the forties, four notes rising and one falling, sad but not dramatic. You were offered twenty-three songs. You listened to them one by one. None of them was it.

And then, without deciding to, you stopped searching. One morning the search was no longer among the things you had to do that day, and that's where it stayed.

---

One Thursday in November, going down the stairs, you heard it again: coming from 2B, among voices and the smell of food, those same four notes. You stopped on the landing, hand on the railing. You didn't ring the bell. You didn't know what you'd have said if they'd answered. The notes repeated once more and then cut off, called away by a voice wanting something for dinner. You kept going down.

---

In January, having lunch with your brother-in-law, he asked how you really were, and for the first time you chose the honest answer instead of the manageable one.

—There's a melody Clara used to hum when she cooked. I don't know what it's called. Now I hum it.

—And? —he said.

—I don't know. Just that.

---

It's still there, with you, when you wash the dishes or wait for the bus or shave. It comes out of you with the same ease as things the body keeps without asking permission. You don't look for it anymore. You know Clara hummed it for years and that now it's yours in the same diffuse way the apartment is yours, or the drawer only she knew how to open: not because you chose it, but because it stayed.

It was enough. Or it wasn't enough, but it was what there was, which sometimes comes to the same thing.
