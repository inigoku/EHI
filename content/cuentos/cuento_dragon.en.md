---
title: THE DRAGON
---

He imagined it at eight, one night when the new house still smelled of paint and he knew no one in the city. It was not a decision. He was looking at the bedroom wall, where the streetlamp drew an elongated stain, and the stain suddenly had a back, and the back had scales, and the scales moved when he breathed. That easily. That little.

He is sixty-one. The dragon is still there.

He has told no one. Not his wife, in thirty years; not his children, who no longer live at home; not the doctor who once asked him, routinely, whether he slept well. He sleeps well. When he does not sleep, he goes to see the dragon.

It has changed with him. At first it was green, like the ones in books, and it lived under the bed, which was the only place in the world he could keep watch over. Later it moved to the hills that can be seen from the kitchen window, and it turned the color of stones when it rains. Now it is old. It has a broken scale on its left side that he does not remember giving it and has not been able to take away. It flies less. Some nights it does not fly.

That is what he could not explain, if he had to explain it to someone: that the dragon does not always do what he wants. That there are nights when he goes down to the cave with an idea—tonight we fly to the sea—and the dragon stays lying there, looking at him with those eyes that do not blink, and there is no way. He knows the dragon is him. He knows it the way he knows his name. And yet there is something there that resists him, that has its own weight, that does not let itself be moved by merely wishing.

He has sometimes thought that this is what distinguishes an imagined dragon from a mere idea: that the idea lets itself be thought and the dragon, not entirely.

There is one thing he has never done, and that is to ask the dragon what it sees when it looks up. From inside the cave, he means. From inside the hills the color of wet stones. Whether it ever notices that there is someone beyond the rock ceiling thinking it, sustaining it, deciding whether tonight it flies or not. Whether the dragon has, in its own way, a practice. A note it holds upward without knowing whether anyone hears it.

He does not ask because he suspects the answer, and the answer gives him vertigo. Not for the dragon. For himself.

Because if the dragon can look up and see nothing—and still be there, with its broken scale, with its own weight, refusing to fly when it does not want to—then he too has no way of knowing what lies behind the ceiling of his own room. Someone might be thinking him with the same constancy with which he, for fifty-three years, has been thinking a dragon. Someone who also does not understand why he sometimes does not do what is expected of him. Someone who does not answer him, not because they are not there, but because answering would break the cave.

He gets up, drinks some water, goes back to bed. Before falling asleep, as every night, he says goodnight to the dragon. The dragon does not answer. It has never answered. But it stays.

He does not know either whether someone, somewhere, is saying goodnight to him. He falls asleep anyway.
