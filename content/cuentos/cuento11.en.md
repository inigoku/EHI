---
title: THE ONE WHO PLAYS THE STRING FROM THE OTHER APARTMENT
---

---

On the fourth floor of the building on Calle del Compte lives someone who plays a string instrument at night.

No one knows exactly who it is. The building has forty-two residents and the fourth floor has six apartments. In one of those six, there is someone with an instrument. It should be easy to determine, and yet it hasn't been, possibly because no one has knocked directly on the doors of the fourth floor to ask, and no one has done so because the music has the kind of presence that makes asking seem impolite.

The music begins between nine and ten at night. It ends when it ends.

What is difficult to explain, and yet all the neighbors who have tried end up mentioning, is that the music seems to know what is happening.

---

Ramona, from 2A, lost her mother in March.

On the nights of those first days, the music from the fourth floor was slow, with long pauses between phrases, like someone thinking aloud about something that has no conclusion. Ramona listened to it from her bed, in the dark, sleepless. She did not cry while she listened. Something in the rhythm was too similar to the rhythm of what she was feeling for crying to be necessary.

In May, when life had begun to return to the ordinary, the music had also changed. More direct. Clearer.

Ramona didn't mention it to anyone for weeks. Then she told her neighbor in 2B, who listened to her with an expression that Ramona recognized as that of someone who already knows.

---

Tomás, from 3C, is of the opinion that it is a coincidence.

He has thought it through and believes the human mind is very good at finding patterns where there are none, especially when under emotional stress. A simple experiment would prove it: keeping a record of what happens in his apartment every night and comparing it with the type of music he hears. With enough data, the pattern would disappear.

He has not kept the record. There would be something strange about documenting one's own pain with such precision, as if turning it into data made it another kind of thing.

What he does know is that the night he argued with his partner until two in the morning, the music from the fourth floor was of a kind that he described, to himself, as uncomfortable. Syncopated. With something inside that would not resolve.

It is a coincidence. He is almost certain.

---

The neighbor in 4D does not hear the music because she has used earplugs to sleep for years.

When asked about the musician on the fourth floor, she says she doesn't know what they are talking about. It seems impossible to her that she wouldn't have heard it. But the earplugs are good, and so are the years.

---

The building's concierge, Bertran, has been in the job for eleven years and keeps a notebook where he records incidents: leaks, uncollected packages, noise complaints.

One night three years ago, he wrote the name of the musician on the fourth floor in the notebook. He had deduced it alone, without asking anyone.

Then he crossed it out.

The notebook still has that page, with the name beneath the crossing out. Bertran consults it sometimes for other things and does not look at it.

---

One October night, Ramona went up to the fourth floor.

She reached the landing. From the corridor she could hear the music, closer than usual, with the different texture sound has before it is muffled by walls. That night the music was of a kind she didn't know how to classify: not sad, not happy, something in between that in Spanish has no precise name and that in other languages perhaps does.

She stood there for a moment, her hand raised toward the door of 4D.

Then she lowered it.

She returned to her apartment down the stairs, slowly. When she arrived, the music was still reaching her, muffled by the floors, with the distance it always had.

That too, she thought, was a way of being close.

---

What no neighbor has done is speak directly with the one who plays.

This is not exactly out of shyness. It is because of something harder to name: the feeling that asking would change something. That if you knew the musician's name, if you knew it was a specific person with a specific history and a more or less good technique, the music would become something else. It would cease to be what it is now: the sound of something arriving through the walls of a building of forty-two residents and which, in some way that no one has been able to fully verify, knows what is happening.

It might know nothing. It probably knows nothing.

But the question of whether it knows something is asked at night, in the bedrooms of the building, and at night questions carry more weight than by day.

So no one has knocked on the doors of the fourth floor.

And the music continues.
