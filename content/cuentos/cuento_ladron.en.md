---
title: THE THIEF OF INTERIORS
---

It was said of him that he could open anything.

It was not idle boasting; it was a simple statement of fact. Doors that had been sealed for generations, chests whose owners had died without opening them, boxes within boxes within boxes: everything yielded sooner or later to his hands, which knew how to listen to what a mechanism wanted before the mechanism itself knew it was saying it. He did not steal out of greed. He stole because he could not bear for something to be closed while he was on the outside.

He was told of a box at the end of a house at the end of a city at the end of a road. It was said that inside lay a treasure no thief had ever reached, not because the box was stronger than the rest, but because it had seven locks, and each was of a different kind, and none opened with what had opened the last.

He went.

### I. The first lock

The first lock was of force.

It was the one everyone expected, the one any thief knows how to force: a mechanism of metal and resistance that yields when subjected to more pressure than it was designed to withstand. The thief studied it for a night, applied his iron at the exact angle, and the lock surrendered with the dry sound of things that only knew how to say no until someone insists enough.

He smiled. If they were all like this, it would be a short night.

They were not all like this.

### II. The second lock

The second lock was of patience.

There was nothing to force: it was a mechanism that only opened if turned slowly, so slowly that any haste jammed it back to the beginning. The thief, accustomed to swift dexterity, had to learn something else. He spent three days turning a wheel one degree per hour, undoing an afternoon's work with a single tremor, starting over again. On the third day, his hands had learned not to want to arrive. The lock opened when he ceased to be in a hurry to open it.

It was not the same as the first. The first demanded what he had in excess. This one demanded something he did not know he lacked.

### III. The third lock

The third lock was of silence.

It jammed with noise. Any sound—the scrape of a tool, his own breath, his heartbeat if heard too loudly—closed it. The thief had to quiet himself in a way he had never needed to before. He learned to breathe without his breathing being heard. He learned to be without making the noise of being. And in that silence, for the first time in years, he heard things his trade had always masked: that his hands trembled even though he did not tremble, that there was an old exhaustion beneath his dexterity. The lock opened when there was no longer anyone around his hands making a noise.

### IV. The fourth lock

The fourth lock was of renunciation.

It had a simple and cruel trap: it held two latches that had to be opened at the same time, and he only had two hands, and to reach the second latch he had to let go of the tool with which he had opened the three previous locks. His father's tool. The one that had made him a thief. He had to let it fall into the well that opened beneath the box, with no guarantee that there would be anything on the other side of the gesture.

He held it for a long time.

He let it go.

He heard it fall, and heard it not hit bottom, and the lock opened with the hand he now had free.

### V. The fifth lock

The fifth lock was of memory.

It asked for a name. Not just any name: the mechanism only yielded to the name of someone the thief had loved and left behind for his trade. There were several. He chose the one that weighed on him the most, said it aloud in that empty house, and hearing himself say it was worse than letting go of the tool, because the tool fell and that was that, but the name, instead, returned.

The lock opened. He stayed for a while with the name in his mouth, which did not close as easily as the latch.

### VI. The sixth lock

The sixth lock had no keyhole.

The thief searched for it everywhere: there was no slot, no wheel, no latch, nothing his hands knew how to interrogate. He spent an entire day running his fingers over a smooth surface that offered no way in. And he understood, slowly, that this lock could not be opened from the outside. That the only way past it was to stop trying to open it: to accept that there was a part of that box that could not be accessed, that had to be crossed without forcing, by trusting. He withdrew his hands. He stood still before the smoothness. And the smoothness, when it ceased to be besieged, opened on its own, the way some things open only when one gives up trying to open them.

One remained.

### VII. The seventh lock

The seventh lock opened with a bare hand.

It asked for no tool, no patience, no silence, no renunciation, no name, no trust. It asked only that he place his open palm upon it—the palm of the thief, the one that had forced so many doors—and wait. The thief placed his hand. He felt, beneath the skin, something beating the way living things beat. And for an instant, before the end, he had the certainty that on the other side of that final sheet of metal there was no object. That whatever the house guarded behind seven locks was not a thing that could be taken out and carried away.

He pressed his palm down.

The lock yielded.

And the box disappeared.

It did not open: it disappeared. In the exact instant the final frontier surrendered, the entire box—the metal, the seven defeated locks, whatever might have been inside—ceased to be, as if it had never been. No hollow remained in the air. No mark remained on the table. There was nothing left to steal, because there was no longer an inside from which to take it.

The thief stood with his palm open over the spot where the box had been.

He did not shout. He did not curse. He stood very still, as he had before the sixth lock, and understood—without words, the way one understands things that cannot be said—why it had disappeared.

The treasure was not inside the box.

The treasure was the inside.

The thief who could open anything had, at last, opened everything.

And by opening it entirely, he had made it disappear.

He lowered his hand.

He left the house at the end of the city at the end of the road with empty hands, which in some cities is the only honest way to return from certain places.

He had stolen nothing.

He had understood something, which is the opposite of stealing: you do not take away a thing that belonged to another, you are left with something you cannot show to anyone.
