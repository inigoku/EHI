---
title: THE HABIT OF WATER
---

The water began to recede without warning.

There was no warning, there was no drought. In Tarel, speaking was always too late: things happened first, then they stayed, and with time they became history.

The city floated on a brackish lagoon so ancient that no one remembered a beginning. Wooden houses, worn ropes, joints made with patience. Children learned the sway before the names of the streets; they knew how to return before knowing how to leave. To come home was a verb: a repeated action, a small habit of the body.

The water was neither a background nor a landscape. It was the habit itself.

I was an archivist. In fact, I slowly watched that which did not yet ask for a name.

### I. The Strangeness

The change did not arrive as absence, but as strangeness. The oar touched bottom where there had been a meter of water. The boats woke up crooked, as if they had spent the night hesitating. The nets came up lighter; the faces, heavier.

I noted the first strip of mud without urgency, the way one corrects an old map without yet knowing which city will remain on the other side of the correction.

### II. What the Water Knows

—It was not there before —said Kema, pointing to the dark edge beneath her house—. The water covered it. The water knows.
—What does it know?
—When to let go... and when to stay.

I wrote it down, though I did not know under which category to file it. It was not the first time someone had given me an exact answer to a question I did not know I had asked badly.

### III. The Guest

The water receded without haste. It did not tear away: it took its leave. Rotted piers. Mooring rings embedded in mud. Marks of past levels that no one wanted to read as prophecy.

It was a guest who gathers their things while you sleep and leaves the room ready for someone who will not arrive.

### IV. The Two Tales

In the archive lived two tales. One said that Tarel had always floated. The other, that we fled toward the water after a war or after a thirst so old it no longer hurt. In both appeared the same word, written with different strokes: **home**.

I did not choose between them. An archivist does not cross out one version to make room for the other: he lets them coexist, even when they contradict each other, because together they explain the same thing from angles that never touch.

### V. The Delegation

Months later the delegation from the Continent arrived. Their boats were metal. Their clothes did not learn to get wet. They walked slowly, as if the ground might break. They asked about everything. They listened less than they spoke. They measured the lagoon in numbers. They unrolled papers the wind tried to read before they did.

They gave figures without looking up. They said the level would drop further. They did not say for how long, nor at what cost.

Someone asked if the water would return. They said they did not know, and said it in the same voice used to say it will not rain.

—You will have to choose: stay, or go.

No one answered right away. I noted the phrase the way one notes the weather: too general to argue with, too precise to ignore.

### VI. The Division

Tarel split without breaking. Some families dismantled their houses with an efficiency that was frightening. They burned dried algae. They promised to return. They promised to write. Not everyone looked back on the way out.

Others stayed and learned the language of the mud: where to step without sinking, when to wait, which sound announces a crack and which only fatigue.

The children walked. Some cried for the lost buoyancy. Others showed off new blisters like medals no one had given them.

The archive changed. It no longer kept only stories of the water, but of what remained when the water left.

### VII. The Farewell

The Council's last message arrived. It recommended evacuation. It used a word that sounded precise and did not fit Tarel's tongue. We decided not to repeat it. Not because it was false, but because it did not know where to live.

There was no rebellion, no ceremony. There were small gestures: new ropes strung between platforms and firm ground, houses that leaned before accepting the weight, old songs sung more slowly to accompany footsteps where there had once been oars.

I left gaps in the archive. Some changes do not admit a complete record.

On the last day I put my hands in what was left of the lagoon. It was warm, murky, salty. I let it run between my fingers. I did not say goodbye.

---

The water of Tarel returned at night. Without announcement. Without signs.

In the morning the inhabitants found the shore where it had always been. The same mud. The same stones. The same line of salt on the low walls.

No one could say if the water had brought anything with it.
No one could say if it had left anything behind.

---

> **If you only take away one idea:** Tarel is not a decorative metaphor. It is the experiment in the form of a story: something emerges from the water, something lives on the water, something returns to the water. And between those three instants, there is a city that loved, remembered, lost, built. The fact that it is no longer locatable in the water does not erase that it was there.
