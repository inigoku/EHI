---
title: THE HABIT OF WATER
---

The water began to recede without warning.

There was no warning, there was no drought. In Tarel, speaking was always too late: things happened first, stayed after, and only with time became history.

The city floated on a brackish lagoon so ancient that no one remembered a beginning. Wooden houses, worn ropes, joints made with patience. Children learned the sway before the names of the streets; they knew how to return before knowing how to leave. To come home was a verb: a repeated action, a small habit of the body. The water was neither a background nor a landscape. It was the habit itself.

I was an archivist: I watched slowly what did not yet ask for a name.

—This wasn't here before —said Kema one morning, pointing to the dark edge left beneath her house—. The water covered it. The water knows.
—What does it know?
—When to let go... and when to stay.

I wrote it down, not knowing under which category of the archive to file it. It was not the last time someone gave me an exact answer to a question I did not know I had asked badly.

The water receded without haste. It did not tear away: it took its leave. Rotted piers. Mooring rings embedded in mud. Marks of past levels that no one wanted to read as prophecy. It was a guest who gathers their things while you sleep and leaves the room ready for someone who will not arrive.

In the archive lived two tales about Tarel, and I never chose between them. One said the city had always floated. The other, that we fled toward the water after a war, or after a thirst so old it no longer hurt. In both appeared the same word, written with different strokes: **home**.

I spent the following days noting what the retreat had uncovered: an anchor with no boat, a wooden bowl swollen with old water, three steps that had never led to any door. The archive grew more in that drought than in any other year of my trade.

Then, one night, without warning, without signs, the water of Tarel returned.

In the morning the inhabitants found the shore where it had always been. The same mud. The same stones. The same line of salt on the low walls.

No one could say if the water had brought anything with it.
No one could say if it had left anything behind.
