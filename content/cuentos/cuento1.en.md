title: THE HABIT OF WATER
---

The water began to recede without warning.

There was no warning, there was no drought. In Tarel, speaking was always too late: things happened first, then they stayed, and with time they became history.

The city floated on a brackish lagoon so ancient that no one remembered a beginning. Wooden houses, worn ropes, joints made with patience. Children learned the sway before the names of the streets; they knew how to return before knowing how to leave. To come home was a verb: a repeated action, a small habit of the body.

The water was neither a background nor a landscape. It was the habit itself.

That is why the change did not arrive as a lack, but as a strangeness. The oar touched bottom where before there had been a meter. The boats woke up tilted, as if they had doubted all night. The nets returned light; the gazes, heavy.

I was an archivist. Or so my appointment stated. In fact, I slowly watched that which did not yet ask for a name: minimal tides, births without ritual, deaths without announcement, stories the elders told the wall when the world pretended not to listen.

I noted the first strip of mud without urgency, like one who corrects an ancient map without yet knowing what city will remain on the other side of the correction.

—It was not there before —said Kema, pointing to the dark edge beneath her house—. The water covered it. The water knows.
—What does it know?
—When to let go... and when to stay.

I wrote it down, though I did not know in what category to archive it.

The water receded without haste. It did not tear away: it took its leave. Rotted piers. Mooring rings embedded in mud. Marks of past levels that no one wanted to read as prophecy.

It was a guest who gathers their things while you sleep and leaves the room ready for someone who will not arrive.

In the archive lived two tales. One said that Tarel had always floated. The other, that we fled toward the water after a war or after a thirst so old it no longer hurt. In both appeared the same word, written with different strokes: **home**.

Months later the delegation from the Continent arrived. Their ships were metallic. Their clothes did not learn to get wet. They walked slowly, as if the ground might break. They asked everything. They listened less than they spoke. They measured the lagoon in numbers. They unrolled papers that the wind tried to read before they did.

They spoke figures without looking up. They said the level would drop further. They did not say for how long nor at what cost.

Someone asked if the water would return. They said they did not know, and they said it with the same voice one uses to say it will not rain.

—We will have to choose to stay or to leave.

No one answered immediately. I took note of the phrase as one takes note of the weather: too general to debate and too precise to ignore.

Tarel divided without breaking. Some families dismantled their houses with a frightening efficiency. They burned dried algae. They promised to return. They promised to write. Not everyone looked back as they left.

Others stayed and learned the speech of the mud: where to step without sinking, when to wait, what sound announces a break and what sound only exhaustion. Some stayed adjusting their stride, others kept retracing the same footprint.

The children walked. Some mourned the lost flotation. Others showed off new blisters like medals no one had given them.

The archive changed. It no longer kept only stories of the water, but of what remained when the water left.

Seeds appeared beneath the silt, rusted tools, marks we did not know whether to interpret as past or as future.

I found an unsigned text, almost erased by the salt:

> *"The water sustains us as long as we remember that we also sustain it."*

I dreamed of the emptied lagoon only once. It was not dead. It slept beneath layers of earth. It breathed slowly. Upon waking, my hands were closed as if they still held something.

The final message from the Council arrived. They recommended evacuation. They used a word that sounded exact and did not fit the tongue of Tarel. We decided not to repeat it. Not because it was false, but because it did not know where to dwell.

There was neither rebellion nor ceremony. There were small gestures: new ropes between platforms and solid ground, houses that tilted before accepting the weight, old songs sung more slowly to accompany footsteps where before there had been an oar.

I left gaps in the archive. There are changes that do not admit a complete record.

On the last day I put my hands into the lagoon that remained. It was warm, murky, salty. I let it run through my fingers. I did not say goodbye.

The water of Tarel returned at night. Without announcement. Without signs.

In the morning the inhabitants found the shore where it had always been. The same mud. The same stones. The same line of salt on the low walls.

No one could say if the water had brought anything with it.
No one could say if it had left anything behind.

---

> **If you only take away one idea:** Tarel is not a decorative metaphor. It is the experiment in the form of a story: something emerges from the water, something lives on the water, something returns to the water. And between those three instants, there is a city that loved, remembered, lost, built. The fact that it is no longer locatable in the water does not erase that it was there.
