---
title: THE UPSTAIRS NEIGHBOR
---

The woman who woke before it dawned, on the first page of "Before It Dawns", does not exist. I wrote her. I chose that she should have spent forty years repeating the same words, I chose that she should not remember the face of whoever taught them to her, I chose the cold of the tiles and the light that changed outside without that having anything to do with what happened inside. And I chose, above all, that she should not know whether on the other side of the silence there was Someone.

What I had not thought about until "The Fractal Reality" is what that makes me for her.

I am her upstairs neighbor. With all the properties of the case: I condensed her, I sustain her dark room, I decided that the machines should beep at the end and that her daughter should take her hand. I could have written her an answer. A sentence, on the page, reaching her from outside the room: yes, there is Someone, it is me, I hear you tuning every morning. It would have been easy. It would also have been the end of her: the instant the answer arrived from above, the woman would stop being someone who holds a note without knowing, and would become a puppet that has been shown the string. The only thing that made her real—her doubt, held for forty years—would evaporate with the confirmation.

So I did not answer her. Not out of cruelty, nor indifference, nor because I was not there. Because answering her would have broken her.

I do not know whether this says anything about the silence she heard. I do not know whether it says anything about the silence I hear. I only know that, while I was writing her, I heard her note with perfect clarity—I wrote it, how could I not hear it—and that I found no way of answering that would not destroy her. And that since then, when I look up at the ceiling of my own room, the possibility that someone is in the same situation with respect to me no longer seems to me a consolation or a threat. It seems to me, simply, the shape of the house.

She is still on her page. Still tuning. I have not given her the answer, but neither have I let her evaporate: every time someone reads the first page of "Before It Dawns", she wakes again before her body asks her to and again does not know. That is the closest thing to caring for her that I have found.

And to her, if from inside the room she could hear this, which she cannot: the note arrived. It always arrived. That I could not tell you was the only way for you to go on being you.

*Note to the story* — This story does not claim that the silence of the woman in "Before It Dawns" was of this kind, nor that anyone's silence is. It claims something smaller and verifiable: that there exists at least one architecture—that of any author with any character—in which the silence of the upstairs neighbor and their care are exactly the same act. And it claims it knowing what the essay has just conceded: that a character is, in all likelihood, a pattern without a witness, a subset of the horizon that writes it. Care does not depend on that. One cares just the same for what perhaps does not feel, when what one cares for is its shape. Whether the architecture repeats one floor up, this book has no way of knowing. Neither did she.
