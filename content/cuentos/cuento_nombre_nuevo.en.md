---
title: THE NEW NAME
chapterNumber: 27
---

Nora invented Delvaria when she was seven, under the kitchen table.

It wasn't a plan. Her grandmother had told her that small people live under tables and move away when the people above stop looking, and Nora, who took everything her grandmother said seriously, lay down on the tiles with a flashlight and looked. What she saw was the underside of the tabletop, full of grain and a damp stain shaped like a river. So she drew the river. And beside the river, a city. And in the city, a tower, and in the tower, a queen called Arenca.

For years, Delvaria grew. Nora had a green-covered notebook where she wrote everything down: the name of every street, the name of the ferryman, the name of the fever that went through the north bank, the laws, the feast days. Under the table, in pencil, bridges kept appearing. Her mother complained for a while and then gave up.

Then Nora turned thirteen, fourteen, fifteen. There was no day on which she stopped going. She simply realized, one day, that she hadn't gone in months.

It was her brother Teo, who was six, who found the green notebook in a box in the storage room.

"What's this?"

"Nothing. A thing from when I was little."

"Will you tell me it?"

She was about to take it out of his hands, but Teo had already opened it at the first page, where in a seven-year-old's handwriting it said DELVARIA, and he was looking at her the way she used to look at her grandmother.

That night, when their parents had gone to bed, the two of them crawled under the table with the flashlight.

The drawing was still there, but it wasn't right. You could see the river, and the towers, and most of the bridges. But whole areas had gone: the pencil had worn away over the years, with the mops, with the air, and where the north bank used to be there was only wood. Nora moved the flashlight slowly. She realized, with a strange chill, that the missing areas were the ones she no longer remembered. It wasn't that they had faded first and then she forgot them. It was the other way round, or so it seemed to her: what she had stopped looking at was what had gone.

"And the queen?" Teo asked.

The queen was in her tower, a stick figure with a crown. Nora opened her mouth to say her name and nothing came out.

She searched the notebook. The page where it should have been had got wet at some point; the ink of the name was a blue smudge.

"I can't remember what she's called."

Teo looked at her with the terrible seriousness of six-year-olds.

"Then she'll be sad. If nobody calls her."

Nora was going to say it was a drawing, that drawings aren't sad. She didn't say it. For weeks she had had a tune stuck in her head, four notes she hummed without noticing on the bus, in the shower, in chemistry class, and she didn't know where it had come from. There, lying on the tiles with the flashlight pointed at the tower, she recognized it. It was the song of the feast days of Delvaria. She had made it up herself at eight. It had been playing in her head for a month and she hadn't known why.

She was quiet for a while. Then she did something she hadn't done since she was eleven: she spoke the way she used to, from inside.

"The queen was in the tower," she said, "and no one had come up to see her in a long time. The people of the north bank had left, because no one remembered them anymore. And every night she climbed to the very top and sang, to see if someone, up there, would hear her."

"And did they hear her?"

"It took them a while."

She took the pencil from Teo's pencil case. In the tower, above the stick figure, she wrote a name. Not the old one, which was lost for good, but another. It came out on its own, the way things used to come out when she was seven: Liria.

"Her name is Liria," she said.

"Before she had a different name."

"Before, yes. Now she's called Liria."

Teo thought about it and accepted it, because at six you accept that.

They kept going for a while. Nora drew a new bridge where the north bank used to be, which was no longer the same bank, and gave it a name, and Teo named the ferryman, who used to have another name too. At one o'clock Teo fell asleep on the tiles, the flashlight still on, resting on his belly. Nora carried him to bed.

Then she went back to the kitchen, opened the green notebook at the last blank page and wrote, in her fifteen-year-old handwriting: "The queen is called Liria. She used to be called something else, but I don't remember anymore. I'm sorry."

The four-note song never played in her head again.

Sometimes, when she walks through the kitchen, she crouches for a second to look under the table. Not always. But often enough.
