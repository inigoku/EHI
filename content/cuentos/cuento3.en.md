---
title: THE BOY WHO LEARNED TO MEASURE TIME
---

---

The summer I was eleven lasted longer than the others.

Not that the calendars lied. June, July, August: the same ninety days as always. But inside those ninety days there was something adults did not have, a density, a way of not ending, which I didn't know how to name back then and which now, looking back from my late forties, I recognize for what it was: time that took a long time to be spent.

We lived on a street of low houses with gardens that bordered one another without fences between them. Mr. Montserrat was eighty-four years old and had an olive tree's shadow that took up his entire backyard. He spent his afternoons in a wooden chair beneath the olive tree, reading or not reading, with the book open on his lap anyhow. He was the kind of person who learns to distinguish between sitting doing nothing and just sitting. That summer, for reasons I no longer quite remember, I decided he was the right person to ask certain things.

—How long does summer last? —I asked him one Tuesday.

He looked up from his book.

—Ninety days —he said.

—No. How long it lasts.

He looked at me for a while. Then he said:

—For me, less than before.

That was not what I wanted to know, but it was the right answer. I understood that later.

---

That summer I had invented a system of measurement.

Not with a watch. The watch measured hours, and the hours of summer were elastic in a way the watch did not recognize. My system used other units: the time from when you went out to the street until your mother called you for lunch. The time of a shadow crossing the patio. The time of an ant going from the flowerpot to the curb, which depended on whether the ant was carrying a load or not.

I filled a notebook with these measurements. I gave them names: shadow-time, ant-time, time-waiting-for-something-to-arrive. I had no conversion system between them. There was no need: each one measured something different, just as meters and kilos measure different things even though both are things that fit into a number.

What I discovered, without fully understanding it, was that August afternoons held many shadow-times inside them. More than June afternoons. Not because the shadows were longer —June has the shortest shadows of the year— but because in August the shadows moved more slowly. Or so it seemed to me. Or the time in which I watched them was thicker.

I didn't write it down in the notebook because I didn't know how. But I remembered it.

---

Mr. Montserrat taught me a game that had no name.

It consisted of sitting beneath the olive tree with closed eyes and waiting for something to happen. Nothing in particular. Just waiting.

—How long do we wait? —I asked the first time.

—As long as it takes —he said.

That irritated me a little because I wanted a measurable answer. But I sat down anyway on the ground, which in August smelled of dry earth and something I wouldn't know how to identify now, but which back then was the most normal smell in the world.

What happened the first time was that a bird landed on the lowest branch of the olive tree. I could hear it moving, its small weight making the wood creak. I didn't see it: my eyes were closed. But I listened to it move for what seemed like a long time, and then I heard it leave, and then there was silence, and the silence also lasted.

—How long did it last? —I asked Mr. Montserrat.

—How long did it seem to you?

—A long time.

—Then it lasted a long time.

He didn't say anything else. We both just sat there quietly for a while, me trying to make the bird's silence —the one that was no longer there— last a little longer.

---

My mother used to say that time flies when you are older.

I would ask her sometimes, during those long summer after-dinner hours that the adults stretched out with coffee and conversation while I tried to slip away: how long did summer last when you were eleven? She would say she didn't remember well, but that it seemed to her it lasted longer. That summers now went by fast, and the ones back then did not.

—Why?

—Because when you're older there are too many things to do.

I was left with the question more than the answer, which is what usually happens when you ask a grown-up something like that. Besides, I had a problem with that reason: Mr. Montserrat didn't have many things to do —he spent his afternoons in the wooden chair, he had reduced everything to that— and it didn't last for him the way it did for me either.

I didn't find the reason. Or I found it once and then couldn't get it back. That also happens with certain things.

---

That summer there was an afternoon storm that wasn't supposed to happen.

The clouds arrived suddenly at five o'clock, when there were still three hours of daylight ahead, and the sky turned the color it turns when summer pretends it knows how to do autumn. I was in Mr. Montserrat's garden. The wind smelled of wet earth before a single drop fell.

—It's going to rain —I said.

—I know —he said.

—Should we go inside?

He didn't move. He stayed in the wooden chair looking at the olive tree, which was moving its branches in a specific way I had never seen. As if the tree also knew what was coming and was waiting for it with a posture that wasn't exactly fear, but wasn't calm either.

The rain arrived hard, all at once, the way it liked to arrive in August. We both got wet. Mr. Montserrat didn't seem to mind particularly. Neither did I, since I was already there.

It lasted ten minutes. Then the sun returned as if nothing had happened.

I didn't write that afternoon down in the notebook. There was no need: some things are kept somewhere else.

---

Mr. Montserrat died in November of that year.

My mother told me one afternoon, without preamble. He knew it, I suppose. Those who spend their afternoons looking at the sky with that specific calm usually know.

The olive tree remained there. That happens with olive trees.

For his game I have neither a name nor a definition. Only the memory of having my eyes closed beneath a branch, with the August heat clinging to my arms, waiting for something to happen without knowing what. And the sound of the bird moving. And then the silence of the bird that was no longer there.

And that both of them, while it lasted, lasted a long time.

---

Now I am in my late forties and summers last as long as they last.

Sometimes, in August, I look for a shadow and close my eyes for a moment. Something doesn't always happen. When it does, it lasts as long as it seems to me to last, which sometimes is a little and sometimes is more than I expected.

I don't name it, I don't measure it, I don't carry the notebook with me. But somewhere in a drawer in my head that old system is still there, waiting: one more shadow-time, unrecorded.
