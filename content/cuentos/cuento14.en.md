---
title: THE ROOM WHERE NO ONE ASKED
---

---

The room had a wall clock with Roman numerals.

I know this for certain because I looked at it for a long time. Time, in that room, was something you needed to know exactly: how long you had been there. How long until it ended. The clock told the numbers but it did not tell what I needed to know, which was when it would be safe.

There is a specific smell. I am not going to describe it because the description would turn it into a clinical symptom, and I do not want it to be a clinical symptom. It is just a smell. Many smells in the world remind you of things. That one reminds me of that room, which is the reverse of the usual order, but it is the order I have.

---

What I learned much later is called the freeze response.

It is not what people think it is. People think that fear always produces flight or fight, and that freezing is a failure: the body betraying the person. But freezing is an ancient and efficient response: when neither flight nor fight is possible, the nervous system reduces visibility. The body becomes still, small, minimal. It consumes less. It waits.

The problem with waiting is that the body stores what happened while it waited. Not as a story that is told, not as a declarative episode: as a physical recognition, faster than thought. The body that once again encounters the smell, the angle of light, the temperature of that kind of room does what it did then: it becomes still, small, minimal.

Not because there is danger now. Because it learned that there was danger in that kind of place and learning precedes reason. That is not weakness. It is exactly what the system must do: store the threat to survive the next time. The problem is that the next time sometimes never comes, and the system keeps storing it anyway.

---

The room where no one asked was a normal room.

This is what is hardest to explain. It was not a place designed for what happened in it. It was a room that existed for something else and that was something else most of the time. It had chairs. It had a window looking out onto an inner courtyard. It had the clock with the Roman numerals.

No one asked because the situation was one of those that produce that specific kind of silence: the silence of people who know but prefer not to know that they know. It is a silence distinct from ordinary silence. It has texture. If you look for it, you find it in many places, in many normal rooms, in many families and offices and groups where there is something that everyone knows and no one names.

No one asked. That, too, is a fact.

---

What I learned, much later, in a very different room with a person who did ask, was to distinguish between then and now.

Then and now are not always evident. The body does not automatically distinguish between the smell of then and a similar smell of now. It recognizes the pattern and executes the response it learned. Teaching the body the difference between a room with a Roman clock from then and a room with a Roman clock from now takes time. It is not a decision that is made and implemented. It is repeated practice, millimeter by millimeter, until the system learns that there is a difference. That then was then and now is now, and that the body can be in the now even though it sometimes tries to go to then.

You never completely finish learning it. Or you do finish and I have not arrived there yet. One of the two, and I still do not know which.

---

What I do know is this: the precision I developed in that room has been, over the years, useful in other places.

The attention to the angle of light in a room. The registering of sounds behind visible sounds. The rapid knowing of whether a situation is safe or not, prior to conscious thought, which is sometimes an excess and sometimes exact information, and the work is to distinguish when it is which.

It was given to me without my asking. I did not choose it. But it is what I have, so I use it when it serves and I leave it still when it does not.

I learned that too in a room, in a different room, with someone who did ask. Rooms are not all the same. That is something it took me time to understand, but which turns out to be true.

---

The clock with the Roman numerals made the sound that clocks make when the rest of the world is in silence: a regular tick, without drama.

It no longer stops me. Or it stops me less. Or it stops me and I notice it and wait for it to pass and it passes, which is the most important difference between then and now, and which cost what it cost to learn.

What happened in that room, happened. It does not disappear: it is the kind of thing that does not disappear. But it is no longer the only thing I know about rooms, about clocks, about the time that passes in places where something important is happening and no one says it out loud.

I also know other things. That also counts.
