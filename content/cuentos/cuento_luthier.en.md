title: THE LUTHIER'S SHOP
---

### I. The One Who Entered Backwards

The first time Marta entered the shop, she did so backwards. Not in an elegant or calculated way. She simply pushed the door with her shoulder because she was carrying too many things in her arms and didn't want to drop any. She entered like that, facing the street, and then turned around and saw the Luthier sitting behind the counter with a piece of sandpaper in his hand and the face of someone who has spent the whole morning waiting for exactly this.

—Is this the place? —Marta asked.

—Depends —said the Luthier.

—They told me you made special instruments.

The Luthier placed the sandpaper on the counter with care, as if the sandpaper had its own opinions about where to rest.

—I make instruments —he said—. The adjective is supplied by the client.

Marta looked around. The shop smelled of wood and something harder to name, like when it rains on dry earth. There were instruments on the walls, on the shelves, on the tables. None had a price tag.

—Can I ask you something first? —Marta said.

—You have entered my shop backwards —said the Luthier—. You have already asked me everything important.

He stood up, took a measuring tape from the drawer, and came around the counter.

—Who is it for? —he asked.

—For me —Marta said—. And for someone else.

The Luthier nodded as if that were exactly what he expected. He began to measure. Not Marta—that would have been strange. He measured the space between Marta and the door. Then the space between Marta and the nearest shelf. Then he stopped, put away the tape, and looked at her.

—How long have you two been without knowing how to speak? —he asked.

Marta opened her mouth. She closed it. She counted mentally.

—About three years —she said.

—Good —said the Luthier—. That is manageable.

He returned to the workshop without explaining what was manageable or why the opposite would not have been.

Three weeks later, the Luthier called her. Marta entered facing forward this time. The instrument was on the counter. It was small. Unexpectedly small. It did not seem capable of filling three years.

—It doesn't have to fill it —said the Luthier—. It only has to provide the starting frequency.

Marta took it with both hands. Something in the weight—or in the temperature of the wood, or in the exact way it fit between her fingers—produced a sensation she could not name until the next day: it was recognition. Not of the instrument, which she had never seen. Of the rhythm the instrument was going to sustain.

The Luthier watched her leave from the counter. He picked up the sandpaper. He went back to work. On the table he had a notebook with a single line: "What the water leaves behind when it recedes does not always have a name." He had written it years ago. He didn't remember why.

Three years is manageable.

### II. The Notebook of Grievances

The second client arrived with a list. Not a list of instruments. A list of grievances. Twenty years of grievances, handwritten in a spiral notebook that he placed on the counter with the solemnity of someone presenting evidence before a court.

—I want an instrument to forget someone —he said.

The Luthier looked at the notebook without opening it.

—Is he alive? —he asked.

—He died six months ago.

—And yet you bring it in writing?

The client looked at the notebook as if he had just realized what he had done.

—I have been keeping it in writing for twenty years —he said—. Habit.

The Luthier nodded. He stood up, came around the counter, and began to walk slowly around the client. He didn't measure him. He just walked. The client followed his movement with his eyes, somewhat uncomfortable.

—What did he do? —asked the Luthier.

—Excuse me?

—Your neighbor. What exactly did he do that bothered you?

The client opened his mouth. He closed it. He pointed to the notebook.

—It's all in there.

—I prefer you tell me.

There was a pause.

—Music until two in the morning. Four or five times a week during the first years. Then less, but never entirely. The garbage bags on the landing, always the day before collection. The dog. The weekend visitors who parked in my spot. The disputes with the homeowners' association, which he always managed to wriggle out of. The noise of the chair—he had a wooden chair, on parquet, and he dragged it every morning at exactly a quarter past six.

The Luthier listened to all this without interrupting.

—And now? —he asked when the client finished.

—Now... I still hear the chair. At a quarter past six. Even though he is no longer there. Even though the apartment is empty.

—Of course —said the Luthier.

—Of course? —The client frowned—. That is not normal.

—It is completely normal. The problem is not memory. The problem is that you have spent twenty years building a very precise model of that man. You know exactly how he reacted. What made noise. How he parked. How he moved in the morning. That model does not need him to be alive to keep functioning.

The client stared at him.

—And you can turn it off?

The Luthier returned behind the counter.

—I don't have instruments for forgetting —he said—. I never have.

—Then what are yours for?

—To connect. To process what remains of someone in the reservoir. To give the model a place to go instead of continuing to run on empty.

—Is that different from forgetting?

—Completely different —said the Luthier—. To forget is to erase. What I offer you is closer to resolving.

—Do you know the difference between a ghost and a memory? —asked the client.

The Luthier thought.

—Ghosts do not know they are dead —he said.

—Exactly. Mine does. That is the worst part. He is still there, dragging the chair, and we both know he shouldn't be.

The Luthier opened a drawer the client had not seen before. He took out something small, without an immediately recognizable shape. He placed it on the counter next to the notebook of grievances.

—This one does not connect with anyone —he said—. It connects with what comes after someone. It is different.

—Does it work?

—Depends on what you understand by working. It will not make you forget the chair. But you might stop hearing it as a threat.

The client looked at the object for a while. Then he picked it up.

—How much?

The Luthier named a price. The client did not protest, which said something about how long he had been searching. When he left, the Luthier put the spiral notebook away in the drawer. He had not opened it. He never opened the lists. There was no need. Twenty years of precise grievances are exactly the same as twenty years of precise love, seen from the other side of the frequency.

### III. The One Who Didn't Know How to Name It

The third client arrived mid-morning and stood by the door for a time the Luthier found unusually long. He did not look at the instruments. He looked at the floor.

—Can I help you? —said the Luthier from behind the counter, without looking up from what he was doing.

—I don't know —said the client.

The Luthier looked up. It was the first time anyone had answered that question like that.

—Sit down —he said.

There were no chairs in sight. The client looked at him.

—It was a figure of speech —said the Luthier—. What brings you here?

The client did not answer immediately. When he did, he chose his words like someone crossing a river stepping on stones.

—There is someone. We have known each other for many years. And in the last year I have noticed that something has changed. I don't know if what I feel has changed or if I simply notice it now. And I don't know what to call it.

—What do you call it now? —asked the Luthier.

—Friendship. Out of habit.

—And what name would you give it if not out of habit?

Pause.

—I don't know.

—Yes, you do —said the Luthier. He did not say it with cruelty. He said it like someone pointing out an obvious fact—. The problem is not that you don't know what it is. The problem is that you already know and you are afraid of what it costs to name it.

The client stared at him.

—What does it cost?

—Naming it changes what you are. If you include that person in what you are, losing them would no longer be losing someone. It would be losing a part of yourself.

—That is too much.

—Yes —said the Luthier—. It generally is.

There was a silence.

—And if I name it and it is not reciprocated? —asked the client.

—Then you will have named something that exists even if it is not reciprocated.

—That is no consolation.

—No. But it is more honest than continuing to call it friendship when it no longer is.

The Luthier stood up. He did not go to the usual shelves. He went to an area of the shop the client had not yet seen, further in the back, where the instruments were less recognizable and spaced further apart. He took something from the highest shelf. He held it for a moment before placing it on the counter.

—Most of the instruments I make are for connecting —he said—. This one does something different. It shows the geometry as it is. Not as you would wish it to be.

—What does that mean?

—It means that if there is something on the other side, it will find it. And if there is nothing, you will also know.

The client looked at the instrument.

—And if I prefer not to know?

—Then don't use it. But it is already here.

The client went a long time without saying anything. Then he picked it up.

—How does it work?

—When you are ready —said the Luthier—, you will know.

He paid. He left. The Luthier looked at the gap on the shelf. It was the last of its kind that he had. They took a long time to make because he never knew for sure when he was going to need them. He would have to make another. He picked up a piece of wood he hadn't touched in weeks, waiting.

### IV. The One Who Was in a Hurry

The fourth client arrived in a hurry and left in a greater one. He entered while the Luthier was attending to someone else. He did not wait. He positioned himself by the counter with the posture of someone who knows exactly what he wants and only needs someone to hand it to him.

The Luthier finished what he was doing without rushing.

—What are you looking for? —he said.

—Something that works fast —said the client.

—For what?

The client hesitated for the first time.

—To feel that I am in the right place. Even if just for a moment.

The Luthier looked at him for a longer time than the client expected.

—How often do you need that moment?

—More and more.

—And each time it lasts less?

The client did not answer. The answer was in the silence.

The Luthier went slowly to the back of the shop. He took his time. When he returned, he brought nothing.

—I don't have what you are looking for —he said.

—Why not?

—Because what you describe is not an instrument. It is what happens when the instrument has ceased to be enough.

The client looked at him.

—Then what do I have?

—A horizon that has recalibrated. The problem is not what you are looking for. It is that the threshold from which you are looking has shifted.

The client stood still for a moment. Then he said:

—Can that be fixed?

—It can be recalibrated. Which is slower and more difficult than fixing.

He left without buying anything. The Luthier watched the door close and then looked at his empty hands. Then he picked up the instrument he had been working on from the bench and continued fixing it. Or recalibrating it.

### V. The Thin Wood

The fifth client entered, closing the door with excessive care, as if he feared the latch might break something. He stood for a moment with his back to the room, one hand still on the doorknob, before turning around.

He was a young man. He held his shoulders slumped inward, as if he were wearing a coat that wasn't his.

He closed his eyes. He breathed in the smell of wood and dry dust.

—There is silence here —he said.

The Luthier observed him from his stool, without letting go of the tool he held in his hands—a fine file, with a dark handle.

—There is the normal noise of a shop.

—No. There is the other kind of silence. The kind that weighs.

The Luthier placed the file on the counter. He positioned it carefully, parallel to the edge, as if it needed to be aligned.

—Come closer —he said.

The client stepped forward. When he was a meter from the counter, the Luthier stood up. He did not pick up the measuring tape he had used with Marta. He did not walk around the client as he had with the one with the notebook of grievances. He just stood there, looking at him, one hand resting on the counter.

—What are you hiding from? —he asked.

—From everyone. I walk into a room and I fill up. If someone is sad, my chest grows heavy. If someone is afraid, my pulse races. It's not that I understand them. It's that I become them. And I no longer have room for myself.

—Since when?

—Since always. But I used to be able to handle it.

—And now?

The client lowered his gaze.

—Now I go home and I don't know which of the voices I hear in my head is mine.

The Luthier nodded. Slowly.

—Your wood is too thin —he said—. Any external vibration makes it sound. That is not a defect. It is a property. But at its current volume, it is going to break you.

—Then give me a wall. A plug. Something to isolate me.

—I don't sell plugs.

—Then a shield.

The Luthier shook his head, just once.

—You cannot thicken the wood of an already finished instrument —he said—. It would ruin it. If I build you a wall, you will stop feeling the weight of others. You will also stop feeling your own. Absolute silence is the death of the instrument.

The client looked at the floor.

—Then I will break.

—Or you can learn to resonate without retaining.

The Luthier opened a lower drawer. He took out a small, heavy object. He set it on the counter with a dry, complete sound, like when a stone finds its place in a dry stone wall.

It was a tuning fork. Not one of the usual ones. The stem was thicker, the tines shorter, and the metal—if it was metal—had a deep color, almost matte black, as if it had been underwater for a long time.

—Take it.

The client took it. It weighed more than it looked. Cold, dense, like a long bone.

The Luthier took it back. He struck it gently against the edge of the counter. Almost nothing was heard—a low, brief tone—. But the client felt the vibration travel up his feet, up his spine, until it stopped in his sternum. He didn't hear it. He inhabited it.

—This is not going to isolate you —said the Luthier—. It is going to remind you what your frequency is when the frequencies of others scream too loudly.

—Is it an anchor?

—Something like that.

—How is it used?

—When you walk into a room and begin to fill up, you touch it. It doesn't need to sound. You only need to feel it in your hand.

The client closed his fingers around the tuning fork.

—And if I lose it?

—Then you will no longer need it. By then you will know how to do it without it.

He paid. He left. The Luthier stood for a moment, looking at the spot on the counter where the tuning fork had been. Then he picked up the file and returned to his stool.

He was working on a piece of dark wood, almost finished. It didn't lack shape. It lacked weight. He was smoothing it to add, inside, a small mass of lead. Without that weight, the instrument he was making would not be able to serve its next owner, who had not yet appeared but he already knew would appear.

He worked slowly. Anchors are made that way.

### VI. The Owner of the Weight

She appeared in spring, as the Luthier knew she would appear, although he did not know it would be like this: with a sleeping baby against her chest, secured with a wrap, and the face of someone who has gone months without sleeping two hours straight.

She didn't say what she was looking for. She stood by the counter, swaying without realizing it, with that rocking motion mothers do even when they aren't carrying the child, because the body has incorporated it into the craft of standing.

—How old is he? —asked the Luthier.

—Four months.

—And you?

The woman looked at him. It was evident that no one had asked her that question in four months.

—I don't know —she said. And then, because the shop had something that made you say things in their true order—: Everyone told me this came naturally. That I would look at him and I would know. I look at him and I care for him and I do everything that needs to be done, day and night, without failing once. But it is like playing behind a glass. I hear him cry and I know what's wrong before anyone else, and even so I can't... —she searched for the word and chose the smallest one—: I can't reach him.

The Luthier did not say that this was normal, although it was, because the word normal consoles no one who is where she was.

—New instruments do not tune themselves —he said instead—. They are tuned against another. Someone has to provide the first note, and sustain it long enough for the other to find it.

—And who provides the note?

—You do.

The woman let out a short laugh, without joy.

—I don't have a note. For four months I haven't even known where I am.

—That is something else —said the Luthier—. Your note is there. What you lack is the weight to sustain it. It sounds and it fades. It sounds and is carried away by any current: exhaustion, fear, the list of what is missing. And a child cannot tune himself against a note that moves.

He went to the workshop. He returned with the dark wood instrument, the one he had finished in winter. It was small, sober, unadorned, and when he placed it on the counter it sounded against the wood with a deeper thud than its size promised.

—It's heavy —the woman said upon picking it up, with the baby still asleep against her chest and the instrument in her free hand.

—It has lead inside. I put it in before I met you.

She held it. The weight traveled down her arm, down her shoulder, down her back, to her feet, and something in her swaying became slower. It didn't stop: it settled, the way a ship settles when it finds its draft.

The baby moved against her chest. He didn't cry. He repositioned himself, searching with his head, and went back to sleep in the new rhythm, which was barely different from the previous one and was completely different.

The woman stood very still. She looked at the Luthier over the child's head.

—What happened?

—He knew where you were —said the Luthier—. Children do not look for a perfect mother. They look for a stable frequency to return to.

—And when I put the instrument down?

—At first you will notice it. Then the weight will remain in your body, just as the swaying remained. Good instruments work so they are no longer needed.

The woman paid with one hand, without undoing the new stillness. At the door she turned around.

—How did you know the weight? Before meeting me.

The Luthier shrugged.

—Weights are all similar —he said—. The difficult part is the wood around it. And that, you provided.

The heaviest instrument in the shop went out into the street weighing, in the woman's arms, almost nothing.

### VII. The Viola of the Hollow

The last client of the year brought a viola in a case that was not its own.

It was obvious: the case was for a violin, and the viola was squeezed inside, wrapped in a wool sweater to compensate for the difference. The woman placed it on the counter and opened it with the care of someone opening something that has not been opened in a long time.

—It was my son's —she said.

The Luthier did not ask. There are past tenses that already say it all.

The viola had a crack in the top plate, along the grain, from the bridge downwards. It was not recent: the edges were darkened, the wood had worked around the wound for years, closing it in its own way, without help.

—How long has it been? —asked the Luthier, and both knew he was not asking about the crack.

—Nine years.

The Luthier held the viola up to the light of the window. He turned it slowly. He tapped the top plate with his knuckle, here and there, listening.

—And why now?

The woman took a moment to answer.

—Because now I can.

The Luthier nodded and placed the viola on the cloth of the counter.

—I can close the crack —he said—. A spruce cleat, hot hide glue, a week in the press. It will be firm. But you need to know one thing before leaving it with me.

—Tell me.

—It will not sound like before.

The woman looked at him.

—Will it sound worse?

—It will sound different. Repaired wood vibrates around the repair. The crack stops being a wound and becomes part of the structure. The instrument you take home in a week will not be the one your son played. It will be one that has gone through what it has gone through.

—And you can't...? —the woman began, and did not finish, because she already knew the answer and it seemed more dignified not to force him to say it.

—No —said the Luthier anyway—. No one can do that.

The woman looked at the viola for a long while. Outside, a bus went by. The shop barely trembled, and the hanging instruments each responded with their note, a minimal and disordered chord, like a conversation in another room.

—Do it —she said.

She returned ten days later. The Luthier brought the viola out of the workshop and handed it to her without ceremony. The crack was still visible: a lighter line on the top plate, honest, without concealment.

—You haven't covered it up?

—I have closed it. Covering it up is another trade, and it is not mine.

The woman ran her finger along the line. Then, without asking permission, she sat on the stool by the window, placed the viola on her shoulder—clumsily, with a memory of gestures that was not hers but learned from watching—and drew the bow across a string.

A single note. Long.

The Luthier listened from the counter. What he had said was true: it did not sound like before. It sounded deeper in the center, with a new edge, something like a voice that has cried and no longer hides it.

The woman lowered the bow.

—It is not him —she said.

—No.

—But it knows him.

The Luthier did not answer. There are diagnoses that only the client can make.

The woman paid, put the viola away in the wrong case, with its wool sweater, and went out into the street. The Luthier stood looking at the stool by the window, where the afternoon sun now arrived with no one there.

Then he took the broom and swept up the day's shavings, which is what a workshop does with what is left over from repairs: it does not keep everything.
