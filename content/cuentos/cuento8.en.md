---
title: THE BRIDGES WITHOUT PASSAGE
---

---

In this city, bridges are crossed with open hands.

It is no metaphor. It is protocol. Palms facing up, fingers extended, the back of the hands visible from both sides. Everything you carry to the other side must fit between your two hands—no bags, no backpacks, no pockets full of everything one normally drags along. If it does not fit in your hands, it does not cross with you.

No one remembers when this began. The city's historians have three distinct theories, and none is entirely convincing. What they do know is that it has been going on long enough for the citizens to have built a language around what is carried.

---

**I. Iron Bridge — Ana**

Ana was taking her granddaughter to the bridge for the first time.

The protocol was not difficult to explain: open hands, palms up, only what fits between the two. What you carry is what you say. If there is no object, you cross with empty hands, which is also a message.

—What would you carry today? —the granddaughter asked.

Ana opened her hands. They were empty. She had not thought of crossing this morning and so she had prepared nothing.

She looked at them for a moment.

—I do not know yet —she said.

That, too, was an answer.

---

**II. Water Bridge — Benedikt**

Benedikt crossed with empty hands the day his mother died.

The people who saw him cross knew what it meant. In this city, empty hands are not an oversight: they are a declaration. You cross with nothing when what you bring has no object form, when the weight of what you carry is of a kind that palms cannot hold, when the only honest thing is to arrive without pretending you can reduce it to a thing.

There are crossings that demand this.

The woman waiting for him on the other side carried nothing either. That is also protocol: when someone crosses empty, the one who waits empties their hands too. So that the encounter is of the same kind on both sides. So that there is no asymmetry in what is offered.

The river passed beneath the bridge, making the sound rivers make when nothing specific is asked of them.

They did not say much. What had to be said did not need words, and the words they used were of the small kind, the ones that serve to let time pass without doing harm.

Benedikt crossed back at dusk, when it was time to return. He carried in his hands what the woman had given him before he left: a small cloth, folded, the color of heavily used things. It was not valuable. It was exactly what it was: a small cloth someone had placed in his hands so he would not cross back empty.

He did not know what to say about that. Sometimes the gesture is enough, and adding words diminishes it.

---

**III. Bridgekeeper — Sala**

Sala had spent eighteen years maintaining the Bridge of Merchants, and he knew how to read the crossings the same way others learn to read the weather: with the eye, with the accumulation of things seen, without the knowledge having to pass through conscious thought anymore.

What people carry, what they do not carry, the speed of the crossing, whether they look to the other side before arriving or wait until they are already in the center: everything says something. Slow crossings are conversations. Fast ones, urgencies. Those who stop in the middle and look at the water are the ones who have not yet decided what they are going to say when they arrive.

The strangest thing he had seen, in eighteen years, was a couple who had been crossing the same bridge in opposite directions for many years. He from east to west. She from west to east. They would meet in the center, exchange what they carried—always the same objects: he carried a stone the size of a fist, she carried a feather—and continue in opposite directions. Each would arrive at the other side with the other's object, and return the next day with their own.

Sala had watched them for so long that he stopped taking note of them. The day they did not appear, he noticed.

He did not know what had happened. Probably one of the things that happen.

What he did know was that somewhere, in two houses on opposite sides of the river, there was a stone and a feather that had crossed too many times to know anymore which bank they were from. That the stone held something of the woman and the feather held something of the man, not as information you can read, but as the kind of thing objects hold when touched by the same hands for years.

That bridges, when you use them for a long time, do that: the two shores hold one another in the objects passed from hand to hand. Without either shore becoming the other. Without the river disappearing.

Only with the shape of the other side, held in things that fit in the hands.
