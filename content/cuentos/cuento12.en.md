---
title: THE CITY OF SMALL LOSSES
---

In Arenilla, the citizens began to forget the name of the market before forgetting the market itself.

The market was still there: the fruit stalls, the hot oil, the smell of cilantro. But when someone tried to explain to a stranger how to get there, they would stop mid-sentence. *The market...* and then a wave of the hand, an approximate direction. The stranger arrived all the same. The city kept functioning.

Aurelio kept a notebook. He was not an archivist by profession —his title said something else, though no one remembered exactly what—, but someone had to take notes. He wrote in small handwriting and left wide margins for notes he never added.

---

The first entries were recent names.

The bar they had opened on the corner of the square four years ago: its name had vanished from the speech of its patrons, even though the sign still hung above the door. People ordered coffee and kept coming back. When someone asked *do you know if the corner bar is open on Sundays?*, the other would say *the corner bar* as if that were the name, and Aurelio would write it down: *proper noun → deictic expression.*

What took him a while to notice was the order. The most recent things disappeared first. Then, slowly, the intermediate layers. The very old things endured: the name of the river, that of the bridge over the river, the patron saint of the main festival, which was still celebrated on the usual date even though no one remembered the year it began.

It was as if the city had a memory that emptied itself from the edges inward.

He spoke with the municipal doctor about this. She listened patiently and at the end asked him if he had ever thought about retiring. Aurelio took note of the conversation and filed it under a category he invented himself: *answers that avoid the question.*

---

There was a woman in the northern neighborhood, Concepción, seventy-eight years old, who no longer knew the name of her street but returned home every day without a problem.

Her feet knew. She turned left at the exact spot, crossed the threshold without searching for the key in the wrong pocket, climbed the four steps of the entryway without counting them.

Aurelio followed her one afternoon, taking notes.

He asked her what the street was called.

Concepción pointed at the ground with her cane. *This one*, she said.

He wrote it down. And he stood looking at the entrance for a while, because in a sense Concepción was right: *this one* was the most exact name a street could have, the only one that could not be lost as long as someone was standing on it.

He did not know in what category to file it.

---

The losses reached the colors that winter.

It was not that people were colorblind. They saw well, they responded to colors as always. But the names became imprecise. The blue of the sky was called *blue* and that was it, without the *clear sky* or the *January morning blue*. The orange of the sunset lost first the *golden*, then the *orangey*, and was left as *that color over there*, pointed out with a finger toward the west.

The painters at the market kept mixing tones with precision. But when a customer asked for the exact color of their kitchen wall, the two would look at each other for a while and eventually find the tone without naming it. Only with the eye.

Aurelio did not know if that was a loss. He was sure that something was missing. He was not sure that what was missing was needed.

---

He went to the municipal library to look for documentation. The librarian took him to the local history section. The books were organized, the spines in their place, the return dates correctly stamped.

But several titles escaped him as he read them. Not illegible —the letters were there— but as if whoever had written them had not been entirely sure of what they wanted to say. *History of...* followed by something the eye slipped over without retaining. *Memoirs of a...* and then a name that Aurelio read, that his pupil touched, but that his mind did not keep.

The librarian saw that he had stopped in front of a shelf.

—Lately there are several like that —she said, as if she had noticed it a long time ago but had not found the moment to mention it.

—The titles?

—The meaning of the titles.

Aurelio picked one at random. The cover said something about the river. The text was coherent, the prose careful. It told the story of a family that lived by the river. He reached the end of the first chapter and would not have been able to say the name of any character, although there were some. Only the image: the river, which did not look like the river of Arenilla but which was unmistakably the river of this book, the only river this book could have.

He returned it to its place.

The information was there. Only it no longer had a way to be retained.

---

There were two old friends, Ernesto and Manel, who met every Thursday on the bench in the square to play cards. They had been doing it for many years, although neither of them remembered how many. They used to know that.

One Thursday in February, Manel arrived late and sat down and took out the cards and said:

—Where was it that we met?

Ernesto thought for a while. He had the feeling of knowing the answer, that specific feeling of something that is about to arrive and does not. Like searching for a word you have on the tip of your tongue.

—It was in... —he began.

He did not finish.

Manel nodded. He didn't either.

They kept playing. The game they remembered well. The movements, the turns, the permitted tricks and the ones that were not. What they could no longer do was place the beginning. But the beginning, deep down, did not change the game they had in front of them.

Aurelio was in the square. He did not approach. He noted from afar: *two men who do not remember the origin of a friendship and continue it all the same.*

---

In spring, Aurelio himself began to forget.

Not the names yet: those endured a little longer, because of his trade. He first forgot the last name of the doctor he had been going to for fourteen years. Then the name the corner bar had had before it disappeared. Then the year he had started the notebook.

He looked for the first entry. It was there: a date, a name, a brief note. The data were legible. But he no longer remembered the context that made that name meaningful. It was like reading a record by another archivist, written in the same language, with the same handwriting, about events that at some point must have mattered.

He wrote it down: *[name without available context].* With brackets, to distinguish it from the other entries.

That night he thought that the notebook was becoming an archive of what the city had lost, and that he was losing the key to read the archive. That at some point the notebook would be only a collection of words without the web that bound them.

That perhaps it already was, in part.

He slept well. In the morning he noted that down too.

---

Concepción died in August. It was at dawn, in her house, in her bed.

At the burial, someone asked what the street where she had lived was called, for the obituary. No one knew for certain. In the end they put the house number and the northern neighborhood, which was enough for those who knew her and too specific for those who did not.

Aurelio looked for Concepción's entry in the notebook.

*This one*, he had written.

He closed the notebook. The August sun fell flat over the headstones without favoring any of them. It was hot. People wore dark clothes and were hot and endured it because it was what had to be done.

There was not much else to say.

---

The city went on.

The river continued to be called the same. The bridge too. The main festival was celebrated on the usual date. The market had a new name that no one remembered having chosen and that everyone used.

The children of Arenilla named things with the specific confidence of those who do not yet know that names can leave. They invented names for cracks in the walls, for the neighborhood dogs, for the puddles that appeared after the rain and that Aurelio knew would not exist long enough to deserve a name, and which they named anyway, with the same seriousness with which they named permanent things.

Aurelio watched them from the bench in the square. He did not write anything down.

There were things that the notebook was not the proper place to keep.

---

In September he wrote the last entry of the year.

*What is lost is not the city. It is the map we convinced ourselves was the city. Beneath the map, the city goes on. It knows its own streets. It recognizes its inhabitants even if—*

He closed the notebook. Outside, the city did what cities do: continue without anyone asking it to.
