---
title: THE FULL SQUARE
---

She arrives alone and walks toward the center, where the noise is densest.

She does not know at what hour she stopped feeling the cold. An hour ago she still had her coat buttoned to the neck and was thinking about the shopping list, about the pending conversation with her sister, about whether she would make the last train. Now all of that is somewhere else, put away in a drawer that does not need opening. All there is is the chant, and her own voice inside it, and the growing difficulty of knowing where one ends and the other begins.

The whole square breathes to the same rhythm. Ten thousand lungs, a single beat. When the chant rises, it rises with her inside it, and when it falls, it falls with her inside it, and at some point—she could not say which—it stops being ten thousand people shouting the same thing and becomes a single thing that shouts, with ten thousand borrowed mouths.

She has never felt anything like this. Not in love, which also asked her to dissolve the boundary a little, but only with one other person, one at a time, slowly. This is different: it is instant fusion, without negotiation, without the clumsiness of getting to know someone. It is enough to be here, to shout what the others shout, and the membrane gives way without anyone needing to ask permission.

Someone beside her—she does not know their name, she never will—looks at her for a second with a joy that seems to recognize her. It does not recognize her. It recognizes that she too is inside. That is enough.

When the square falls silent for an instant, between one slogan and the next, there is a strange, almost religious silence, and in that silence she notices something she did not expect: it is not only her voice she has lent. It is also her judgment. For a while, as long as this lasts, she is not evaluating every phrase she shouts with the same cool head she would use to evaluate anything else. She is shouting it because the others are shouting it, and that, here, is reason enough.

The chant rises again. So does she.

She could not say whether this is the closest she has ever been to something true, or the farthest she has ever been from herself. She suspects, without being able to prove it yet, that the two things might be the same.
