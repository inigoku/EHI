---
title: THE CLAY VESSEL
chapterNumber: 26
---

When the water of the lagoon went away, Tarel was left with the cistern. It lay under the square from before anyone knew how to write, and for years it was enough: you went down a stone stair, filled the bucket, carried it up. That winter the rains got in through a crack and left it cloudy, with a taste of roots that not even the children could stand.

Maro, the potter, decided to empty it. He consulted no one and explained nothing. One morning, before dawn, he went down with a bucket, filled it, brought it up and poured it into the ditch that fed the gardens. Then he went down again.

I noted it on the first day the way one notes an oddity. On the second, as a habit. By the tenth I no longer knew which category to file it under.

People watched him from the rim of the well. Some said the old man had lost his mind; others, more charitable, that he was paying off a debt only he knew about. Maro corrected no one. His hands had been bad for a couple of years (the same tremor that had taken his father, first the cup, then the signature, then the spoon), and he carried the bucket up clutched to his chest so as not to spill it. He spilled it anyway. He went back down.

One afternoon I went down with him. He didn't ask me to, but he didn't send me away either.

Down there it was cold and smelled of wet stone. Maro leaned over the water to fill the bucket and stayed still longer than he needed to.

"Look," he said.

I looked. In the dark water was his face, and behind his face, further in, something that was not the bottom: as if the cistern were a window and on the other side there were a larger room, with someone in it.

"It's the reflection of the rim," I said.

"I know," he said.

He went on filling the bucket.

After thirty days the water had dropped enough for the walls to show, black with moss, and on them the marks of the levels of other centuries. After forty there was no water left. There was mud: a thick layer, almost black, that smelled of everything that had ever fallen in.

That day Maro went down without a bucket. I stayed up top. I saw him sit in the mud, in the exact center of the circle, his hands open on his knees, and close his eyes. He stayed like that until the sun stopped coming in through the rim. He wasn't praying, or not with words. He was still the way nobody is still, not even asleep.

When he came up he was covered in mud to the elbows and had a face I had never seen on him.

"I wasn't alone down there," he said.

"Who was there?"

He shrugged. He looked at his hands, which were trembling as always.

"Someone who had spent forty days watching me take the water out."

I didn't know what to answer, so I didn't. I noted it that night without a category.

The next day Maro brought up mud from the cistern in the same bucket and took it to his workshop. For a week he didn't come out. When he finally opened the door, there was a vessel on the wheel.

It was ugly. There is no other word. Its mouth was crooked, one side thicker than the other, and all over its surface were the prints of fingers that had not been able to keep still: small ripples, like the ones rain leaves in a puddle. Maro, who had made the best jugs on the lagoon when there was a lagoon, looked at it the way he never looked at his jugs.

"It's badly made," I told him, because it seemed to me he wanted to hear it.

"It's made with what there is," he said. "The mud from below and the hands I have."

He ran his thumb over one of the ripples.

"My father, at the end, couldn't hold a cup. He got angry with his hands as if they belonged to someone else. I did the same for two years." He lifted the vessel, which weighed more than it looked. "But they don't belong to someone else. They're how things reach me. If they don't tremble like this, it isn't my vessel."

He fired it in the old kiln, the one no one lit anymore. It didn't crack.

He set it in the square, next to the rim of the empty cistern, without a lid. People laughed a little as they passed. The children put their hands in to see if there was anything inside. There was nothing.

Three weeks later it rained for the first time since autumn. It rained all night, slowly, without wind. In the morning the cistern held a hand's breadth of clean water, and Maro's vessel was full to the brim, not a drop outside, with the sky caught inside it like a small mirror.

Maro looked in and said nothing. I looked in too. I saw my face, and behind my face, further in, something that was not the bottom.

I don't know what it was. I noted it like this: "Maro's vessel is full. No one filled it. Or someone filled it and did not come down to say so."

Maro still makes crooked vessels. He says the straight ones used to come out right because he had nothing to put inside them.
