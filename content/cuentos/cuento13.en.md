---
title: THE CLOCK OF THE BODY
---

---

Félix Cardoner was a watchmaker by trade before he retired. For forty years he repaired wall clocks, pocket watches, chronometers, alarm clocks of a kind no one makes anymore. His hands had learned to move inside mechanisms where a tenth-of-a-millimeter error brings everything to a halt. There was something monastic about that work: silence, concentration, time reduced to the sound of an oscillating balance wheel.

I saw him for the first time in my office when he was seventy-one. His right hand trembled. A resting tremor, pill-rolling, the specific type of Parkinson's: not when he uses the hand but when he leaves it still. The still hand trembles. The hand in motion calms.

*It is as if the hand did not know what to do when it has no work*, he said on that first visit. *When I work, it stays still. When I rest, it moves.*

I observed him for a moment. I asked him to extend his arms. The tremor stopped almost completely upon active extension, just as he had described. It was a perfectly precise description of the pathophysiology: the basal ganglia in Parkinson's lose the fine regulation of movement, so that the system that should be still when not acting activates on its own. The watchmaker had described the mechanism of his disease with the same precision with which he would have described the mechanism of a broken watch.

I explained dopamine to him, the circuit that decides when to initiate a movement and when to stop it. He listened with the attention of someone accustomed to understanding mechanisms.

---

What Parkinson's does to time is harder to explain, and it is what Félix taught me most during the years I saw him in my office.

The subjective time of a Parkinson's patient is not the same as clock time. I know this from what patients say, not from what the books say: the books mention it as a secondary symptom and patients mention it as the thing that disorients them most.

*There are days that go very slowly*, Félix told me on the second visit. *I get up and by the time I reach the kitchen for breakfast it seems a lot of time has passed, but the clock says it's a quarter to nine. And then sometimes it's the other way around: I look and it's two in the afternoon and I don't know where the morning went.*

The system that regulates the sensation of duration—the perception of passing time—shares circuits with the motor system. The same dopamine that moves the body moves internal time. When dopamine is lacking, movements become slow and time becomes inconsistent. The inner clock and the kitchen clock cease to align.

Félix looked at me with an expression that was not exactly ironic but resembled it.

*Forty years fixing clocks*, he said, *and now mine isn't running right.*

---

What Félix taught me most was about the windows.

Parkinson's patients who take levodopa sometimes have episodes of temporary normalization: the two or three hours when the medication is at its peak of action and movement becomes fluid again. We neurologists call them windows. They do not last. But while they last, the patient is himself in the sense that the body had before the illness.

Félix had his windows in the morning, approximately two hours after the first dose. During those two hours he would go to his workshop—which he had not closed, only used less—and work. His hands functioned with the precision they remembered. Time ran at the correct speed.

One day he described to me how he experienced the transition.

*I notice when it sets in*, he said. *It is as if I had been looking at the world through a pane of glass and someone wiped the glass clean. What is outside does not change. What changes is what I can see and how I can move toward it.*

And the reverse transition, when the medication wore off:

*It is the opposite. The glass clouds over again. But I know it before it fully happens. The body gives warning. Time begins to thicken a little before the fingers notice it.*

This description—time thickening as a precursor signal to the motor state—I had not heard before with such precision. Félix noticed it because forty years of working with mechanisms had taught him to heed small signals. Clocks give warning before they stop, if you know how to listen to them.

---

*Those two hours*, he told me once, *are the longest of the day. And the shortest. The longest because I get a lot done in them. The shortest because I know they will end.*

I thought about this for a long time after the appointment. That the same interval can be the longest and the shortest depending on whether you know it is ending. That the density of time does not depend only on how many things happen, but on how well you know that what you have is temporary.

It is not an idea I have found in neurology books, at least not formulated like that. But it is what a watchmaker with Parkinson's taught me during the years he was in my care, and it seems to me more precise than many of the things that actually are in the books.

---

The last time I saw Félix in my office, he brought a watch he had repaired that morning. It was an antique pocket watch, its silver case worn by the years. He showed it to me open: the mechanism clean, the gears in place, the balance wheel oscillating with the regularity of things that work.

He told me he had repaired it during that morning's window. That it had taken him longer than it would have taken before, but that it had turned out well.

I closed it and handed it back to him. The tick of the balance wheel came through muffled by the silver case, like a small heart doing its work.

*Does it work well?* I asked.

*It's running well*, he said. *For now, it's running well.*

I did not write that in his chart. There is no field on the form for "it's running well, for now" — and yet it was the most accurate note I had that day.
