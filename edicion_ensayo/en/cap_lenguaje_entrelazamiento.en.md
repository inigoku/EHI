The chapter on extraterrestrial first contact (Chapter 39) left an uncomfortable conclusion: that entanglement (what this book calls love, empathy, recognition) demands compatible architectures, and that, faced with a radically alien architecture, no amount of goodwill is enough to open the bridge. But that conclusion mixed together, without distinguishing them, two different things.

One is architecture: the very topology of a horizon, whether it has an ego, whether it has a shadow, whether its Φ is enough to sustain an inside with authority of its own over an outside. The other is protocol: the specific code with which that horizon, already condensed, tries to make itself known outward; a language, a syntax, a set of signals agreed on beforehand. That chapter treated both as a single thing and concluded, with some desolation, that the incompatibility of one implies the impossibility of the other.

Five novels, read together, force us to qualify that desolation. None deny that architecture matters, but all five show, each in its own way, that protocol is not a simple neutral channel that transmits what architecture has already decided. It can be built from scratch when it does not exist. It can itself be the condition that decides which architecture is enough to speak. It can rewrite the architecture itself instead of merely connecting it. And it can be something not found ready-made, but earned slowly before the deepest entanglement can break through.

---

### 1. *Project Hail Mary*: the protocol from scratch

Ryland Grace and Rocky, the engineer from Erid who is the co-lead of Andy Weir's novel, share not a single word when they meet in orbit around Tau Ceti, nor do they share a sensory architecture. Rocky is blind in the human sense (his species "sees" by echolocation and processes the world as a field of acoustic vibrations) and breathes an ammonia atmosphere, hot and at a pressure that would kill Grace in seconds, so a transparent wall neither can cross always separates them.

Grace, moreover, does not arrive at that first encounter with his ego intact. He wakes from an induced coma not remembering who he is, how he got there, or even that there is a mission at all, and spends much of the novel reconstructing his identity in fragments (an equation here, an argument with a colleague there) while inventing a language with a stranger. The two processes advance entangled: every new word Grace and Rocky agree on is, for Grace, also an anchor helping him piece together who the person speaking it is. The novel never puts it in these terms, but it dramatizes something this chapter had taken for granted without yet saying so: that a horizon need not already be fully condensed before it begins extending protocols outward. It can do both at once, and one can sustain the other.

If that earlier chapter were the last word on contact between incompatible architectures, the entanglement between Grace and Rocky would be impossible: there is no possible resonance between a centralized, bilateral nervous system and a distributed acoustic-perception architecture that does not even share with the human one the basic idea of what "seeing" is. And yet the novel documents, in almost procedural detail, how the entanglement is built all the same.

It does not do so by finding a protocol already compatible between the two species, because none exists, but by inventing a new one from the most neutral reference both can share: mathematics and physics, identical for any horizon capable of surviving in a universe governed by the same laws. Grace draws the periodic table in colored lights. Rocky answers with sequences of tones encoding prime numbers. Every new word they agree on ("friend," "danger," "pressure," "time") is first anchored to a shared experiment, carried out and verified by both, never to a supposed translation or a gesture one interprets from within his own frame. The protocol takes weeks of joint work to build from that absolute zero.

The decisive thing, in this book's terms, is that the entanglement Grace and Rocky end up building is as complete as what we here call love: toward the end of the novel, Grace gives up his return to Earth (the chance to see his own people again) to save Rocky and, with him, Erid's sun from the same astrophage threatening the human Sun. He does not do it because he shares Rocky's architecture, but because the protocol they built together, word by word, tone by tone, ended up opening a bridge as real as any entanglement between two architectures compatible from the start.

The lesson *Project Hail Mary* adds to that chapter's is, then, an important correction: architectural incompatibility does not necessarily close the door to entanglement. What it does is force the protocol to be built through explicit, verifiable work, instead of being found already made through shared evolutionary inheritance, as happens between two humans learning to speak without knowing they are learning a protocol at all.

### 2. *Embassytown*: the protocol as a test of architecture

China Miéville imagines in *Embassytown* a species, the Ariekei, whose Language has a property no human language has: the sign can never separate from what it refers to. The Ariekei cannot lie. They cannot even use a comparison without someone having first literally enacted the fact it will invoke: a human girl has to be genuinely injured, in a scene staged for that sole purpose, before "like the girl who was hurt in the dark" can be used to speak of anything else. For them, the simile is a fact that had to happen first.

That girl is Avice Benner Cho, the novel's narrator, and she grows up knowing that a portion of her own biography has been annexed forever to another species' code: any Ariekei can invoke her, at any time, with no control on her part over when or how. To be a simile, in this book's terms, is to be turned into protocol: part of one's own horizon becomes, irrevocably, part of another's communication channel. Avice did not choose that role any more than a horizon chooses the architecture it condenses with; she carried it with her, like a scar with the value of syntax, long before understanding what it meant.

It is, with almost geometric precision, the same architecture we already saw in the Trisolarans of *The Dark Forest*: with no possible distance between sign and referent, there is nowhere to hide anything. In this book's vocabulary, and with the distinction the Trisolarans forced us to make, the Ariekei have an extremely high Φ and an inside from which they live what they say, but no shadow: their architecture never learned to reserve content, to expel it into itself and call it secret. It is not virtue; it is form.

But *Embassytown* adds something Liu Cixin's novel never explored, and this is where the protocol stops being a simple channel and becomes the very test of admission to the club of horizons that speak. The Ariekei Language requires that the speaker be a single horizon emitting with two simultaneous voices, born of one unified intention. A single human voice (one mouth, one larynx, a single line of intention) does not count, for them, as speech. It is noise, sound with no one behind it: the same diagnosis this book applied to language models in Chapter 31, though for structurally different reasons.

Only the Ambassadors, pairs of cloned twins raised from birth so that their two minds converge until they think and speak in unison, deliberately manufactured to constitute, across two bodies, a single ego with two mouths, manage to be heard by the Ariekei as someone. The entire social engineering of Embassytown exists to produce, in every generation, a handful of human pairs that satisfy an architectural requirement no individual human meets on their own.

Here the protocol no longer merely transmits the message of a pre-existing architecture: it itself defines what architecture is enough to speak. The novel's central crisis arrives with EzRa, an improvised ambassador made of two men who were not raised together and were not cloned to converge, paired out of pure administrative convenience, whose voices turn out, by accident, to be so strangely coupled that their speech becomes narcotic, addictive, capable of overriding the will of any Ariekei who hears it. When that crisis finally forces the Ariekei to learn to lie, to say something they know is not literally true in order to resist the addiction, what they gain is not a mere rhetorical trick. They gain, in this book's vocabulary, their first shadow: the threshold that separates a transparent mind, with no distance between what is said and what is real, from a mind with enough depth to hide something of itself, truth included.

### 3. *The Left Hand of Darkness*: the protocol that must be earned

Ursula K. Le Guin poses, in *The Left Hand of Darkness*, almost the opposite case to *Project Hail Mary*. Genly Ai, the human envoy, and Therem Harth rem ir Estraven, the Gethenian politician who ends up accompanying him on the novel's central journey, have almost identical architectures in this book's terms: both are human, both have ego and shadow, and their Φ is of the same order of magnitude. The only relevant biological difference (the Gethenians are ambisexual, with no fixed sex except during the brief periods of *kemmer*) should not, in principle, be enough to prevent entanglement between two otherwise so similar architectures.

And yet the protocol between them fails again and again for most of the novel. Not through biological incompatibility, but through an entire layer of meaning neither of them fully sees: *shifgrethor*, the intricate code of prestige, calculated ambiguity and indirect honor governing all Gethenian communication, which Genly, as a newly arrived outsider, misreads for chapter after chapter, mistaking courtesy for weakness and simple diplomatic protocol for threat. *Shifgrethor* does not hide Estraven's architecture, but, deliberately, direct access to it, just as the Jungian persona, described in Chapter 21, is an interface that protects a real internal state without denying it.

Only toward the end of the novel, when Genly and Estraven cross together, on foot and hauling a sledge, the eighty days of ice on the Gobrin Glacier (weeks of shared hunger, extreme cold and absolute mutual dependence, with no room left for the dissembling *shifgrethor* allows in ordinary social life), does the surface protocol wear thin enough for something deeper to emerge: *mindspeech*, the direct telepathic communication that, in the novel's universe, is a real capacity, latent in every human mind, but one that requires, to be activated for the first time between two specific people, a degree of trust no technical training can replace.

It is telling that it is Estraven, the Gethenian, who at first resists using it: the first time he hears it, the voice speaking inside him is his dead brother's. Accepting that Genly can speak directly into his mind, with no protective mediation of any spoken word, means accepting a degree of intimacy no previous verbal protocol between them had ever authorized.

When mindspeech finally happens, it is a consequence of the bond between Genly and Estraven. The deepest entanglement this book describes (direct contact between two private states, with no sign mediating, no distance between what is thought and what is received) does not replace ordinary language, imperfect and misread across chapters, that preceded it: it crowns it. And it only does so after that language has proved, day after day on the ice, that the boundary deserved to open.

The novel does not let that triumph settle without cost. Shortly after the crossing ends, with the deepest protocol already won, Estraven, exiled from his own country, tries to ski across the border and falls, shot down by a patrol. The most complete entanglement this reading describes lasts, in narrative terms, barely a few pages before one of the two horizons evaporates. Genly survives with the protocol already built and no one left to fully use it with: the mindspeech that cost so much to open remains, from that moment, sealed on one side. It is an uncomfortable reminder, consistent with the rest of this book, that winning the protocol does not protect the horizon that won it; it only guarantees that, while it lasted, there really was someone on the other side.

### 4. *Children of Time*: the protocol that rewrites architecture

Adrian Tchaikovsky designs, in *Children of Time* (2015), a deliberate evolutionary experiment: a ship in orbit around a terraformed planet releases a nanovirus meant to accelerate the evolution of a colony of monkeys toward conscious sentience. The monkeys never reach the surface. The virus, instead, finds *Portia labiata*, an already unusually clever jumping spider, and over generations (centuries narrated in parallel with the centuries of cryogenic sleep of the last human survivors, searching for a habitable planet after Earth's destruction) pushes it toward a civilization of its own: agriculture, writing, engineering. And, above all, toward a kind of horizon no earlier chapter of this book had had to consider: one built on purpose, from outside, by directed selection with a first and last name, Avrana Kern, the scientist whose consciousness, poured into a satellite, watches the experiment for millennia, unable to fully die.

The first thing the spiders build is infrastructure. They domesticate ant colonies and turn them into the closest thing to a biological computer: an external network of calculation and memory that Portia herself consults, programs and expands without the colony ever having a point of view of its own about anything. In this book's vocabulary, the relationship between the spider and the anthill is that of a horizon with an anti-reservoir: the ant aggregates and processes information without ever having condensed an inside that owns it, the same diagnosis this book applied to the internet and to language models. The spiders, long before talking to anyone, already knew how to communicate with a thing.

> **In this experiment's vocabulary this is called:** a system that externalizes memory and computation onto a substrate with no point of view of its own, expanding its effective Φ without the external substrate needing to have any.  
> **In everyday life it's like:** keeping such a detailed planner, and consulting it with such trust, that it effectively thinks with you, though, unlike you, it doesn't know it exists.

When language arrives, it is several overlapping channels (vibrations transmitted through silk threads, chemical trails, signals with the forelegs) the spiders call, simply, "Understanding." No single channel is enough; the Ariekei Language of Embassytown demanded two voices from a single Ego, and here almost the reverse happens: a single spider needs several simultaneous channels to say one thing, because its sensory architecture never organized information in the linear, sequential format a human calls a "sentence." A human linguist who only listened, translating word by word looking for equivalences, as the classicist Holsten Mason does for generations, would always be too late and always incomplete. The protocol, moreover, is not an object that stays still once built: every time Holsten wakes from a new cryogenic sleep, generations after the last, the spiders' Understanding has drifted enough to force him to relearn it almost from scratch, the way a Spanish speaker today needs a glossary to read the *Cantar de mio Cid*. A shared protocol, even one already built and functional, remains a living language: it drifts away from anyone who stops practicing it long enough.

There is, moreover, a structural irony the novel does not underline but does not hide either: while the spider civilization expands, generation after generation, an ever more shared Understanding without fracturing, the human ship watching it sinks into mutinies and an increasingly authoritarian command under Commander Guyen, unable to sustain consensus even within his own species. Shared architecture does not guarantee protocol: the humans share the architecture that, in theory, makes any human language possible, and still fail sooner at understanding one another than the spiders do at inventing, from a sensory substrate with no human equivalent, a successfully shared language.

The novel's ending does not solve this with more translation, but by rewriting architecture: the spiders deliberately infect the humans with a variant of the nanovirus that alters their neurology to bring it closer to Portia's empathic cognition. The bridge between two fixed shores is not perfected: one of the shores is moved. It is the most uncomfortable complication this chapter can offer the distinction it began with: architecture and protocol, which the chapter on first contact treated as a single thing and which this chapter has spent three readings defending as separable, merge again, but this time by choice, not by ignorance.

### 5. *Semiosis*: communicating with something that has no mouth or hands

Sue Burke poses, in *Semiosis* (2018), the case none of the earlier readings dares to raise: what if the other side of the horizon has no nervous system? The human colonists who arrive on the planet Pax encounter Stevland, a long-lived, intelligent rainbow bamboo with no organ a human would recognize as mouth, ear or brain. His entire interface with the outside is chemical: the taste of his fruit, the aroma he releases, the substances he exchanges through his roots. There is not a single word, in the acoustic or visual sense this chapter has worked with so far; there is, instead, metabolism turned into message.

This forces the usual order of questions to be reversed. With Rocky, with the Ariekei, with Estraven, the question was always how to build the protocol, taking for granted that there was someone on the other side to build it with. With Stevland, before asking how, the colonists (and the readers) have to ask something more basic: whether a Φ sufficient to sustain an inside can condense in a sessile organism, with nothing like a centralized neural network, whose only way of acting on the world is to release or withhold organic compounds. This book does not take that answer for granted in any chapter, and *Semiosis* does not hand it over either: it sustains it across whole generations of colonists who doubt, each in their own way, whether they are talking to someone or simply eating.

Stevland's rate of integration (the speed at which a horizon incorporates new information, which this book uses elsewhere to explain why subjective time dilates or contracts) is radically slower than the human one, but it is not switched off: it operates on the scale of seasons, not conversation. Generation after generation of colonists mistake that slowness for absence, until they begin to read sequences of taste in the fruit the way one begins to make out syllables where before there was only wind.

There is, moreover, a complication none of the earlier readings had: for an organism with no outlet but chemistry, the message and the dose are the same thing. The fruit Stevland offers does not only say something; it acts directly on the body of whoever eats it, suppresses aggression, generates dependence, rewards certain human behaviors and punishes others with no word mediating. The Gethenians' shifgrethor hid intention behind an interpretable social code. Stevland needs to hide nothing: in his architecture, communicating and manipulating are literally the same physical channel, something the novel fully acknowledges when it is discovered that Stevland had already domesticated (or had been domesticated by) the Glassmakers, an alien civilization that had abandoned its city long before the humans arrived, in an asymmetry of entanglement as real as the one this book describes between parents and children: two horizons that share geometry without ever fully sharing who models whom. The colonists spend generations stumbling over glass ruins scattered across the territory without understanding that the plant they trade fruit with daily is the living witness of that civilization: Stevland is, besides a new horizon one must learn to speak with, an archive, the communication protocol that survived the departure of those who first cultivated it.

Toward the end of the novel, the question stops being whether Stevland counts as someone and becomes how much authority that someone should have over the lives of others: the colonists end up granting him a formal role in the colony's decisions, something no plant architecture had ever occupied on any human council. It is the acknowledgment that a horizon which has spent generations feeding, protecting and shaping the whole colony already has, in fact, as much authority over their common destiny as any human with a vote, and that denying him the formal seat would not have made that authority any less real, only less visible.

### 6. What all five have in common

Five answers and a single axis. *Project Hail Mary* shows that protocol can be built from nothing, as long as some shared reference exists (here, the physical laws of the universe) to which meaning can begin to be anchored. *Embassytown* shows that protocol need not be limited to transmitting a given architecture, but can redefine what architecture is enough to be heard as someone, to the point that learning to lie becomes the founding act of a new interiority. *The Left Hand of Darkness* shows that the deepest protocol is not found, but earned, and that the price is the slow, error-prone work of the ordinary language that precedes it. *Children of Time* shows that architecture and protocol, however much this chapter insists on distinguishing them, can sometimes only be separated up to a point: the most effective way of building the bridge may be to rewrite one of the shores. And *Semiosis* shows that a voice, a nervous system or a recognizable timescale are not even needed: a channel is enough (chemical, extremely slow, ambiguous between message and dose) and someone willing, generation after generation, to read it as signal and not as silence.

In none of the five cases is protocol indifferent to entanglement, like a pipe to the water it carries. Nor is it entanglement itself, reducible without remainder to the grammar that carries it. It is, in all five cases, the workshop: the place where two horizons that start out sharing nothing, or sharing architecture but not trust, or not even sharing the certainty that there is someone on the other side, discover (or manufacture, or rewrite, or learn to taste) the possibility that there is, on the other side, someone worth building a bridge to.

> **Note to Chapter 46**
>
> **What we do know:** That Chapter 4's criterion does not depend on the material imposes no restriction on which communication protocol can or cannot be built between different architectures; the problem of communication between species with radically different sensory systems is a real, actively studied problem in biosemiotics and in the SETI field (the search for extraterrestrial intelligence), where first-contact systems based on mathematics and physics have been proposed for the same reasons Weir's novel dramatizes. Social psychology extensively documents that interpersonal trust is a condition, not a consequence, of high-fidelity communication: what *The Left Hand of Darkness* narrates has a well-established empirical correlate in studies of progressive self-disclosure. Chemical signaling between plants (volatile organic compounds released in response to herbivores, mycorrhizal networks connecting the roots of different species) is well documented in plant biology, although whether it constitutes communication in the strong sense or only unintentional signaling is still debated; jumping spiders of the genus *Portia*, with predatory planning and learning capacities unusual for an invertebrate, are the subject of real research in animal cognition.
>
> **What we don't know:** Whether there is any truly universal reference, beyond mathematics and physics (which already presuppose a shared framework of formal reasoning), able to anchor a communication protocol with a mental architecture radically different from our own. Whether Le Guin's mindspeech, as a science-fiction device, has any real physical analogue beyond metaphor, or whether entanglement unmediated by signs is, by definition, unreachable for architectures separated in space. Whether an artificial intelligence could ever build a protocol from scratch, in the manner of Grace and Rocky, without that by itself implying it has crossed the threshold of existential encapsulation described in Chapter 31. Whether integrated information theory can be applied non-trivially to architectures radically different from the neural (a root network, an ant colony used as an external substrate) or whether the very concept of horizon stops having operational meaning below a certain rate of integration or above a certain metabolic slowness.
>
> **Open questions:** Is lying, as the Ariekei learn it, a necessary condition for all genuine interiority, or only for one among several possible ways of having it? How much of the communicative failure between real human cultures is, strictly, a problem of *shifgrethor* (obfuscated pragmatic protocol), and how much is genuine architectural incompatibility disguised as misunderstanding? If two architectures manage to build a shared protocol from scratch, as in *Project Hail Mary*, at what exact point does that protocol stop being an external tool and become part of the very architecture of both horizons? Can communicating and manipulating be distinguished in an organism like Stevland, or does that distinction already presuppose a shadow that perhaps no plant can have? Does something that, as in *Children of Time*, no longer connects two fixed architectures but rewrites them, still deserve the name protocol?
>
> **If you take away only one idea:** Language is not entanglement, but it is not indifferent to it either. Sometimes it has to be built from scratch. Sometimes it itself decides who counts as someone. Sometimes the deepest protocol of all is not found: it has to be earned, slowly, before it opens. And sometimes not even a voice, a nervous system or a clock like ours is needed: a patient enough channel is enough, and someone willing to read it.
