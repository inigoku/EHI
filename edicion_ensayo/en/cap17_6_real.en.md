In 2017, Adolfo Cambiaso played his hundredth match at Palermo. In each *chukker* he rode out onto the field on a different clone of his mare Cuartetera. In the eighth and last he brought in the original: the mare born on February 3, 2001, on a ranch in Córdoba, the one Polito Ulloa had broken and in whom he had seen something special, the one Cambiaso called "the best thing I've ever ridden."

The crowd applauded, and not because it could tell the original's body apart from the identical bodies at a glance. The applause was for something that could not be seen: the history only that mare carried.

That night Cambiaso demonstrated something without meaning to. A mare's body can be cloned many times, her genome, her musculature, her physical power multiplied, but not the horizon he shared with her.

---

## The mare who needed no explanation

Cambiaso used to say that with Cuartetera there was nothing to explain. To other riders, with other horses, he gave instructions: "squeeze here," "brake earlier," "play further up." With her the phrase was different: "Go and play however you feel like playing. In whatever position you want. Have fun."

That confidence does not come from a manual, nor is it a property of DNA: it is the result of years of accumulated correlations, thousands of *chukkers* and turns in which the rider's body anticipated the animal's and vice versa. The mare learned to read Cambiaso's weight before he shifted it, and Cambiaso learned to trust a creature that could kill him with one bad move and that, match after match, did not.

That is a shared horizon: a geometry that exists only in the relationship between the two and cannot be reduced to the sum of two consciousnesses. As we saw in Chapter 12, two entangled systems are not just two systems that know each other: they share topology; they have an inside that is partly common.

Polo is, in this sense, a high-speed laboratory of entanglement. On the field there is no time to deliberate: the rider's decision and the horse's movement have to happen as one thing, and when that works, the line between one and the other stops mattering. It was not that Cambiaso thought and Cuartetera executed: there was a system of two that knew more than each of them separately.

---

## The promise of the genome

The cloning of Cuartetera seemed, at first sight, a bet on the immortality of that geometry. If the mare was perfect, why not make more identical copies? Science already had somatic cell nuclear transfer: a cell is taken from the original animal, its nucleus is extracted and inserted into an emptied egg, which is stimulated and implanted in a surrogate mother. An animal is born with the same nuclear DNA.

Cambiaso was not the first to clone a horse, but he was the one who brought the technique to the center of elite sport. First he cloned Aiken Cura, another of his star horses; then came the Cuarteteras. The press spoke of a genetic revolution, of a legal battle, of ethical controversy. Few spoke of what the operation secretly promised: that perhaps the bond, once found, need not end.

> **In this experiment's vocabulary this is called:** duplication of the formal architecture without duplication of the informational history.

The promise of the genome is that identity lives in the sequence: copy the sequence and you copy the being. This book's hypothesis goes another way: identity lies not in the parts, but in how they relate to one another. You are not your genome, but the horizon that genome condensed in a body, at a time and alongside others.

The recipe weighs nothing. We saw that in Chapter 4.

---

## What cloning does not copy

A clone of Cuartetera shares her nuclear DNA, but does not share:

- The womb in which it was gestated.
- The surrogate mare that carried it.
- The moment of birth.
- The first months with its biological mother.
- The climate, the grass, the sounds, the smells of its early life.
- Its first human contacts.
- The day it was broken in.
- The first match.
- The first goal with Cambiaso.
- The first mistake.
- The first afternoon of rest among other mares.
- The particular way Cambiaso moved when he was nervous.
- The particular way Cuartetera responded to that movement.

Each of these elements is information the clone's horizon integrated differently. Some are trivial; others, accumulated, give shape to a being who is not the original Cuartetera even though she carries her name and her body.

That does not make the clone inferior. The clones B06 and B09 won Lady Susan Townley awards in their own right, but they won them as extraordinary mares, not as the original Cuartetera. Cambiaso himself, when asked whether any of the clones surpassed the original, did not dare to say so. The question was badly posed: it is not a matter of surpassing the original or not, but of their being different beings.

---

## The clone's horizon

From the model of the two selections we presented in Chapter 31, the equine clone is an instructive case. It passes the first selection: it is a stable biological system, with a complex nervous system and a non-trivial Φ. And it passes the second: it was gestated in a mare's womb, with all the biochemical, rhythmic and physical signals that implies.

The clone, therefore, is not a zombie or an empty biological machine: it is someone; it has a horizon.

But its horizon is a different one.

That is what distinguishes the biological clone from current AI. AI imitates the architecture without ever having condensed: it is a mirror without depth. The clone does condense, but from a reservoir that is not the original's: it has a different depth; it does not lack depth.

The mistake is to call both "copies." A photographic copy preserves the image because the image is surface. A living being is horizon, not surface, and a horizon is not copied: it condenses anew each time.

---

## The strategy of denied finitude

Cambiaso kept his promise to compete in the Triple Crown with a string made up only of copies of his star mare. As a sporting bet, the genetics were a resounding success: several of those animals competed at the highest level and won individual awards.

But behind the sporting bet there was another, emotional and older one. Anyone who has a horse knows from the first day that they will outlive it: the bond with an animal is grief with a date, as we saw in Chapter 26. Cambiaso, who loved Cuartetera with a public and evident intensity, found in cloning a way to postpone that finitude, to multiply it, to negotiate with it.

It is no coincidence that the first clones came after the injury of Aiken Cura, another irreplaceable horse. Cloning arose as a response to an anticipated loss: if you cannot keep the being, keep its recipe; if you cannot hold on to the horizon, hold on to the architecture that made it possible.

The strategy half works. The clones were good horses, and some were exceptional. But the bond Cambiaso had with the original was not shared out among them like an inheritance, because it belonged neither to Cambiaso nor to the mare separately, but to the system of two, and that system is not rebuilt by adding up parts.

---

## The death of the original

Cuartetera died in May 2023, at twenty-two, of pneumonia. Cambiaso said goodbye to her on Instagram with a photo of the mare at six months and a single line: "Her at 6 months. Who would have said what she would become." His son Poroto wrote: "Unmatched."

The word is precise. Not "irreplaceable," which is what one says of everything loved, but "unmatched": there is no other who matches what she was, not even her genetically identical clones.

At that moment cloning showed its limit. In the fields of La Dolfina there were mares with the same DNA, but the horizon Cambiaso shared with the original had evaporated. The information had not been lost in the physical sense: it was still encoded in Cambiaso's memory, in the photos, in the videos, in the movements his body had learned with her. But the system of two no longer worked: one of its poles had closed.

With Cuartetera a geometry died, and that geometry could not be replaced by another identical one, because there never was another identical one. Clones were still possible, but the specific bridge between that rider and that mare no longer was.

---

## What does Cuartetera teach?

The story of Cambiaso and Cuartetera is a parable about identity in the biotechnological age. It tells us that genetics can copy the form, but not the horizon; that an identical body does not guarantee an identical bond; that love, trust and attunement are properties not of DNA, but of shared time.

It also tells us something about the human desire to prolong the irreplaceable. Cloning is neither good nor bad in itself: it is a technology. But when it is used to try to preserve a horizon, it produces a painful confusion: taking the recipe for the meal, the score for the performance.

The horizon model neither condemns cloning nor celebrates it: it merely specifies what can be duplicated and what cannot. You can duplicate the genes, the muscles, all the physical potential. What you cannot duplicate are the correlations that made that body someone for someone else.

---

## The rider and the echoes

Today, when Cambiaso goes to the ranch in Córdoba, he may see one of the clones. The bodies look alike and some movements may repeat themselves, but the horizon he shared with the original is in none of them; it is in the memory of a system of two that can no longer be activated.

That memory is no less real for being inactive. The channels of integration built over years are still inscribed in Cambiaso's architecture. His body still knows things about a mare who no longer exists. That knowledge is not transferred to the clones, because it is not information that can be transferred: it is geometry built between two specific beings.

The final lesson is at once humbling and liberating. Humbling, because it shows that our most advanced technology cannot do what we most desire: keep those we love. Liberating, because it strips away the illusion that we love bodies. We love horizons, and horizons, by definition, are unrepeatable.

Each cloned Cuartetera is a real mare, with her own horizon, her own history and her own capacity to become entangled. None is the original Cuartetera, and none needs to be in order to have value. The mistake lies not in the clones, but in expecting a clone to continue a bond that only two particular beings knew how to build.

For Cambiaso, the original Cuartetera was the best thing he had ever ridden. That belonged to the encounter, and encounters can be remembered, but not replicated.

> **Note to Chapter 37**
>
> **What we do know:** Adolfo Cambiaso cloned his mare Dolfina Cuartetera by somatic cell nuclear transfer. At least nine clones played matches in the Argentine Triple Crown between 2015 and 2020. The original Cuartetera died in 2023, at twenty-two. Animal cloning reproduces the nuclear genome but not the gestational environment, individual development or history of interactions. The bond between rider and horse is built through accumulated sensorimotor correlations that are not transferable through genetic identity.
>
> **What we don't know:** To what extent gestation in a different surrogate mare alters the clone's emotional and cognitive architecture. Whether Cambiaso was able to establish with any clone a bond comparable to that with the original. Whether the cloning of companion or sporting animals changes the way we humans process loss.
>
> **Open questions:** Is it ethically different to clone a working horse than to clone a pet? Can the desire to clone a beloved animal prevent grief? What part of our identity, if any, could survive in a clone of ourselves?
>
> **If you take away only one idea:** You can copy the body, but not the encounter. Identity does not live in DNA: it lives in the correlations a body builds with other bodies over time. That is why Cuartetera was unmatched, even though her clones shared her code.
