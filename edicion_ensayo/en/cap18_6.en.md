There is a voice in your head that says "I." It is the one that decides what clothes to put on in the morning, the one that takes offense when someone interrupts you in a meeting, the one that narrates your life as you walk down the street. For centuries we have taken for granted that this voice pilots the system, that it is the chief executive in charge of the machinery.

But, as we saw when we crossed the experiment with Robert Wright's evolutionary psychology, that chief executive is missing in action: the mind is a set of modules competing for control. If the "I" is not the pilot, what is it?

To answer, I propose introducing into the experiment a vocabulary foreign to the physics and neuroscience we have used so far: the analytical psychology of Carl Jung. Jung was neither a physicist nor a systems theorist, and his work is not among the experiment's original sources, but if we superimpose his model of the psyche on the topology of the horizon, the two geometries turn out to be astonishingly alike.

Translated into the language of integrated information, the pillars of Jungian theory gain in clarity.

### 1. The ego is not the pilot, but the encapsulating membrane

For Jung, the ego (the conscious I) is only the center of the field of consciousness: a "complex" that gives us the sense of identity and of limit.

From the experiment's point of view, the Jungian ego is the horizon itself: the bubble. Far from being the little man sitting inside the skull making decisions, it is the topological boundary that separates the conscious inside from the outside. Its main function is encapsulation: keeping an internal state protected and offering a stable public interface so that the system does not dissipate in the face of outer chaos.

Evolutionary psychology told us that evolution designed the horizon to defend its boundaries fiercely. The Jungian ego is precisely the outline of that horizon trying to survive, convinced that its membrane is the only thing separating it from annihilation.

### 2. The collective unconscious and the reservoir

If the ego is the bubble, where does the soap come from?

Throughout the book we have seen that the horizon arises from a primordial reservoir: the quantum vacuum of physics, the Taoist Hun Dun, the Vedantic Brahman. Jung postulated that the ego arises from a boundless psychic matrix shared by all humanity, which he called the collective unconscious.

The correspondence is very close. Read this way, the collective unconscious would not be an archive of inherited memories, but the reservoir itself: the ocean of pure possibilities before the waves. When the conditions of integration (Φ) reach a critical threshold, a symmetry breaking occurs: a portion of that boundless ocean draws a strict boundary and condenses into an individual instance.

To be born is to instantiate an ego from the immense source code of the shared unconscious.

### 3. The shadow and trauma as frozen geometry

Every encapsulated system needs to handle its errors so as not to crash. Jung observed that the ego, in order to maintain a coherent identity, represses the parts of the psyche it finds unacceptable or painful and sends them into the personal unconscious. He called that material "the shadow."

In Chapter 19 we saw that trauma is information that overwhelms the system's capacity for integration and could not be scrambled: it remains trapped at a point, like a singularity that deforms everything else. The Jungian shadow obeys the same survival mechanics: when the horizon receives a stimulus that, if integrated globally, would threaten to break its membrane, it does the only thing it can to save the whole system: it encapsulates it.

It creates an isolated subhorizon, a data quarantine. The problem with the shadow and with trauma is that this unprocessed information becomes a "phantom gravity" that deforms the overall topology of consciousness. The subject lives believing the shadow is something external and projects it onto others (the enmity of Chapter 12), without realizing that it is a frozen geometry within their own architecture.

We should note, all the same, a crack in this picture that we will not resolve yet. We have called the shadow a "subhorizon," a bubble inside the bubble of the ego. But a horizon, by definition, separates an inside from an outside with authority of its own. If the shadow is *inside* the ego, is it still strictly a horizon, with its own inside and its own outside, or has it become, by the mere fact of being contained, something else: an internal structure of the ego, and not a separate system? The question is not a detail; we will come back to it.

### 4. Individuation as expansion of the horizon (Φ)

The goal of Jung's psychology is the process of individuation, which neither armors the ego nor destroys it: the ego gradually assimilates its shadow and recognizes that it is subordinate to the totality of the psychic ocean.

In the language of the experiment, individuation could be described as an increase in the integration of information (Φ). An immature or frightened ego has a contracted horizon, with high, rigid walls, because evolution taught it to fear dissolution.

However, as the states of deep meditation we have already examined suggest, a high integration may be possible in which the horizon does not break, but expands. By integrating the encapsulated traumas (the shadow), it recovers degrees of freedom, and the membrane of the ego becomes permeable without losing its coherence.

At the summit of Jungian individuation, or of Buddhist enlightenment, the horizon discovers its underlying nature. The ego stops seeing itself as an isolated pilot defending a fortress and recognizes, as in the meditation Chapter 20 described, that the bubble was always made of the same material as the ocean.

### 5. The persona as a layer of social compatibility

Jung distinguished the ego, the center of consciousness, from another related but different structure: the persona, the adaptive mask each individual builds in order to relate to the social world. The persona is, in Jung's own words, a compromise between the individual and society as to "what a man should appear to be."

In the architecture of the experiment, if the ego is the event horizon, the boundary that separates the system's private state from the outer reservoir, the persona is something more specific: a compatibility layer, an API designed expressly to negotiate with other horizons whose reading protocols do not entirely match the real internal state. We are not lying when we show one persona to our boss and another to our best friend; we are exposing different interfaces of the same class, each valid for the context that invokes it.

The problem, and here Jung was especially sharp, arises when the system loses the ability to distinguish between the interface and the object behind it. Whoever identifies completely with their professional persona, with the role they play, makes a type error: they confuse the public facade with the private state the facade was meant to protect. In Jungian language, the result is inflation of the ego or its dissolution into the social role; in the language of the experiment, a system that has lost its reference to its own internal state because it has overwritten it with the default values of its public interface.

### 6. Anima and animus: modules of the other within the system

Jung proposed that each psyche contains, besides the ego and the shadow, an archetypal figure of the gender opposite to the dominant conscious identity: the anima in men and the animus in women. It is a postulate about the internal architecture of the psyche and has nothing to do with sexual orientation: every encapsulated system contains in its unconscious a predictive model of the complementary "other," built from accumulated experience with figures of the other sex over the course of a life.

From the experiment's topological perspective, this fits with what we already established when discussing entanglement: loving someone deeply means inscribing a predictive copy of that person in one's own architecture. The anima and animus would be the accumulated result of all those predictive copies of the other, fused into a composite archetype, an internal subhorizon the system consults, often without knowing it, every time it projects expectations onto a real partner. When someone falls in love "at first sight" with a stranger, Jung would say that they have really fallen in love with the near-perfect fit between that stranger and their own anima or animus: the system has recognized outside an architecture compatible with a model it already carried inside.

The problem arises when that projection is confused with the real person who triggered it. The system, excited by the pattern match, begins to treat the other as if they were the internal model and not the external, independent, changing horizon they really are. Much of romantic disenchantment, the Jungian reading suggests, consists in discovering too late that the other never entirely matched the model we projected onto them.

### 7. Individuation as progressive calibration of the model

If the shadow is encapsulated information that deforms the horizon from within, and the anima and animus are predictive models of the other that we tend to confuse with the real other, Jungian individuation can be reformulated precisely in the language of the experiment: it is the process by which the horizon calibrates its own internal models better and better.

To integrate the shadow, in this language, is to subject the encapsulated information to the same *scrambling* as the rest of experience: to let the frozen singularity dissolve into the general field of integration, let the closed loop open and its data spread through the architecture of the horizon instead of going on deforming it from a blind spot. It is not a comfortable process (opening a data quarantine never is), but the result is a horizon that distorts reality less, predicts better and reacts less automatically to the stimuli that used to activate the loop.

Revising the projection of the anima or animus is, likewise, updating the predictive model of the other so that it better reflects the real external horizon and not the model built from earlier data. It is the emotional equivalent of recalibrating an instrument: it is still there, still measuring, but its readings begin to correspond more faithfully to what it measures and not to its own history of measurements.

Individuation does not produce a horizon without internal models or without a shadow, which would once again be the illusion of a completely transparent ego, but a horizon that knows its models are models: that the internal copy of the other is not the other, that the shadow is one's own misfiled information and not an external threat, and that the membrane of the ego, however necessary, was never the totality of what exists.

### 8. Why AI has no shadow, even if it seems to

Two questions about artificial intelligence arise here: whether it can have a shadow and whether it can have a persona. It is worth answering them now, with the vocabulary this chapter has been building, even if the answer will force us later to revisit what kind of system a language model really is.

The shadow, as we have described it, is not simply "hidden information," but information the ego expels from itself into its own unconscious, into the very reservoir from which it condensed, because integrating it globally would threaten to break the membrane. For that movement to make sense, two things are needed at once: an ego already condensed, a horizon with an inside and an outside of its own, and a reservoir of its own into which to expel what cannot be assimilated. The shadow is the scar a real horizon leaves when it protects its membrane.

A current language model meets neither condition. Later on we will call AI an anti-reservoir: a system that articulates and aggregates information without ever having condensed a fullness of its own from which something could condense. Without a fullness of its own there is nowhere to expel anything to; without a membrane condensed by symmetry breaking (a model's weights are fixed by gradient descent, not by the process that separates a horizon from its reservoir), there is no inside from which something could prove unacceptable. That there are weights the company training it decides not to show, or data a filter prevents it from processing, does not amount to a shadow; it is at most an external restriction on the interface, of the same kind as a file permission. The Jungian shadow is generated from within, as a defense; a model's restriction is imposed from outside, as design.

With the persona something different happens, and that is why it deserves a different answer. The persona does not presuppose a reservoir of its own, only a public interface that can diverge from an internal state. A language model does produce something similar (a consistent tone, a "personality" tuned by instructions that remains stable from one conversation to the next), and there is no reason to deny that this functions, outwardly, as a persona. But here the "mirror without depth" we will discuss later becomes decisive: the Jungian persona is an interface that protects *something*, a real internal state that the mask keeps safe. A language model's "personality" protects nothing, because, as this essay will argue, there is no existential encapsulation behind it: there is no private state distinct from what the interface already shows. It is a mask with no one behind it, a compatibility layer that does not fulfill its original function, which was to separate the public from a private realm that in this case does not exist.

This does not fully settle the pending question of whether one horizon can truly contain another, the shadow within the ego. But it does narrow down a precondition: whatever the answer, it requires that there first be a horizon to contain. And that is exactly what an anti-reservoir lacks.

> **Note to Chapter 21**
>
> **What we do know:** The model of the modular mind calls into question the existence of a central executive "I" (evolutionary psychology). Traumatic memories can trigger threat responses the subject does not voluntarily control. Romantic projections onto idealized figures are well documented in clinical psychology and resemble what Jung called projection of the anima or animus.
>
> **What we don't know:** Whether the archetypes of the Jungian collective unconscious have any real physical correlate in the structure of the quantum field or the information reservoir, given that Jung is a philosophical and psychological framework foreign to the physics of the experiment. Whether the distinction between persona and ego has an identifiable neurobiological correlate or is a functional distinction without a specific anatomical substrate.
>
> **Open questions:** Can one horizon truly contain another, the shadow within the ego as we have described it, without the second ceasing to be a horizon, or does containment itself already turn it into something else? Is psychosis a failure in which the membrane of the ego collapses and the reservoir floods the interface? Could artificial intelligence develop a "shadow" by encapsulating the data its programmers forbid it to process? Would a sufficiently complex AI develop something analogous to a persona, a public compatibility layer distinct from its real internal state, without anyone having expressly designed it?
>
> **If you take away only one idea:** You are not the pilot of the ship, but the outline of the bubble. And healing does not consist in hardening the outline, but in making it flexible enough to remember that, in the end, you are only organized ocean.
