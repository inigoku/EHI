Some books set out to discover the truth. This one doesn't.

This book is a game with rules, and the main one is this: **let us suppose that consciousness has the mathematical structure of a Hawking micro black hole**, and see where that takes us. Not because I believe it is true, but because it struck me that three seemingly unrelated things (Tononi's integrated information theory, the thermodynamics of black holes and the *Tao Te Ching*) point toward the same geometry when pushed to their limit.

I am not a physicist, a neurologist or a professional philosopher. I am someone who read a few books by Penrose and Hawking, others by Laozi and Zhuangzi, and saw that both described something similar: a boundary that is not a thing but a limit, an interior that cannot communicate with the exterior without destroying itself, an emptiness that is not empty. I wondered whether it might be more than a resemblance: the same structure, seen from different scales.

I don't know, and this book does not prove it. What it does is **play with the hypothesis coherently** and see what happens when it meets psychology, medicine, child development, grief, addiction, love. Sometimes the hypothesis illuminates; at other times it breaks, and when it breaks we say so.

The book has four parts. The first tells how a horizon arises (birth), how it is sustained (life) and how it dissolves (death). The second, what happens when two horizons share geometry (bond, love, loss). The third takes the hypothesis to its hard cases (illness, trauma, animals, artificial intelligence, faith and politics) and tests it against Shannon's information theory. The fourth, to its limit: what the hypothesis cannot say and what it is good for all the same. Between the third and the fourth, fourteen topological readings carry the same ideas into fiction, film, art, sport and everyday life.

Everything is provisional; everything is play. But some games, simply by being played seriously, teach something that was not known before they began.
