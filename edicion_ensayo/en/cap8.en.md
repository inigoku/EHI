In 2013, Maldacena and Susskind reframed the question of what quantum entanglement is. ER=EPR holds that the entanglement between two quantum systems is a geometric connection. Two entangled particles would be joined by a microscopic Einstein-Rosen bridge, a topological structure of spacetime that connects their interiors however far apart their exteriors may be.

The bridge is not traversable: it does not allow information to be sent faster than light. But the geometry exists, and the connection is real in the same sense that any curvature of space is real.

> **In physics this is called:** Einstein-Rosen bridge.

## [SIMULACIÓN ENTRELAZAMIENTO]

As we saw in Chapter 11, two entangled systems therefore share topology: they have an inside that is partly common, and their horizons are not entirely separate.

### The neurobiology of attachment

Before talking about entanglement in adults, we need to understand how it is built. In the 1950s the psychiatrist and psychoanalyst John Bowlby proposed that humans, like other primates, come equipped with a biologically programmed attachment system. It is necessary for survival. Babies who fail to form attachment bonds can waste away to death even when they have food and shelter; René Spitz documented this in orphanages and called it "hospitalism."

Mary Ainsworth, Bowlby's collaborator, designed the "strange situation" procedure: observing how children react when their mother leaves the room and comes back. She identified three main patterns (secure, anxious and avoidant attachment), to which a fourth, disorganized attachment, was later added. More than personality styles, they are neural architectures built in early childhood out of repeated experience with those who care for us.

The attachment system works through specific brain circuits: the orbitofrontal cortex, the amygdala, the hypothalamus, the endogenous opioid system. When the baby is with its attachment figure, those circuits calm down; when separated from it, they activate. The repetition of those cycles (calm, separation, reunion, calm) builds an internal representation of the world as a predictable and safe place, or as a dangerous and unpredictable one.

What Bowlby called the "internal working model" is, in the language of the experiment, an architecture of the horizon: a configuration of integration channels that determines how the system will process the presence and absence of others. Secure attachment creates a horizon able to expand toward others without losing itself. Insecure attachment creates a horizon that, faced with closeness, either contracts (avoidance) or overflows (anxiety).

### The social baseline

James Coan and Lane Beckes developed social baseline theory: the human brain is not made to function alone, and its reference state presupposes access to others. When someone close is present, the brain devotes fewer resources to detecting threats and maintains a more efficient representation of the world. The presence of the other is part of normal functioning.

> **In neuroscience this is called:** the social baseline.

The mechanism is **self-other overlap**: the brain incorporates close people into its own neural representations. Brain activity in response to a threat aimed at oneself closely resembles the activity provoked by a threat aimed at a close friend, but not that provoked by a threat aimed at a stranger. The friend is not represented as "another self" in a figurative sense, but in the same regions and with the same patterns as one's own self.

> **In neuroscience this is called:** self-other overlap.

The other horizon is incorporated into the internal architecture of one's own. Their patterns, rhythms and ways of reacting become part of how the system processes the world. The integration is literal, not metaphorical.

And here is the decisive detail: it depends not on affection, but on **familiarity**.

### Adversarial entanglement

Coan and Beckes point out that self-other overlap is tied to familiarity, not to positive affect. The brain does not incorporate the people it loves, but the people it knows well enough to predict.

In this sense, an adversary of twenty years is one of the most integrated figures a horizon can have. To survive a prolonged conflict, the horizon has to model the other with extraordinary precision: anticipate their reactions, foresee their moves, recognize their patterns before they are complete. The internal model of the adversary is, if anything, more detailed than that of an ally, because prediction errors cost more.

Two horizons locked in conflict for twenty years are as entangled, share as much geometry, as two who have loved each other for the same length of time. The difference lies not in the depth of the coupling, but in its **valence**. The bridge exists in both cases; what changes is what flows across it. Two neighbors who have hated each other for decades know each other better than many married couples.

From the point of view of entanglement, intense love and intense hatred are the same phenomenon with a different sign. The intensity of the conflict measures the depth of the entanglement, not its absence.

> **In physics this is called:** the same magnitude of coupling with the sign of the interaction reversed.  
> **In everyday life it's like:** two magnets: the force is the same whether they attract or repel; only the direction changes.

### Empathy as resonance

Empathy is not imagination, nor does it consist only in "putting yourself in someone else's shoes": it is resonance. Your nervous system reproduces, on a reduced scale, the other's state. Mirror neurons, discovered by Rizzolatti in macaques, fire both when you perform an action and when you see someone else perform it; they do not distinguish between the one acting and the one watching. Your brain "feels" what the other feels not because it imagines it, but because it runs it.

But empathy has limits: we cannot resonate with what we cannot model. A torturer can know precisely what their victim feels (Chapter 16 will return to this) and still not resonate with it; not because they lack mirror neurons, which they have, but because they have built a model of the victim in which the victim's subjective experience does not count. Dehumanization is empathy directed at a model that excludes the other's inner life.

### The ghost: the model running idle

Mary-Frances O'Connor describes the mechanism of grief like this: the brain builds a predictive model of the people close to it, and that model keeps running normally even after the person dies. It is the normal functioning of a system designed to predict.

In love, this produces the grief everyone knows: turning toward where the person should be, making coffee for two, starting to dial their number before remembering. In prolonged conflict, the mechanism is identical, but it expresses itself differently. The horizon does not look for the other: it anticipates them. It knows exactly what they would do in this situation, how they would react to this piece of news.

The ghost is the direct consequence of having built a very precise model of someone who is no longer there to generate the signals that model expects.

Physics offers an image: an incomplete *scrambling*. The other's horizon dissolved and its information was spread through the reservoir, but the surviving horizon is still organized to receive its signals. The integration channels built over twenty years remain open. The entanglement persists even when one of the two poles has disappeared, because it was inscribed in the architecture of the surviving horizon, not only in the relationship between the two.

### When the bond breaks

Not all entanglements end in death. Some end in rupture: divorce, betrayal, abandonment, gradual estrangement. The process is different, but the mechanism is similar.

When a bond breaks suddenly, the remaining horizon has no time to adjust its architecture. The integration channels are still open, but what they receive is no longer the other's signal, but silence, absence or, worse, the signal of someone who is no longer the person you modeled. The result is enormous dissonance: the system predicts one thing and receives another. It is like following the GPS to an address that no longer exists.

Gradual ruptures are different. They give the horizon time to readjust, close channels and rebuild its architecture without the other, but they also give the entanglement time to deepen before it breaks. There is no general answer as to which kind of rupture hurts more: it depends on the architecture of the horizon, the history of the bond and the conditions of the environment.

### The asymmetry of entanglement

Entanglement is not necessarily reciprocal in the same measure.

In requited love, the two horizons model each other with similar intensity, and the entanglement is more or less symmetrical.

In an asymmetric enmity (a troublesome neighbor who hardly thought about the other, while the other spent twenty years accumulating grievances), the entanglement is much greater in one direction than the other. The wronged neighbor modeled the troublesome one with the precision of someone who needed to predict him in order to defend himself. The troublesome neighbor modeled the wronged one much less, because the conflict cost him nothing.

The ghost lingers longer the more asymmetric the entanglement was in that direction: whoever built the most model takes the longest to dismantle it.

The paradox is that the wronged neighbor has spent twenty years paying the other more attention than anyone else has. His notebook of grievances does not prove that he mattered to the neighbor, but exactly the opposite: that the neighbor mattered to him far more.

### The limit of entanglement

There is a limit. Entanglement cannot be total: if two horizons merged completely, they would stop being two. Identity needs a boundary. The deepest love is an expansion of the self that includes the other without losing itself. They are two overlapping circles: the common area is the bond, but each remains a circle.

Contemplative traditions have been exploring this limit for millennia. Advaita Vedanta speaks of the identity between Atman and Brahman, the individual self and the universal one, but also of maya, the illusion of separation. Mahayana Buddhism speaks of the bodhisattva, someone who attains enlightenment, the dissolution of limits, but decides to remain in the world to help others. They could merge, but choose to keep a horizon of their own while sharing the geometry of the whole.

> **Note to Chapter 12**
>
> **What we do know:** Self-other overlap (Coan and Beckes) is real. O'Connor describes grief as the persistence of a model of the absent person. Mirror neurons have been recorded in macaques (Rizzolatti), and there are signs of analogous systems in humans. The attachment system (Bowlby, Ainsworth) is well documented.
>
> **What we don't know:** Whether ER=EPR operates at biological scales. Whether entanglement between brains is physically real. How a predictive model dissolves.
>
> **Open questions:** Is hatred an entanglement with a negative sign? Why do some griefs last decades and others months? Is there an optimal limit to entanglement, or is deeper always better?
>
> **If you take away only one idea:** Knowing someone deeply, whether you love them or hate them, inscribes them in your architecture. When they leave, the architecture goes on waiting for them.
>
> **Further reading:** Coan & Sbarra (2015), Social Baseline Theory; O'Connor (2022), *The Grieving Brain*; Bowlby (1969), *Attachment*; Ainsworth (1978), the strange situation; Rizzolatti & Sinigaglia (2008), mirror neurons; Maldacena & Susskind (2013).
