There is a moment almost everyone has experienced and almost no one has managed to name.

It sometimes happens when you wake up suddenly, in the middle of the night or before dawn. For an instant, half a second or three, you do not know who you are, or where you are, or what time it is. There is perception: the darkness of the ceiling, perhaps a sound, the weight of the sheets. But there is no one perceiving. Or rather, there is something that perceives, but it has no name yet, no history, no recognizable body.

Then the self returns. In milliseconds the story is rebuilt: it's me, I'm in my bed, it's night. The world takes shape again, and that shape includes you as its protagonist.

That instant without a self, before the story returns, is the edge of the horizon: the moment when the horizon, having spent hours contracted in deep sleep, has not yet fully recovered its usual geometry. It is the only moment of the day when most people touch, without meaning to and without knowing it, the boundary of what they are.

---

## The map of the territory

> *"The oar touched bottom where there used to be an echo." — The Habit of Water*

Before going through the states one by one, it helps to have the complete map, and the map has a surprising shape: it is a plane.

For a long time we imagined the spectrum of consciousness as a simple vertical scale: full wakefulness at the top, coma at the bottom and, on the floor, death. The model is intuitive, but it is missing something important.

Contemporary neuroscience has identified two independent dimensions that define a system's state of consciousness. The first is the **level of arousal**, the metabolic activity of the brain. The second is **integration**, how much information the system generates as a whole: Tononi's Phi, which we met in chapter 1. (Φ cannot be calculated in a real brain; what is measured are approximations, such as the perturbational complexity index, which falls in deep sleep and anesthesia and recovers in wakefulness and REM sleep. When this book speaks of "high Φ" or "low Φ" in a brain, it is speaking of those approximations.)

What is revealing is that these two dimensions do not always go together. Wakefulness and deep sleep lie at opposite ends of the integration axis, but deep meditation occupies a place with no equivalent: low arousal and high integration at the same time, at least to judge by the long-range gamma coherence recorded in expert meditators (Lutz et al., 2004). It is a quadrant no other state occupies.

> **In physics this is called:** a two-dimensional space of states of consciousness (arousal × integration).  
> **In everyday life it's like:** a device that measures temperature and humidity: two numbers that do not always rise together.

From wakefulness, then, one can go in two directions. One goes down (sleep, anesthesia, death), and in it the horizon contracts; the other goes diagonally, and in it the horizon expands without the body having to be active. They are opposite movements of the same system.

## The interface of causality

David Hume saw it with a clarity that today's neuroscience sometimes forgets: we never perceive the fabric of causality, only the constant conjunction of events through our own interface. The recordings suggest that low arousal and high integration coincide in deep meditation, a quadrant of the map that no other state occupies. But the map does not say what produces what. There is no visible thread joining the state of the brain to the texture of what meditating feels like, just as there is no observable bridge connecting the electrical discharge of a biological tissue to the private experience of a thought.

And there is something even more uncomfortable: causality itself may belong to the interface and not to the territory. The fundamental physical laws are symmetric in time; the arrow that runs from cause to effect is not written in them, but in the way a system that needs to anticipate in order to survive keeps its accounts of the world. If that is so, asking what causes consciousness might be a grammatically correct and geometrically ill-posed question: like asking what lies north of the North Pole. It fails not because it is hard, but because it starts from a map that ends before it gets there.

## REM sleep: the horizon that looks inward

If you read the electroencephalogram of someone in REM sleep without context, you would conclude it was an awake brain. The high-frequency activity, the cortical desynchronization, the elevated metabolism: everything points to wakefulness. In fact, during REM sleep the brain consumes as much energy as when awake and active. And yet the person is asleep, completely paralyzed, disconnected from the outside world, generating an experience with no point of contact with consensual reality.

How is that possible? It depends on which parts of the brain are switched on and which are switched off.

During REM, the dorsolateral prefrontal cortex, seat of critical thought, metacognition and judgment, receives less blood and shows less activity. It is the region that, when we are awake, lets us say "wait, this makes no sense" or "this is a dream." Without it, the dreamer accepts without blinking that they are flying, talking to the dead, watching buildings change shape. At the same time, the limbic system (the amygdala, the emotional regions) is hyperactive. Dreams carry such an emotional charge because the rational filter that usually modulates emotion has lowered its guard. It is like a theater where the house lights go down and the stage shines brighter than ever.

Here the instant with which we opened this interlude reappears, but seen from the other side.

That waking up without knowing who you are is the threshold of REM crossed in reverse. In REM sleep, the critical self switches off and experience seems entirely real without needing to be: the horizon is closed to the outside but open to the inside. When you wake suddenly from deep sleep, the critical self has not yet switched on, and for that half second there is no one organizing what is perceived into a story.

In terms of the horizon, during REM the horizon does not contract but shifts: it stops being open to the outside world and expands inward. The information it processes is entirely endogenous: memories, emotions, patterns and stories generated from its own resources. That is why dreams seem completely real while they last: there is nothing to check them against.

### Sleep and memory

During sleep the brain does not rest: it reorganizes. Studies show that while we sleep the hippocampus, the structure that encodes episodic memories, replays sequences of activation similar to those of wakefulness, but much faster. It is as if the brain were "rehearsing" what happened during the day, consolidating the patterns worth keeping and discarding the rest.

But there is something more surprising. During REM sleep the brain does not merely consolidate what it already knows: it recombines. Elements of different memories mix and create associations that did not exist before. This is when the brain is most creative, because it is not limited by the logic of waking. Several scientific discoveries have been attributed to dreams, such as Kekulé's structure of benzene or Mendeleev's periodic table.

## Lucid dreaming: the mirror inside the dream

There is, however, an exception: a state in which the horizon of REM acquires, without leaving the dream, something ordinary dreaming never has, the capacity to see itself.

It is the lucid dream, in which the dreamer realizes they are dreaming. For an instant, a small feedback window opens in the horizon: the prefrontal cortex, which was switched off, receives a brief signal. The EEG confirms it with a characteristic peak of gamma waves in the frontal region. For a moment the horizon acquires a mirror-like property: it can watch itself dream.

People who practice lucid dreaming develop techniques to prolong that state: checking the dream's consistency (can I read the same text twice?), anchoring themselves in sensory details or simply reminding themselves that they are dreaming. With practice, some manage to hold lucidity for minutes, exploring what for them is an inner world with the properties of an outer one.

## Deep sleep: the contraction

And then there is deep sleep.

Deep sleep is contraction, a real, measurable, profound contraction.

Its slow waves, delta waves of between 0.5 and 4 cycles per second, are the signature of a massively synchronized brain. In wakefulness, neurons fire out of sync: different groups do different things at different moments, and that asynchrony is precisely what allows a rich integration of information. In deep sleep, millions of neurons fall together into a common rhythm. The horizon does not radiate: it is opaque.

Phi approaches the lowest values possible in a living adult. It does not reach zero, which would be death, but it comes close.

> **In physics this is called:** the collapse of Phi through massive synchronization.  
> **In everyday life it's like:** a concert in which all the musicians play the same note in unison: it sounds powerful, but there is no melody.

### Sleep homeostasis

Why do we sleep? For decades the question had no satisfactory answer. Today we know that sleep is an active, regulated process essential for survival: animals deprived of sleep entirely die sooner than those deprived of food.

The simplest mechanism is chemical. During wakefulness the brain accumulates adenosine, a by-product of neuronal metabolism that works as a signal of "sleep debt": the longer you have been awake, the more adenosine you accumulate. Caffeine works by blocking its receptors; it tricks the brain into believing it is not tired.

But adenosine does not explain everything. There is a second system, the circadian one, regulated by the suprachiasmatic nucleus, a molecular clock in the hypothalamus that synchronizes the sleep-wake cycle with daylight. The two systems, homeostatic and circadian, work in parallel, sometimes in the same direction and sometimes in opposite ones. That is why there are times when you are tired and cannot sleep, or awake and sleepy.

### The synapse and sleep

Giulio Tononi proposed one of the most influential hypotheses about the function of sleep: the **synaptic homeostasis hypothesis**. During wakefulness, synapses are constantly strengthened: every experience, every piece of learning and every new stimulus potentiates neural connections. But they cannot strengthen indefinitely; there are limits of energy, space and noise. If all synapses were equally strong, the system would lose its capacity to tell signal from noise.

Deep, slow-wave sleep is the moment of renormalization. Synapses weaken in proportion to their strength: the strongest ones, those you used most during the day, weaken more than the weak ones. In this way the system returns to a baseline from which it can go on learning. Without this process, the brain would saturate within days.

The hypothesis makes testable predictions. It has been shown that sleep deprivation reduces the capacity to form new synapses, that the intensity of slow waves is related to the amount of learning the day before, and that the selective inhibition of certain synapses during sleep is associated with the consolidation of specific memories.

And it explains something we know from our own experience: we remember nothing of deep sleep, not because it does not exist, but because there was no one there to remember it. The horizon was so contracted that there was no experience to record. That is why the instant on waking, when the self has not yet returned, happens precisely after deep sleep and not after REM. From REM you wake from a dream, from a story, from someone who was doing something somewhere. From deep sleep you wake from nothing.

Is deep sleep, then, the state most like death? Yes, with one decisive structural difference: in deep sleep the synaptic architecture remains intact and basal metabolism active, so recovery is guaranteed. Every night we cross that edge and come back; death is the crossing without return.

But there is something more, something recent studies have found at the threshold of death, which does not appear in deep sleep and which changes the whole picture.

In 2013, Jimo Borjigin's team at the University of Michigan detected something unexpected in rats subjected to induced cardiac arrest: at the moment of collapse, the brain does not slide gently into silence. There is an intense, coherent gamma activation, not the chaotic noise of stress but an organized, synchronized signal more reminiscent of REM sleep than of deep sleep. In 2023 the same team published similar results in human patients.

If the horizon of deep sleep is opaque and contracted, the horizon at the threshold of death does something very different: a last flash of expansion before going out. As if the system, at the instant it loses all possibility of returning, did exactly the opposite of what it does when falling asleep: instead of a final contraction, a final expansion.

> **In physics this is called:** a terminal gamma burst (Borjigin et al.).

## Meditation: the opposite direction

At first glance, deep meditation and deep sleep look alike: stillness, immobility, closed eyes, less discursive thought. An outside observer could confuse them.

But their neurological signature is the opposite.

In deep sleep there are slow, high-amplitude delta waves, massive synchronization and a low Phi; the cortex does not propagate signals beyond its local region. In the deep meditation of advanced practitioners there are high-frequency gamma waves of extraordinary amplitude and long-range synchronization between frontal, parietal and occipital regions: the profile that, in the language of IIT, would correspond to a high Phi, although no one has measured it directly.

The difference is not one of degree but of direction.

Deep sleep contracts the horizon because the system loses the capacity to maintain differentiation. Deep meditation expands it because the system stops needing to differentiate without losing the capacity to do so. It is the difference between a lamp going out and a sun that shines without merging with anything in particular.

What disappears in meditation is not the horizon but the narrative self: the inner voice, the commentator, the manager of the personal story. The horizon remains active, perhaps more than ever, but without the usual filter that selects which information matters and which can be ignored.

Some contemplative traditions describe a state that deserves separate mention: the *turiya* of the *Mandukya Upanishad*, the fourth state, beyond waking, dreaming and dreamless sleep. It is the witness-consciousness that persists even in deep sleep without dream content. The few available EEG studies show highly coherent theta waves superimposed on a background of delta waves. As if the witness were floating on the sea of deep sleep without merging with it.

The Tibetan book of the dead, the *Bardo Thödol*, describes the period between death and rebirth as a sequence of states with a precise phenomenology. What is remarkable is not whether that description is literally true, but that it anticipates with surprising accuracy the phenomenology of the near-death experiences that medicine documented centuries later. Borjigin's gamma burst and the Clear Light of the *Bardo Thödol* might be the same phenomenon described in different vocabularies.

## Insomnia: when the horizon cannot close

There is a disorder that illuminates sleep by showing its absence. Insomnia is the inability to contract the horizon. The person is exhausted, but the system does not reach the threshold of massive synchronization proper to deep sleep. Either the horizon stays ajar (shallow sleep, waking every hour), or it fails to settle into REM (fragmented dreams that do not restore).

The neuroscience of insomnia shows something revealing: insomniacs do not have fewer slow waves, but less coherent ones. The synchronization is partial and irregular, like a choir in which every singer hits the right note, but each in their own time. So the brain fails to achieve the synaptic renormalization deep sleep provides, and the insomniac gets up as tired as they went to bed, sometimes more.

## What the instant of waking teaches us

> *"I left gaps in the archive. There are changes that admit no closure." — The Habit of Water*

We return, at the end, to the beginning.

That half second without a self when you wake suddenly is the system working as it should: it is recovering from a deep contraction and expanding the horizon again, from almost nothing to the complete geometry of the self. What is perceived in that instant, with no one perceiving, is the horizon in its barest state, before the story dresses it and gives it a name.

Ordinary wakefulness is the active horizon, integrated, open to the world and to itself. REM sleep is the horizon turned inward, processing at full power but without outside contact. Deep sleep is the horizon contracted almost to its minimum, the everyday approximation to the state in which integrated information falls to zero. Deep meditation is the horizon expanding in the opposite direction, beyond ordinary wakefulness, toward an integration sustained with very little arousal, something ordinary activity does not usually reach. And death is, perhaps, the final contraction, followed, according to Borjigin's data and the cartography of the *Bardo Thödol*, by a last flash in which the horizon does something we did not expect.

Every night we rehearse contraction without dissolving: we cross the edge and come back. The self reappears in half a second, rebuilds the story and picks up the thread. And the ease with which it returns, the astonishing automatism of that reconstruction, is the best proof that the horizon knows what it is, even when no one is looking.

> **Note to the Interlude**
>
> **What we do know:** REM sleep has the electrical signature of wakefulness, with the prefrontal cortex switched off. Deep sleep shows massive delta waves and low perturbational complexity (PCI). High-amplitude gamma waves have been recorded in expert meditators (Lutz et al., 2004). Coherent gamma bursts after cardiac arrest have been documented in rats (Borjigin et al., 2013) and in some patients (Xu et al., 2023). Tononi's synaptic homeostasis hypothesis has growing experimental support. Adenosine regulates sleep debt.
>
> **What we don't know:** Whether the terminal gamma burst is the neural correlate of near-death experiences. Whether *turiya* is a measurable state distinct from deep sleep. Why selective insomnia affects the coherence but not the quantity of slow waves. Whether the causal arrow belongs to the interface and not to the territory: that is this book's own interpretation, not an established consensus.
>
> **Open questions:** Why does the system expand rather than contract at the threshold of death? Is deep meditation a "third path" between waking and sleep, or something qualitatively different? Can lucid dreaming teach us anything about the nature of the self?
>
> **If you take away only one idea:** Every night you cross the edge of your own horizon and come back. Death is the same crossing, with no guaranteed return.
