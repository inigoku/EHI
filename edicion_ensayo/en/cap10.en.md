### Will as a function of the horizon

What is will? The intuitive answer is that it is the horizon's capacity to direct itself: intention, the impulse that precedes action and guides it.

But the horizon is a field of integration with a topography of its own: some inputs weigh more than others, some directions have more inertia, some patterns are more consolidated because they have been active longer. What we call will is a function that emerges from the field itself, not a force acting on it from outside. The horizon that decides something is not above its architecture: it is inside it, and takes the direction that architecture makes possible.

This is not determinism, in the sense that the decision was written in advance. It is something more precise: the decision arises from a structure with a history and a chemistry, which carries the shape of everything that has configured it. Will is real, the horizon really does steer itself, but it is not sovereign in the absolute sense that moral language usually assumes.

The most accurate image is that of a river. The river flows, and the flow is real: the water really goes from one place to another. But it flows along a bed it has itself been carving out, and changing course, though not impossible, takes time, erosion and sometimes a flood that opens a new path. It is not enough for the river to want to.

From the horizon model, will is the capacity of the integration system to generate a direction sustained over time. Not a one-off act, but a vector with inertia: hard to change, but also hard to stop once under way. And that vector depends on resources. An exhausted horizon, with high cortisol and low serotonin, has a genuinely weaker will than the same horizon when rested. The reason is that the field of integration that generates will is degraded, not that it is less of a person.

Will is a physiological resource.

### The hijacking of the threshold

Addictive substances flood the dopaminergic system with a reward signal that can be up to ten times that of any natural reward. The horizon does not receive greater pleasure, but a prediction of pleasure so overwhelming that it recalibrates the whole system.

The mechanism is this: the dopaminergic system, designed to signal when something is worth repeating, receives a signal of a magnitude no natural reward can generate. To compensate, it reduces the number of available receptors, just as one lowers the blinds when the light is dazzling. When the artificial signal disappears, the system is left with fewer receptors than before, and what used to generate a signal (the bond, novelty, achievement, everyday pleasure) no longer does so with the same intensity. The threshold has risen, and now it takes more to reach it.

And this is literal: neuroimaging shows that the prefrontal cortex, seat of intention, of the capacity to delay and of the evaluation of consequences, loses functional connectivity with the reward system. The horizon still has will; what it has lost is access to the circuits that translate it into action against that particular impulse. The will is there, but the bridge is cut.

It is the **Parkinson's of desire**: the horizon knows perfectly well where it wants to go, and knows that it does not want to go where it is going, but the system does not execute what the horizon commands.

That is why asking someone deep in addiction to use their willpower has the same logical structure as asking a Parkinson's patient to walk faster. The will is there; what is damaged is the bridge between intention and action. And that damage is the foreseeable consequence of what the molecule does to the system.

Besides shifting the threshold, addiction distorts the hierarchy of salience of the entire horizon. The addicted horizon does not merely pursue a substance: it has reorganized its integration system around that axis, and everything else (people, projects, bonds) becomes background noise. The system that assigns importance has been recalibrated toward a single object that responds with an intensity nothing else matches. In the terms of the model, the horizon is left contracted: it integrates less, resonates less and generates less dense time in any direction other than the substance.

Seen this way, recovery is a slow recalibration of the threshold, which takes months and sometimes years and happens above all through the bond: other horizons that sustain the natural reward signal long enough for the receptors to regenerate and the threshold to fall. Oxytocin as an antidote, not out of romanticism, but because it is the molecule that activates the reward system through the pathway of the bond, a pathway that does not wear down the receptors it depends on.

> **In neuroscience this is called:** down-regulation of reward receptors.  
> **In everyday life it's like:** lowering the blinds because a blinding light is coming in: when the light goes, you are left in the dark.

### Dopamine and narrative

One of the most insidious things about addiction is that the system recalibrates not only the receptors but also the story. The human brain is a story machine: it turns experience into narrative, narrative into identity and identity into predictable behavior. In addiction, that machine is put at the service of the impulse.

Dopamine generates not only desire but **prediction**. The dopaminergic system constantly calculates the difference between what you expect and what you get. If the outcome matches expectation, there is hardly any signal; if it exceeds it, there is a burst; if it falls short, there is frustration.

In addiction, the system learns to predict the substance with such certainty that any other reward (a meal, a hug, an achievement) generates an insufficient signal. The brain builds a narrative in which the substance is the only possible source of well-being. It is the only one that generates enough signal to register as positive, whether or not that is true.

### When love becomes the object

Chapter 13 described sexual contact as the only case in which the overlap of two horizons is also physical and the boundary between inside and outside loses its sharpness. That ambiguity of the edge produces the most powerful reward signal the system can generate naturally: dopamine of anticipation, norepinephrine of novelty, oxytocin of contact, endorphins of physical pleasure. The full cocktail. The nervous system is designed, quite literally, for that to matter more than almost anything else.

But that design presupposes that the signal comes accompanied by someone: a real horizon on the other side of the contact, with its own timing, its own unpredictability and its own capacity not to respond. That friction, paradoxically, keeps the system calibrated: the signal fluctuates, no one can control it alone, and the system cannot optimize it without the other's participation.

Sex addiction appears when that condition breaks, when the horizon finds a way to access a version of that signal it can control and repeat without depending on the availability, unpredictability or friction of another real horizon. Pornography, for example, offers infinite novelty on demand: a stimulation that ceaselessly activates the noradrenergic system without the cost of negotiation or the risk of rejection. Dopaminergic anticipation is triggered without ever resolving into a real bond, because there is no other horizon to bond with.

The result is the same as in the general mechanism of addiction: the threshold rises. Real intimacy, with its friction, its silences and its unpredictability, generates a signal that the system, recalibrated by artificial hyperstimulation, no longer finds sufficient. The other has not changed: the reward system no longer registers what they offer with the intensity it now needs.

What makes sex addiction a special case is that the circuit it hijacks is precisely the circuit of love: the same receptors, the same system of dopaminergic anticipation, the same zone of ambiguity of the horizon that Chapter 13 described as the seat of the deepest bond. It is the opposite of an excess of love: the love system chasing the signal of love without the love. Dopamine without oxytocin; the chemistry without the other.

The horizon that has reached that point does not only lose access to the signal of the bond. It also loses the capacity to resonate with another horizon on that frequency, which is exactly the one it would need to recalibrate. The tie is cut at both ends at once.

### Work addiction

There is a form of addiction that culture does not call by that name: work addiction. What looks like excessive dedication is the hijacking of the reward system by an activity that generates a predictable, controllable signal.

Work offers something the human bond cannot: measurable results, visible progress, immediate reward. Every email answered, every task completed, every goal met produces a small dopaminergic burst. The system learns to seek those bursts with the same logic with which it seeks the substance: because they are a more reliable signal than the unpredictability of other horizons.

But work, unlike the substance, does not destroy receptors. It destroys something harder to repair: the capacity to tolerate the ambiguity of the human bond. The work-addicted horizon loses the patience demanded by conversations that never end, silences that never resolve, emotions that don't fit into a *sprint*. The time of the bond, which Chapter 10 described as a density born of resonance, becomes "wasted time" compared with the efficiency of work.

### The horizon born sensitive

So far, this chapter has described addiction as something that happens to the horizon: a recalibration triggered by an external signal that exceeds the system's capacity to respond. But some horizons arrive at that encounter with their threshold already shifted from the start.

The best-documented case is **ADHD**, which involves a dysfunction of the dopaminergic system: specifically, a chronic hypoactivity of the reward system that makes the baseline dopaminergic signal insufficient to sustain directed attention. The horizon with ADHD does not suffer from a deficit of intelligence or motivation in the abstract, but from difficulty generating enough internal signal for a specific task, chosen by reason and not by impulse, to compete with the system's background noise. The horizon knows what it wants to do; the system does not generate the signal that would make doing it feel possible.

The consequences are clear. The horizon with ADHD actively seeks stimuli that raise the dopaminergic signal: urgency, novelty, risk, intensity. Not by character, but by architecture. When it comes across a powerful source of stimulation (a substance, a pattern of behavior, a screen designed to maximize reward), the system responds with disproportionate intensity, precisely because it had been in deficit for a long time. Unlike classic addiction, here the threshold does not rise with use: the system starts out with little signal of its own and with sensitized receptors, waiting.

That vulnerability is a property of the architecture. A horizon with ADHD that develops an addiction has not failed morally: it has followed the logic of a system that seeks what it lacks, and has found it in the wrong place because the right places did not generate enough signal.

### The controlled demonstration

**Parkinson's disease** eliminates all the confounding variables that ADHD still leaves open.

There is no history of trauma, no adverse social context and, in most cases, no prior atypical dopaminergic architecture. There is a disease that gradually destroys the dopaminergic neurons of the substantia nigra and a treatment that tries to replace what the disease destroys. And under that controlled treatment, between fourteen and forty-two percent of patients develop impulse control disorders (pathological gambling, hypersexuality, compulsive shopping, binge eating) that did not exist before.

The mechanism is the one the model predicts. The dopaminergic system, deprived of signal by the disease for months or years, has sensitized its postsynaptic receptors to the maximum, precisely because the presynaptic signal was scarce. When treatment arrives, dopamine falls on a system that was not calibrated to receive it at that intensity, and the result is a relative overdose of the reward circuit even though the dose is, by the parameters of movement, perfectly therapeutic.

The cleanest evidence is that the effect depends on dose and on the type of drug: dopamine agonists, which activate the receptors directly, produce more impulse control disorders than levodopa alone, and the combination of both more than either separately. The dose weighs more than any prior trait of the patient, though it does not decide everything: age, temperament and personal history modulate the risk, and most treated patients never develop these disorders.

That is why Parkinson's is the strongest argument against the moral narrative of addiction. In most cases there is no prior drug use, no addictive personality, no social vulnerability; there is a molecule. And when the molecule changes, the horizon changes, whoever it belongs to.

But the most revealing case, and the hardest to resolve, is the one that combines both conditions.

A person with ADHD who is diagnosed with Parkinson's carries two simultaneous deficits: the structural hypoactivity of ADHD, which has spent a lifetime sensitizing the reward receptors, and the neurodegeneration of Parkinson's, which destroys what presynaptic signal remained. The result is a reward system at peak sensitivity, hungry by architecture and further sensitized by chronic deprivation.

When dopaminergic treatment arrives, there is no correct dose. The dose that treats the motor symptoms inevitably overwhelms the reward system, which has been waiting decades for exactly that signal; the dose that doesn't trigger it doesn't treat the Parkinson's. The horizon is trapped between two failures of the same system: with too little dopamine it cannot move, neither in time nor in the body; with enough to move, the reward system activates in a way it cannot control.

This case dismantles any remnant of the moral narrative. In this horizon there is no place from which will could operate as ordinary language assumes: the field is compromised at once in its original architecture, in its present pathology and in the only available treatment. The will has not failed; it had nothing to stand on.

### Recovery as reconstruction

If addiction is a recalibration of the threshold, recovery is the reverse recalibration. Will has little to do with it: what it takes is time, conditions and the slow regeneration of the receptors the system stopped producing. Studies suggest that the dopaminergic system can take twelve to eighteen months to recover fully, sometimes longer. Meanwhile, the horizon lives in a world in which nothing generates the signal it needs, except the substance it cannot go back to.

The bond plays a decisive role in recovery. Oxytocin, the molecule of the bond, activates the reward system through a pathway that does not wear down receptors. A recovering horizon surrounded by stable bonds receives enough reward signal to sustain itself without the threshold rising again. That is why community-based recovery programs (twelve-step groups, group therapy, therapeutic communities) achieve better results than solitary attempts. What acts there is the chemistry of the bond, more than the morality of the group.

> **Note to Chapter 14**
>
> **What we do know:** The disconnection of the prefrontal cortex by substances and the distortion of the hierarchy of salience (dopaminergic recalibration) are real. Parkinson's treatment can cause impulse control disorders. Work addiction is studied as a behavioral addiction. ADHD increases vulnerability to addiction.
>
> **What we don't know:** Exactly how the serotonin and GABA networks interact with the dopaminergic system in each type of behavioral addiction. How long full recovery of the reward system takes.
>
> **Open questions:** Why do some horizons with ADHD develop severe addictions while others channel that search for intensity into creativity or controlled risk? Is work addiction less harmful than chemical addiction?
>
> **If you take away only one idea:** Addiction is not a moral failure of the will, but the physical hijacking of the horizon's reward circuits and the raising of its threshold. And recovery demands time, not strength of character.
