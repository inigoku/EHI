Chapter {{n:cap_info_intro}} presented information as a relation and mutual information as a first sketch of the bond. Now that love, addiction, the mother-child bond, empathy and grief have shown that bond in action, what remains is to ask about its limit: what capacity a horizon has, what happens when the channel is the system itself, and where Shannon's vocabulary stops being enough.

## I. Shannon's theorem and the limit of the horizon

Shannon proved something with profound consequences for this book: every communication channel has a maximum capacity. Below that limit, information can be transmitted with an error as small as desired; above it, noise wins and the message is corrupted beyond repair.

That limit is a mathematical property of the channel. No matter how much technology is applied: the channel has a capacity and it cannot be exceeded.

Transferred to consciousness, by analogy, it means that a horizon has a finite capacity for integration. It cannot integrate more information than its own architecture allows. A human brain would have a maximum Φ; a dog, another; a worm, another. And that capacity does not grow just by adding neurons: it depends on the architecture of the connections, not on the quantity of matter.

That is why, according to integrated information theory, the worm *C. elegans*, with its 302 neurons, would have a non-zero Φ: its network is small, but complete and integrated. And a twenty-week human fetus, with billions of neurons, would have an almost zero Φ, because its network is still under construction and has not yet become integrated. Information lies not in the number of pieces, but in how they are connected.

### From Shannon to Tononi: information that integrates

Shannon measured the information that travels through a channel. Giulio Tononi, more than half a century later, asked a different question: what happens when information does not just travel, but becomes integrated?

The difference is crucial. According to Tononi's theory, a conventional computer processes information (input, processing, output), but barely integrates it: each part does its job without the state of the whole being more than the sum of the states of its parts. A brain, by contrast, integrates: each region influences the others, and the state of the whole cannot be decomposed into independent parts.

Tononi formalized that difference with Φ: the information a system generates as a whole, over and above what its parts would generate separately. If Φ is zero, the system is decomposable and there is no integration. If Φ is high, the system is irreducible: it cannot be split without losing information.

> **In information theory this is called:** integrated information, Φ, the measure of a system's irreducibility.

Here the connection with Shannon becomes visible: Φ is a measure of information, but of a special kind. It is not the information a system transmits, but the information a system is. It measures not how much it reduces another's uncertainty, but how much the system, as a whole, specifies about its own states. It is information that does not travel outward, but folds inward.

### The horizon as a closed channel

And here we reach the heart of the experiment. If consciousness is an event horizon, and a horizon is a boundary that prevents information from escaping, consciousness is a channel closed upon itself.

Shannon studied open channels: information that goes from a sender to a receiver. But what happens when the channel is the system itself, when information is not transmitted to another but integrated into oneself?

That is what the horizon does: it does not transmit its experience outward (no one can read it), but integrates it inward. What goes out is behavior, words, gestures; the experience itself does not. It is a channel with only one end, the inside. The information does not travel: it folds. It is not communicated: it is lived.

That is, in this model, the fundamental difference between a computer and a brain. The computer is an open channel: information comes in, is processed and goes out. The brain is a closed channel: information comes in, is integrated and stays inside, generating a point of view that cannot be exported.

> **In this experiment's vocabulary this is called:** a closed channel, a system that integrates information without transmitting it outward.

### The information paradox, again

But here a paradox Chapter 2 already raised reappears, and information theory sheds light on it: if the horizon does not let information escape, what happens to it when the horizon evaporates?

In a black hole, Hawking radiation seemed to destroy the information that had fallen in. But quantum mechanics requires that information not be destroyed. The resolution most physicists defend today is that it is not lost: it is *scrambled*, mixed with the environment and made inaccessible, but it does not disappear.

Applied to consciousness, this would mean that the information that constituted a horizon is not destroyed when it dies, but redistributed in the field and becomes part of the state of the universe. No "I" persists, but a trace remains. The theory does not say that trace is legible; it says it exists, that it is mathematically real, and that recovering it would require measuring every particle in the universe and all their correlations, which is impossible in practice.

## II. The bit and the qubit: the difference that matters

Information theory offers one last distinction: the one that separates classical information from quantum information.

A classical bit is 0 or 1; a qubit can be 0, 1 or any superposition of both. A bit can be copied without problem; an unknown qubit cannot be copied (this is the no-cloning theorem). A bit can be read without altering it; a qubit, in general, is altered when measured.

If the information a horizon integrates had quantum properties, those three differences would describe three features of experience we know well: it cannot be copied, it cannot be observed without being modified and it cannot be transmitted as it is. Subjective experience would be non-transferable not because it is hard to communicate, but because it cannot be cloned.

> **In information theory this is called:** the no-cloning theorem, the impossibility of copying an unknown quantum state.

The warning Chapter 11 already gave is worth repeating here: we do not know whether the brain uses quantum information. In a warm, wet medium, decoherence destroys superpositions in fractions of a second, and any model that puts qubits in neurons has to explain how they are protected from that environment. What this chapter holds is more modest: if consciousness is information, and if that information had quantum properties (a hypothesis, not a fact), the no-cloning theorem would naturally explain why experience is non-transferable. The next chapter develops that idea and shows that it also has a classical version, one that needs nothing quantum.

## III. Information is not enough: the limit of the model

Having come this far, it is worth pausing to acknowledge the limit. Information theory explains many things: why consciousness is private, why it has a finite capacity, why it cannot be transferred, why it integrates rather than transmits. But it does not explain why it is felt.

One can describe the information a system integrates, its Φ, its capacity, its entropy; one can predict when it increases and when it decreases. But none of that answers the question of why there is someone in there. Information theory describes the structure of consciousness, not its texture: it is an excellent map of a territory that remains a mystery.

### The gap between information and meaning

Shannon left meaning out on principle. Tononi's theory measures integration, not content. Mutual information measures correlation, not understanding. In every case, what is measured is structure, not lived experience.

Chalmers's hard problem is, put in engineering language, the problem of meaning. We know how information is transmitted; we do not know why any of that information matters: why pain hurts instead of merely being processed, why red is red and not just a wavelength, why there is a difference between having information and being information.

This book does not solve that problem, and no book has. But information theory makes it possible to formulate it with a precision it lacked before: the hard problem is not how information arises from matter, but how meaning arises from information. It is a problem of a higher level, and none of the tools in this chapter (entropy, mutual information, Φ, no-cloning) has the answer.

### Three doors left open

For anyone who wants to keep pulling on the thread, there are three directions the horizon model suggests and that information theory illuminates without closing:

1. **Mutual information as the substrate of the bond.** Chapter 10 spoke of the time of the bond and Chapter 12 of entanglement, but neither used the technical vocabulary of mutual information, which is precisely what measures how much two systems share. Developing it would make it possible to quantify, at least in principle, the depth of a bond: not as a metaphor, but as a quantity.
2. **No-cloning as an explanation of non-transferability.** It is one of the most suggestive ideas in this chapter, and it can be developed without postulating anything quantum in the brain: it is enough to treat experience as a state that is altered by being read. Introspection as a measurement that disturbs what it measures is an idea phenomenology has been exploring for a century, and information theory gives it a formal foundation.
3. ***Scrambling* as a model of grief and death.** Chapter 7 described the evaporation of the horizon as a redistribution of information into the reservoir. Information theory makes it possible to specify what that means: the information is not lost, but becomes inaccessible. That distinction between existing and being recoverable is what grief needs in order to be formulated without false consolation or despair.

Perhaps this is the most honest thing this chapter can say: information theory gives us the best vocabulary available for talking about consciousness without falling into mysticism, but not the final answer. It gives us the question with more precision. The next chapter walks through those three doors.

> **Note to Chapter 34**
>
> **What we do know:** Shannon's information theory is the basis of telecommunications and computing. Mutual information is a standard measure of correlation between systems. The no-cloning theorem is a proven result of quantum mechanics. Tononi's integrated information theory is a theory under development, with defenders and critics.
>
> **What we don't know:** Whether consciousness is integrated information in IIT's technical sense. Whether the brain uses quantum information. Whether information theory will ever be able to explain the hard problem. Whether mutual information is an adequate measure of the human bond.
>
> **Open questions:** Is consciousness a special case of information or something more? Can there be information without a system that integrates it? What happens to information when the horizon evaporates? Is meaning an emergent property of information or something irreducible to it?
>
> **If you take away only one idea:** Information is not a thing, but a relationship. And consciousness is the most intimate relationship there is: the one a system has with itself. Information theory describes that relationship precisely, but it does not explain why it hurts.
