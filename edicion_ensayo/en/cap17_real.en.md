So far we have talked about entanglement as something that happens between two people. But the architecture of the horizon does not stop at the border of our species.

The brain couples with whatever is at hand, and that includes, sometimes more intensely than any other bond, the animals that live in our home.

Admitting it is both humbling and liberating: that the busiest bridge of our horizon may be built with someone who does not speak, does not sign contracts, does not share our grammar of time. And who, despite everything, is there, present, reading our breathing before we ourselves know we are agitated, building with us a common territory that appears on no map.

The question is not whether animals have souls, but whether our horizon has room enough to recognize that theirs exists.

---

## The biological other

Consciousness is a spectrum.

A dog or a cat shares with us the hippocampus, the amygdala and the whole limbic system. Its Φ, its degree of consciousness, is lower than ours, but it is not zero. It has a horizon: it can suffer, it can remember and it can, in the sense of Chapter 13, become entangled.

And the spectrum does not end there. Crows solve problems long thought exclusive to primates. Octopuses, with their brains distributed through their arms, seem to inhabit a mode of consciousness we are only beginning to imagine. Cows form lasting friendships and become stressed when separated from their herd mates. Elephants return to the bones of their dead and touch them with a slowness that seems ritual, that seems, though we cannot prove it, like painful memory.

The horizon model does not require language in order to require compassion; a non-trivial Φ is enough: a system integrated enough for the information circulating inside it to generate a "from within," however humble. A point of view, a perspective, a horizon.

With the dog it goes further. Tens of thousands of years ago, wolves and humans began to calibrate each other. The wolves that tolerated our closeness and the humans who trusted them survived. Generation after generation, the two architectures adjusted until they reached a mutual legibility no other pair of species has achieved. The dog reads our gaze and we read its posture. The dog anticipates our intentions from microsignals (a change in the tension of the hand, an inflection in the breathing) that we don't even know we are emitting. And we learned, without realizing it, to read its tail not as an object that swings, but as a language: position, speed, stiffness, all convey an internal state the dog cannot hide because it does not know how to lie.

That legibility is a resonance built up over millennia.

With the cat, the calibration is more lateral: it more or less domesticated itself. It was the cat that decided to approach our granaries, drawn by the mice, and to tolerate our presence in exchange for protection. The relationship is more contractual in origin, but no less deep in its result. The cat does not read our gaze as faithfully as the dog, although it too can follow a pointing gesture, but it developed another tool: a purr at frequencies similar to those used in some vibration therapies to stimulate bone regeneration. There is no proof that it heals anything, but it is as if coevolution, instead of visual communication, had optimized consolation.

With the horse, the coevolution is more recent, but intense: some five thousand years of war, travel, plowing and, lately, therapy. The horse reads our tension through the saddle, the legs and the distribution of weight. And we learned to trust a creature capable of killing us with a kick, but which almost always chooses not to.

With other animals, the degree of legibility varies. The rabbit lives in an olfactory world we barely understand; the parrot imitates our language, but may not understand it; the fish, although recent studies suggest that some feel pain in more complex ways than we believed, inhabits a horizon so alien that we struggle even to imagine it.

But in every case, in every one with a non-trivial Φ, there is someone on the other side of the bridge.

An asymmetric geometry, but a real one.

## The dog as an evolutionary mirror

The relationship with the dog has something singular that deserves a closer look. Besides understanding us, it has made us understand ourselves differently.

Tens of thousands of years of living together have left their mark not only on the dog's genome, which diverged from the wolf's in genes related to starch digestion and sociability, but also on our lives. Living with dogs changed the way we hunted and organized territory. But it also changed something more diffuse: our capacity to extend empathy beyond the border of the species. The dog was probably the first "other" we learned to see as a subject and not as an object, and that modified something in the architecture of our consciousness.

When a dog looks at us, it activates circuits in us that we usually reserve for humans: regions that light up when we assess a person's intentions also activate at a dog's gaze. There is something in its face, perhaps the result of artificial selection that favored infantile features (broad forehead, big eyes, short muzzle), that triggers an almost reflexive parental response in us.

But it is not only appearance; it is synchrony. After years of living together, dogs and their owners come to show coordinated cortisol levels: when the human is stressed, so is the dog, even with no danger in sight, and when the dog relaxes, the human lets their guard down. The best-documented coupling runs from human to dog; in the other direction the data are weaker, but the system is coupled, like two oscillators that end up beating almost in unison.

In this sense, the dog is an evolutionary mirror. It shows us who we are not because it imitates us, but because it completes us: it amplifies aspects of our horizon that without it would remain dormant, such as vigilance, loyalty or the capacity to enjoy the present without projecting anxiety. A dog does not regret yesterday or fear tomorrow in the human sense, and its presence, free of anticipatory anxiety, serves as a temporal anchor for anyone who lives too much in the future.

## The pet as a child

That legibility explains something increasingly observed: the pet coming to occupy the place of the bond between parents and children.

The human brain is programmed to care. When that instinct, driven by oxytocin and the reward circuits of child-rearing, finds no biological child, it looks for a mold to fit into.

The pet offers the perfect topology. It gives back affection, yes, but it also maintains a permanent dependence. Unlike a human child, who has to learn to fly, the dog or cat never becomes independent: it will not go to college, will not form its own family, will not leave us by its own choice; only death takes it away. In terms of care, pets are perpetual children.

There is nothing pathological in this: it is an adaptation. The human child-rearing system consumes a great many resources and demands an affective return. If the social environment does not offer it a recipient, by choice, through infertility, through loneliness or simply because more and more people reach maturity without children, the brain does not switch off the circuit: it redirects it.

The caregiver's brain calibrates its alert and reward circuits around the animal with a biochemical intensity comparable to what it would devote to a baby. Neuroimaging studies show that when a mother looks at photos of her child and of her dog, overlapping regions of the reward system are activated: the ventral striatum, the ventral pallidum, areas rich in oxytocin receptors. They are not identical (the human child activates areas related to more abstract social cognition, such as theory of mind), but the affective basis is of the same order.

It is the system adapting to a new niche: the adult with no children in their care, whom contemporary civilization produces en masse for the first time. In the nineteenth century, keeping companion dogs with no practical use was a class privilege; today it is a demographic fact.

But there is an important difference the model cannot overlook. The bond with a pet is asymmetric not only in cognitive capacity, but also in power. The human controls the animal's food, outings, medicine, life and death. That imbalance implies an ethical responsibility that the bond between parents and children does not have, or has in a different way: a child, in time, can question their parent, take distance and even, in adulthood, reverse the roles. The pet never can.

That asymmetry makes caring for a pet, in a sense, purer, because it expects no adult reciprocity, but also more vulnerable: the caregiver knows, at some level, that they are solely responsible for a horizon that depends on them completely.

It is a bond of its own kind, with its own topology. The model does not rank: it distinguishes.

## The shared horizon: rhythms and territories

Entanglement with a pet does not happen only in affection. It happens in the architecture of the day.

When there is a dog, the morning has a particular shape: the weight at the edge of the bed, the waiting by the door, the ritual of the walk that turns the start of the day into something shared. The night too: the corner where it curls up, the sound of its breathing, which falls into step with ours, the certainty that someone is keeping watch while we sleep.

These rhythms are the pillars of the bridge. Each repeated walk, each meal at its time, each afternoon on the sofa deposits a layer of shared information in both horizons. The dog learns our schedules better than we do, and we, without realizing it, organize our lives around its needs: getting up early, coming home, planning vacations with an eye to who will look after it.

Physical space changes too. The house stops being ours alone: there is a corner that belongs to the cat, a blanket that smells of dog, furniture arranged according to what a four-legged creature needs to get around. The territory becomes hybrid: half human, half animal, wholly shared.

This interweaving in space and time is what makes a pet's death, besides an emotional grief, a profound domestic reorganization. The house is still there, but it is no longer the same; the schedules persist, but lose their meaning. The seven o'clock walk is still possible, but it has become an orphaned habit.

## The denial of the horizon

If the animal has a horizon and its suffering is real, animal cruelty has a physical status, not just a moral one.

Whoever mistreats an animal starts from a premise: that there is no one on the other side; that the trembling, the howling, the stillness after punishment are reflexes without an inside; that the dog wagging its tail when we come home does so out of a Pavlovian reflex and not out of joy. The model says that premise is false. The damage done to that horizon is real: it changes its topology, leaves a mark, alters its predictive system.

Mistreated animals show hypervigilance, avoidance, reactive aggression, difficulty trusting: the same patterns we call trauma in humans. The difference is one of complexity. Animal trauma acts on fewer layers, but its structural imprint is of the same order. A mistreated dog does not "forget" fear, even though it has no language to tell it: its limbic system registers the threat, encodes the context, generalizes the danger. The animal's body keeps the memory its mind cannot name.

There is also a consequence for the abuser. Empathy is also a brain capacity that is trained by use and atrophies with disuse. Repeatedly denying another's horizon reconfigures the architecture of the one who does it: each time someone looks at an animal and sees an object where there is a subject, they untrain their capacity to recognize subjects in general. That is why the relationship between animal cruelty in childhood and later interpersonal violence has a geometric explanation: the same circuit that denies the dog's horizon, trained for years, ends up being applied to humans. The denial is indifferent to the species of its victim.

But there is a subtler and more widespread form of that denial: the industrialization of animal suffering. We are not talking about the neighbor who kicks his dog, an aberrant but statistically marginal case, but about the legal and economic structure that turns millions of animals with a limbic system into raw material: the sow in a gestation crate where she cannot turn around, the hen that never sees sunlight, the cow chained to a cycle of insemination and milking.

The model cannot tell us what to eat; that exceeds its scope. But it can point out a contradiction: if we recognize that these animals have a horizon, that the sow recognizes her piglets, that the hen establishes complex social hierarchies, that all of them have a non-trivial Φ, the structure that turns them into industrial objects rests on a lie about the nature of conscious matter. It is not only a moral question: it is an inaccurate description of what there is.

Society resolves this tension by segmenting: we love the dog and eat the pig. But, from the perspective of the horizon, that division is arbitrary. No physical property distinguishes the factory-farmed pig from the golden retriever on the sofa in a way that justifies one deserving compassion and the other being material for production. The difference is cultural, not ontological.

This does not mean we should all be vegetarians, but that we cannot pretend the choice has no cost. Eating the meat of an animal with a limbic system, such as a pig or a cow, requires looking away from another horizon. What to do with that is an ethical decision the model cannot make for anyone; all it can say is that what we look away from exists.

## The broken symmetry: when the human dies first

Later we will talk about the owner's grief when the pet dies. First, another, less traveled geometry: what happens in the animal's horizon when the one who dies first is its human?

There are documented cases of dogs that wait for years at the place where they used to see their owner arrive, of cats that stop eating after the death of their reference person, of horses that show signs of depression after losing the rider with whom they competed all their lives.

The model cannot enter the animal's horizon; we have no access to its inner life. But we can measure its external correlates: elevated cortisol, disturbed sleep, active searching for the absent one, reduced exploration of the environment. All this suggests, without proving it but strongly, that the collapse of the bridge happens in both directions.

The animal, especially the dog, organizes its horizon around the human: its schedules, its safe space, its predictive system of rewards revolve around the presence of the one who cares for it. When that presence disappears without explanation, without a body in sight, without a ritual of farewell, without the gradualness that would allow the system to adjust, the animal suffers a structural collapse comparable to the one we would suffer if someone essential vanished overnight.

This should make us think about the responsibility we take on when we cross that bridge. It is not only that we will suffer when the animal dies: if we die first, we leave behind a horizon whose stability depended on us. It is a debt we cannot repay.

## Scheduled grief

The direct consequence of a deep entanglement is that the death of a pet causes a serious structural collapse in its owner's horizon.

Their architecture was organized around the times of the walk, the sound of claws on the floor, the weight on the bed, the emotional regulation the animal provided. With its death, the bridge breaks at one end and the human horizon undergoes a literal self-contraction.

But grief for a pet has an added difficulty: it lacks cultural recognition. When a relative dies, society offers rites, time and validation: funerals, days of mourning, condolences one need not ask for. When a dog dies, those around you often push you to get over it quickly: "It was only an animal," "Get another one," "You knew it wouldn't last long."

Those phrases, seemingly reasonable, are subtle acts of violence. They deny the reality of the bridge that was built and treat the collapse of an entangled state as if it were the breakdown of a replaceable appliance.

From the physics of information, buying another dog to cure the grief makes no sense. The entanglement was not with the concept "dog," but with the exact topology and unique frequencies of that particular horizon: with its way of wagging its tail when you came home, with the smell of its cushion, with the routine only the two of you knew. That cannot be replaced. In time, it is reorganized in memory, but not replicated.

There is something more. Most of us who have a dog know from the first day that we are going to outlive it. It is a grief with a date: the system knows, without quite knowing, that the bond carries the date of its breaking built in. A dog lives ten or fifteen years, if it is lucky; we, if we are, live five to eight times longer. The arithmetic is relentless.

That does not make it lesser, but particular: it is a bond built on a tacit pact with finitude. Every caress carries within it, at some level, the awareness that it is finite; every walk is one fewer of the total. Love for the dog feeds on that precariousness, not in spite of it.

And perhaps, though the model cannot assert this, only suggest it, that is why it teaches something bonds between humans rarely teach: that a bridge can be real even if you know from the beginning that it is going to break.

Between humans, finitude is theoretical until it strikes. With the dog it is numerical, concrete: we know, more or less, how many walks we have left. And that changes the quality of each one, makes it irreplaceable, makes it sacred in its repetition.

Grief with a date is a form of love that includes loss in its definition. And perhaps, only perhaps, it is a school, a rehearsal for the greater ruptures that will come.

---

The human horizon lives entangled with many more things than the binary simplification suggested. With animals, the bridge is asymmetric, but it is built on thousands of years of mutual calibration, and each of these bonds has its own topology and its own way of breaking.

We are not the owners of our pets, but, for a short time, companions on a journey they did not choose and that we do not fully control either. And when the journey ends, as it ends and has always ended, what remains is a horizon that can no longer expand along that path, a shared territory that ceases to exist, a bridge that, though broken, still shows where its pillars stood.

> **Note to Chapter 26**
>
> **What we do know:** That mammals share with us the neural substrates of emotion (Cambridge Declaration on Consciousness, 2012). That coevolution with the dog has produced a unique mutual legibility. That the human brain activates with pets part of the circuits of child care (oxytocin, reward). That mistreatment leaves a real structural imprint on the animal and is associated with diminished empathy in the abuser. That grief for a pet is a genuine collapse, often aggravated by the lack of social recognition.
>
> **What we don't know:** How far the spectrum of consciousness extends in other species. Whether channeling the parent-child bond into a pet fully satisfies the needs of the caregiving system or leaves gaps we don't yet know how to measure. Whether collective anesthesia toward systemic mistreatment can be reversed on a civilizational scale.
>
> **Open questions:** Is the suffering of a dog morally equivalent to that of an industrially farmed pig if both have a horizon? Why does society validate some griefs for animals and ridicule others? What happens in the animal's horizon when its owner dies first?
>
> **If you take away only one idea:** A pet is not an object of consolation or a second-rate substitute, but a real, asymmetric horizon, calibrated over millennia, that occupies a precise place in the architecture of whoever cares for it. And when that horizon goes out, the emptiness it leaves is also real.
>
