Entanglement is automatic and involuntary. Two horizons that have maintained sustained contact become coupled, whether they want to or not, and whether the contact was loving or hostile. The wronged man with the notebook of grievances did not choose to inscribe the other in his architecture: it was twenty years of anticipating the adversary that built that model without asking his permission.

Entanglement is a physical property of the system: it arises from sustained contact the way temperature arises from the motion of molecules. No one decides to have a temperature, and no one decides to be entangled.

But there is something the horizon can decide: whether it **recognizes** that entanglement as meaningful, whether it gives it a name, whether it reorganizes its architecture around that recognition.

In this experiment, love is that act of recognition. Not the entanglement itself, which was already there, but the decision to name it and take on what that name implies.

### Including the other in the self

In the 1980s, Arthur and Elaine Aron developed the self-expansion model: to love means actively including the other in one's representation of oneself. Their resources, perspectives and identity become part of how the horizon conceives of itself. Not as a metaphor, but as a real reorganization of the architecture of self-reference.

The empirical evidence is direct. In the "me / not me" task devised by Aron and colleagues, participants rate personality traits of their own and of their partner's, and afterward they take longer to respond when asked whether one of the partner's traits is also theirs, as if the system had to resolve an ambiguity that did not exist before. The other's traits have come to be processed in the same regions and with the same patterns as those of the self.

Including the other in the self means actively recognizing the entanglement that already existed and reorganizing the internal architecture around that recognition. The loving horizon does not merely have the other inside it: it knows it, names it and accepts the consequences of knowing it.

Here lies the difference the previous chapter could not formulate. The wronged man with the notebook of grievances had his neighbor inside him, as geometry inscribed by twenty years of confrontation, but he never included him in his self: he treated him as interference. The loving horizon does the opposite: it takes the involuntary entanglement that already exists and turns it into something chosen, something that defines who one is.

### The chemistry of love

Helen Fisher divided love into three distinct neural systems that operate semi-independently: sexual desire, romantic love and pair attachment. Each has its own chemistry, its own evolutionary function and its own way of altering the horizon.

**Sexual desire** is mediated mainly by testosterone and estrogens. It is the oldest system, shared with other mammals, and its function is clear: to drive physical closeness with potential reproductive partners. But desire is not love. It can exist without it, and love can exist without desire, something cultures that confuse the two systems find hard to acknowledge.

**Romantic love** is something else. It is mediated by dopamine, norepinephrine and serotonin, and it is a reward system geared toward anticipation, not consumption. The person in love does not seek pleasure, but the possibility of pleasure: every thought of the other activates the dopaminergic circuit of seeking, the same one that lights up when a rat smells food at the end of a maze. That is why romantic love is lived as urgency, as obsession, as an inability to think about anything else: the system is built to keep attention fixed on a single object until the bond is consolidated.

**Pair attachment** is the calmest system. It is mediated by oxytocin and vasopressin. It produces not euphoria or obsession, but calm, safety, the feeling that the world is predictable while the other is near. It is what makes a couple of twenty years feel "at home" without the need for drama or constant proofs. Oxytocin reduces the threat response of the sympathetic nervous system; vasopressin regulates the exclusivity of the bond and, at least in rodents, is linked to monogamy.

The distinction matters because the three systems can come apart. You can desire someone you don't love, be in love with someone with whom you have no secure attachment, or feel deep attachment without desire or passion. Popular culture takes for granted that all three must coincide in a single person "forever," but neuroscience suggests that this expectation is biologically implausible.

### What recognition costs

The cost is concrete: if I include the other in my self, losing them is no longer just losing someone, but losing part of what I am. **Self-contraction**, the Arons' term for the loss of a bond in which the other had been included, is a real contraction of the horizon. Not subjective, in the sense that it "seems" like a loss of identity, but objective: the architecture of the horizon is left reorganized around an absence where there used to be an active presence.

You can stop talking to someone and gradually reduce the entanglement, but you cannot undo having included them in your self. Inclusion, once made, leaves a mark in the architecture of the horizon that no distance erases entirely. That is why a lost love is different from a friendship that ends: not because the feeling is more intense, but because the reorganization of the horizon is deeper. It is like taking down a painting that has hung in the same place for twenty years: the wall keeps the shape and color of what is no longer there.

### Love as a safety signal

A recognized bond (named, accepted, chosen) gives a more powerful safety signal than the same bond without a name.

Coan and Beckes showed that the mere presence of someone familiar reduces the horizon's threat response. Studies of attachment refine that result: what has the greatest effect is **certainty of availability**, more than familiarity itself. A recognized bond is a bond the horizon can rely on: it knows it is there, that it responds, and it knows how to adjust its own activity to that presence. An unnamed bond works in the system like an undeclared variable: the horizon uses it, but cannot refer to it or organize its functioning around something that officially does not exist.

Named love is more efficient than silent entanglement. It does not produce more shared geometry, which was already there, but it lets the horizon make better use of it.

### The fall of love

If romantic love depends on dopamine and norepinephrine, systems of anticipation and novelty, it has a structural problem: novelty runs out. The known cannot be anticipated with the same intensity as the unknown. The dopaminergic system habituates, and the same face, the same voice, the same body generate less and less expectation.

This is biology, not a moral failing of the couple. The novelty-reward system is built to drive seeking, not staying. Staying needs another system: attachment, with its oxytocin and its calm.

For many, the passage from infatuation to attachment is experienced as a loss. The passion fades, the obsession disappears and what remains seems cold by comparison. But what remains is, in many ways, more valuable. Attachment is love on another frequency, one that needs no constant demonstrations because its safety signal is enough. It is fire passing from tall flames to embers: less spectacular, with a longer-lasting heat.

The question every couple has to answer, and which the experiment cannot answer for them, is whether that transition is acceptable or whether the loss of the romantic phase invalidates the bond for one or both horizons. There is no universal answer; only the clarity of knowing what is happening and why.

### The asymmetry of recognition

Unrequited love has a structure of its own. Entanglement is not necessarily one-sided, since it can exist in both directions, albeit with different depths. What is one-sided is recognition: one of the two horizons has named the entanglement and reorganized its architecture around that name; the other has not.

The horizon that has recognized is left in a delicate position: it has taken on the cost of inclusion (exposure, possible self-contraction) without receiving the safety signal that mutual recognition would have generated. It is an asymmetric architecture: the lover's self includes the beloved, but the beloved's self does not include the lover to the same extent. Like an Einstein-Rosen bridge anchored at only one end, the geometry exists, but it cannot bear the weight it was built for.

### Love and time

Chapter 10 described how subjective time depends on the rate of integration. Love alters that rate in a particular way. In the early stages of falling in love, the rate is extremely high: every encounter reconfigures the system, every conversation brings novelty, every shared silence has an unusual density. Time with the beloved is lived as denser than time without them.

But there is an additional effect. When two horizons in love are apart, time stretches as it does not in the absence of other bonds. The wait grows longer, not because the clock slows down, but because the system integrates less information per unit of time: almost all its capacity is taken up modeling the absent one, anticipating the reunion and processing the null signal their absence leaves behind.

### The body of entanglement

So far, this chapter has described love as an informational process: synchronization of predictive models, coupling of temporal rhythms, reorganization of the internal architecture. The description is correct, but it treats the horizon as if it were only an information system, and it isn't. The horizon has a substrate, and it ends where the skin ends.

Sexual contact is the case where this matters especially, because it is the only one in which the overlap of two horizons is also physical. In ordinary entanglement the edges remain intact: each horizon knows precisely where it ends and where the other begins. In sexual contact that certainty becomes ambiguous for a while. Two integrated information systems share the same space, the same skin temperature, the same heart rate and breathing. The horizon does not just include the other in its model: it touches them. And in that zone of contact, the boundary between inside and outside loses its sharpness.

The system does not know exactly where its own integration ends and the other's begins. It does not lose consciousness, but delimitation. It is the experience of a horizon without a clear outline, which contemplative traditions have tried to describe with the same vocabulary they reserve for mystical union, in particular for the dissolution of the outline of the self.

This adds a dimension of its own to grief when there was sex: losing someone with whom one had deep physical contact is not only losing someone inscribed in the internal model, but also the zone of contact in which the horizon existed, at times, without knowing exactly where it ended. One misses not only the other, but also the version of oneself that existed only at that edge.

### Love as a practice

There is a conception of love that reduces it to a feeling, something that happens to you, like rain, and another that reduces it to a decision, something you choose, like a contract. The truth lies between the two. Love begins as a feeling, or as the recognition of an entanglement that already existed, but it only lasts as a practice. Not as an obligation, but as a repeated act of attention, of inclusion, of daily reorganization of the architecture around the other.

Love that is not practiced, not named or actively incorporated into the daily architecture, does not stop being love, but it stops being an active bond. It becomes a dormant entanglement, shared geometry the horizon no longer uses. It may or may not awaken; that depends on someone deciding, at some point, to recognize it again.

> **Note to Chapter 13**
>
> **What we do know:** The self-expansion model (Aron and Aron) and the reduction of the threat response in the presence of a loved one (Coan) have empirical support. The three systems of love (Fisher) are well documented. The transition from passion to attachment is a recognizable pattern.
>
> **What we don't know:** Whether the description of the overlap of horizons in sexual contact has a neuroscientific correlate of merging information fields. How attachment is maintained over the long term.
>
> **Open questions:** How does attachment style affect the speed of self-contraction after the loss of a love? Is the decline of passion inevitable? Can love be a practice without becoming an obligation?
>
> **If you take away only one idea:** To love is to decide to name an entanglement and reorganize your whole predictive architecture around it. And then, every day, to decide it again.
>
> **Further reading:** Aron & Aron (1986), *Love and the Expansion of Self*; Coan & Beckes (2011), "Social Baseline Theory"; Fisher (2004), *Why We Love*; Hazan & Shaver (1987), adult attachment.
