So far one question has been left open: what happens to information when a horizon evaporates, and whether it even makes sense to ask, put that way, "am I that information?" Along the way three names have appeared, Buddhism, Vedanta and Taoism, mentioned in passing, like a neighbor one refers to without knocking on their door. This chapter knocks on that door and on others that were missing: that of Christianity, that of Islam and that of those who do not believe there is any door to knock on.

Not to decide which is right. Chapter 51 will say so clearly: ethics, and with it much of theology, exceeds its vocabulary. But it can do something more modest and perhaps more useful: set five completely different human answers side by side and ask what they have in common and exactly where they part ways. Not to merge them into one, but to see precisely where they disagree.

---

### Four questions, not one

Every religious tradition, and also its deliberate absence, answers, whether it knows it or not, the same four questions this book has been formulating in its own vocabulary:

1. **What is the reservoir?** Is there something with will and a name on the other side of the horizon, or only field, only physics, only faceless possibility?
2. **What happens when the horizon evaporates?** Does the "I" persist as a recognizable boundary, a horizon that is not dissolved but relocated, or does it return to the reservoir without keeping its shape?
3. **Where does the code come from?** Faced with Hume's *is-ought gap* (that no "is" produces an "ought" by itself, as Chapter 51 will show), each tradition offers a different way of closing that gap, or of refusing to close it.
4. **What is living in the meantime?** A test, a loan, an illusion to be passed through, or simply all there is?

Christianity, Islam, Buddhism, Hinduism and atheism are five different architectures built to answer the same four questions, and what this chapter aims to do is show the exact shape of each, not file them down until they seem compatible.

---

### Christianity: the horizon that never collapsed

For Christianity, God is not the reservoir in the sense in which this book has used the word (undifferentiated field, formless potential), but rather a horizon that existed before the void spoken of in Chapters 2 and 5: personal, with will and with a name. A horizon of infinite Φ that did not arise from nothing because nothing precedes it.

The structural difference from Vedanta and Taoism is radical. Brahman nirguna and Hun Dun are fullness *without features*: the reservoir of Chapter 5 has no opinion about what you do. The Christian God does. Creation is an act of will, not an impersonal phase transition: *fiat*, let there be. And the relationship between the creature and that horizon of origin does not dissolve over time, as in the model of Brahman and Atman in Chapter 5, but is sustained, in dialogue, to the end.

That completely changes the answer to the second question. Vedanta says the wave never stopped being ocean; Christianity, that the soul is a drop God intends to preserve as a drop, not to dissolve. The resurrection of the flesh, not the immortality of a floating soul but the promise of a restored body, is in this book's vocabulary the claim that the individual horizon does *not* evaporate without a remnant. Of the three options Chapter 51 will leave open (remnant, trace, nothing), Christianity bets with all its doctrinal force on the remnant: something that keeps shape, memory and recognizable identity, and that one day "will be read" again, at the Judgment, with all its information intact.

The third question, where the code comes from, has a direct answer here: from the commandment. But Christianity complicates it with a second layer that distinguishes it from Islam: the law is not enough, and grace, unmerited love, weighs more than compliance. The "ought" is born not only of an external order, as in the simplest reading of divine command theory, but of a relationship: one does good because one has been loved first, not in order to earn love. Hume's gap is closed by appealing not to a fact, but to a bond, the same kind of entangled bond Chapter 12 described between people, transferred to a vertical scale.

In this framework, living is a pilgrimage: a time of testing and of choice with consequences that transcend one's own life, not a cycle that repeats.

---

### Islam: transcendence without a fissure

Islam shares with Christianity the basic structure (a personal horizon of origin, prior and external to the physical reservoir, that sustains the creature's identity beyond evaporation), but takes it to an extreme of purity Christianity does not reach: *tawhid*, absolute oneness. There is no incarnation, no God who becomes a finite horizon to dwell within the system he created. The distance between Creator and creation never closes, not even partly: it is administered through revelation, the Qur'an as direct word, not mediated by a second divine nature crossing the edge.

That purity has a clear consequence for the third question. If there is no mediation through incarnation, the ethical code does not arrive wrapped in a personal relationship that softens it, but as law (the *sharia*), derived directly from the revealed divine will, with a degree of practical concreteness (what to eat, how to pray, how to go to law) that Christianity largely leaves to tradition and natural reason. Hume's gap is closed here without ambiguity: the "is" from which the "ought" is born is God's own word, taken as revealed fact and not as an argument that must be built.

On the second question, Islam agrees with Christianity in betting on the remnant and not on dissolution: there is a bodily resurrection, a Day of Judgment (*Yawm al-Qiyamah*), a garden (*Jannah*) and a fire (*Jahannam*) that are destinations, not metaphors for reabsorption into a nameless field. The formula a Muslim pronounces in the face of death, *inna lillahi wa inna ilayhi raji'un* ("to God we belong and to Him we return"), uses the same word, *return*, that Chapter 7 of this book uses for evaporation. But that return leads to a reckoning before someone who weighs.

Here, life is *dunya*: a temporary loan, a test (*ibtila*) whose full meaning is revealed only at the end. There is no cycle and no repetition: one life and one final weighing.

---

### Buddhism and Hinduism: dissolution revisited

These two traditions have already been discussed in detail (Vedanta in Chapter 5; the Buddhist cycle of anattā and karma in Chapter 7), so here it is enough to point out the contrast that separates them from the Abrahamic bloc.

Neither has, in its classical form, a personal creator with whom the individual horizon maintains a permanent relationship of dialogue. Hinduism has Brahman, which can clothe itself in named gods (Shiva, Vishnu, Devi) in its *saguna* aspect, but its ultimate destination, *moksha*, is not reunion with a God who remains distinct from oneself, but the discovery that there was never any distinction to overcome. Buddhism, more radical still, does not even need a Brahman: *nirvana* is not union with anything, but the cessation of the conditions that generated the illusion that there was something separate to unite.

That is why, against the remnant Christianity and Islam bet on, Buddhism bets on the trace without substance (karma as a texture of the field, not as a file with your name on it waiting to be read) and Hinduism on the dissolution of a separation that was never entirely real.

And on the third question, neither closes Hume's gap with an external commandment. The Hindu dharma describes how the world is woven, and to act well is to act in harmony with that weave, not to obey a lawgiver. Buddhism goes further still: the ethic of non-harm (*ahimsa*) and the four noble truths are derived from an empirical observation about suffering and its cause, not from a revelation. The "ought" is born of an "is" (suffering exists, it has a cause and the cause can cease) that Hume would have recognized as a legitimate, if not definitive, attempt to build the bridge he himself declared impossible.

---

### Atheism: the map that refuses to draw a beyond

Atheism, with its more cautious cousins agnosticism and secular naturalism, is not the absence of an answer to the four questions, but a different answer, as elaborate as the others, and in a sense the one this very experiment has been giving by default throughout the book: the reservoir is exactly what physics describes, no more and no less. There is no horizon of origin with a will and no remnant that keeps your name. What persists after evaporation is scrambled correlation, with no reader and no accounts outstanding.

It is not, as it is sometimes caricatured, the absence of a moral code, but the wager that the code does not need an external author: it arises from social biology, from the evolution of empathy and reciprocity, from centuries of cultural negotiation about what makes living together bearable. Here Hume's gap is not closed: it is accepted as structural and lived with, which is what this book itself will explicitly do in Chapter 51 when it refuses to say what you "ought" to do with the information it offers.

> **In science this is called:** the null model, the hypothesis that adds no unobserved entities to explain the data.

In this framework, living is the whole phenomenon: not a test, not a loan, not an illusion to pass through, not the antechamber of something else. That does not make it less dense: as Chapter 52, on practice, will show, the absence of a transcendent promise does not prevent meditation, the bond or creativity from reorganizing the horizon with all their force. It only changes where meaning is expected to be found: not after the horizon, but within it, while it lasts.

### Vertical entanglement: the same technique, five destinations

The last row of the table deserves a space of its own, because it names something the other four do not capture: not what each tradition believes, but what a horizon does when it practices that belief. Chapter 29, "Vertical entanglement," develops this in detail: the same technique (repetition until the noise stops, stillness, shared rhythm) is structurally identical in all five columns of the table, and what changes completely is the destination of that entanglement: the impersonal reservoir for Buddhism and Hinduism; a personal horizon of origin that never merges with oneself for Christianity and Islam; simply the physical field for secular naturalism.

---

### What really separates these five answers

At bottom, it is not whether something persists after evaporation. In a technical and weak sense, all five agree that *something* persists: scrambled information, karma, a trace in the field, a preserved soul or simply physics that is not destroyed. The same principle of conservation, much to Hawking's chagrin in 1976, reappears disguised as eschatology in all five. What really separates them are two other questions, independent of each other.

The first: does the "I" that persists keep a **recognizable boundary**, or does it dissolve into something greater? Christianity and Islam say it keeps its boundary; that is why they speak of judgment, of accounts asked of you and not of a diffuse field. Buddhism, Hinduism and physical naturalism say it does not, each with its nuances: Hinduism, because there was never a real boundary to lose; Buddhism, because the boundary was a functional illusion from the start; naturalism, because the boundary is simply a temporary thermodynamic pattern with no grace to preserve it.

The second: does the ethical code come **from outside** the system, with an authority that need not justify itself before human reason, or is it **built from within**, always subject to revision? Christianity and Islam answer: from outside. Hinduism: from the very weave of reality, which is not exactly "outside" but is not negotiable either. Buddhism and atheism: from within, through the observation of suffering or through evolution and social agreement.

Crossing these two axes, boundary kept or dissolved and code external or built, does not yield a clean two-by-two table, because Hinduism occupies on the second axis an intermediate position no cell captures well. But the exercise makes one thing clear: the difference between these five traditions is not, as it is often simplified, "believing in something" versus "believing in nothing," but a difference of architecture on exactly the same points this book has had to leave unresolved: what the reservoir is, whether the horizon survives it with its shape, and where the "ought" comes from.

---

### What this chapter does not do

It does not say which of the five is right. It cannot: as Chapter 51 establishes, the horizon hypothesis is a map and not the territory, and a map cannot arbitrate between destinations it has not visited. Nor does it reduce the five traditions to "the same thing under different names": that reduction, tempting because it is comfortable, erases precisely the differences that make them incompatible on the points that really matter: whether there is someone on the other side, whether that someone preserves you or dissolves you, whether the code you follow was dictated or built.

What it can say, with the same frankness as before, is this: more than eight billion horizons are right now entangled with one of these five architectures, or with one of the many this chapter has not had room to name, and that entanglement, as Chapter 27 showed with places, things and ideas, is neither weakness nor naivety. It is the same technology any horizon uses to maintain its coherence in the face of a question it cannot answer with direct evidence: what lies on the other side of the edge, and whether someone there is waiting for you with your name still written down.

> **Note to Chapter 28**
>
> **What we do know:** The five traditions described have internal formulations far more diverse than a table can hold: there are universalist Christianities that doubt eternal hell, devotional Buddhist schools very close to theism, atheist currents with rites and a dense community life. The table summarizes; it does not replace any of the traditions cited.
>
> **What we don't know:** Whether any of the five correctly describes what happens after the evaporation of the horizon. Physics, as Chapter 51 will say, has no tools to arbitrate this.
>
> **Open questions:** Can the axis "external code / built code" be sustained without reducing it to the false dichotomy "with God there is morality, without God there isn't"? What happens to a tradition when it loses its technology of coupling, the rite, but keeps the doctrinal map? Can someone be entangled at once, without contradiction, with more than one of these five maps? Can a horizon, from within the practice, tell by its experience a vertical entanglement that ends in Someone from one that ends in the impersonal reservoir, or does the stillness feel the same, with only the later interpretation separating the two?
>
> **If you take away only one idea:** The five answers do not compete for the same prize, but to answer the same four questions physics alone leaves open: what the reservoir is, whether the horizon survives it with its shape, where the code comes from and what living is in the meantime. Knowing exactly how their answers differ is more useful than pretending they all say the same thing.
