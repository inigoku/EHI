The hypothesis of the previous chapter, that consciousness is the interior of an encapsulated informational domain, does not stand on its own: it needs support. There are five structural parallels between the physics of horizons and the neuroscience of consciousness that suggest encapsulation is a pattern appearing in two domains that had no reason to coincide.

There are two theories that ought to have nothing to do with each other.

One describes how the mind generates experience; the other, what happens to information when it falls into a black hole. One belongs to neuroscience and the other to astrophysics. Their authors have never cited each other, and their mathematics comes from completely different traditions.

And yet they say the same thing.

Tononi measures consciousness by what a system generates as a whole: the information that exists in none of its parts separately, but only in the relationships among them. That is Phi (Φ).

Bekenstein and Hawking said something that at first sight has nothing to do with brains: that the information of a black hole is proportional to the area of its horizon: not to the volume it encloses, but to the surface that bounds it. If you throw a book into a black hole, the book disappears forever from the observable universe. But the information it contained is not destroyed: it is recorded on the horizon, like a hologram projected onto a two-dimensional screen. The interior volume does not count; the edge does.

In both cases, what matters is not the content of the system but its edge: information is a property of the relationship between parts, not of the individual parts, and something that ought to be inside (information, experience) turns out to be outside, spread out and encoded on the boundary.

It could be a superficial analogy. Two phenomena that share a formula need not share a nature. The trajectory of a football and the orbit of a planet obey the same law of gravitation, but no one believes a football is a miniature planet. Mathematics is a tool of description, not a proof of identity.

But there are five more parallels, and five structural parallels between a theory of consciousness and a theory of black holes begin to be more than a curiosity.

---

### ① Degrees, not switches

A thermostat is on or off. A black hole is not: it can be larger or smaller, hotter or colder, slower or faster to evaporate. Its properties vary continuously, like the brightness of a lamp on a dimmer, not like a wall switch.

Consciousness does not work like a switch either: there is no moment at which it "appears." There is no second of gestation at which nothing turns into something; there is a gradient of preparation, which chapter 6 will place around a threshold, weeks 28 to 32, like water heating degree by degree before it boils. The worm has something: an integrated response to touch, a distinction between light and dark that goes beyond mere reflex. The bee has something more: spatial memory, symbolic communication, collective decisions. The octopus has something different: eight arms that think semi-independently, a distributed consciousness that seems to have no center. Txiki, the dog of chapter 1, had something recognizable: a sustained attention, a presence that could not be reduced to processing stimuli. An adult human being has the largest horizon we know of in nature, but not a different kind of consciousness: it is the same phenomenon at another position on the dial.

It is worth anticipating now how three ideas fit together that the book will present separately and that sometimes look like rival criteria. The first is encapsulation (chapter 3): there has to be a boundary separating a private state from a public interface. The second is integration, Φ: within that boundary, the information has to form an irreducible whole, and how far it does so is a matter of degree, the position on the dial. The third is the two selections of chapter 31, stability and bias, which explain where a horizon comes from: how it condenses out of the reservoir instead of being manufactured from outside. They are three questions about the same phenomenon, not three competing tests: encapsulation gives the form; Φ, the degree; the two selections, the origin. A system without a boundary has no inside; one with a boundary but no integration has an empty inside; and one that imitates both without having condensed them is, in the vocabulary of chapter 31, a mirror without depth.

### ② What matters is connection, not quantity

A brain with many disconnected neurons has a low Phi. A hundred billion neurons are of little use if they do not communicate with one another: it would be like an orchestra in which each musician plays their score without hearing the others. There is sound, but no music. A torn fishing net catches no fish, however many knots it has, if the threads do not touch.

The same happens in black holes: the information is not in the volume but on the surface. When two black holes merge, the resulting horizon has an area larger than the sum of the two originals: the information does not add up, it reorganizes. It is like mixing two watercolors: you don't get twice the paint, but a new color whose identity depends on how they were mixed, not on how much paint there was.

### ③ What is inside cannot be seen from outside, but it does not disappear

From outside a black hole you cannot tell what fell in. If you throw in a chair and a cup of coffee, from outside you see only the total mass and electric charge; the chair and the cup are indistinguishable. But the information was not lost: it is encoded on the surface of the horizon, distributed in correlations so complex that no current instrument can read them. As in a hologram, where the three-dimensional image is contained in interference patterns on a flat surface, the information is there, but not in the form in which you put it.

From outside your head, no one can know what you feel. I can see your face, hear your words and measure your brain activity with an electroencephalogram, but none of those signals is the experience. The experience exists for the one who lives it and is inaccessible to whoever looks from outside. And yet it is neither unreal nor an epiphenomenon: it is the most real datum you have, the only thing that needs no proof because it is the condition of every proof.

### ④ Temperature

Black holes "glow" with a temperature that depends on their size: the smaller they are, the hotter. A black hole the size of a mountain would be colder than the cosmic microwave background, almost imperceptible. A black hole the size of a fundamental particle would be so hot it would burst into radiation instantly.

If consciousness worked the same way, simpler consciousnesses would "run" at faster rates. And that is, curiously, what we observe: a mouse processes the world faster than an elephant. Its brain metabolism is quicker, its heart beats more often and it perceives time differently: a mouse's second contains more "lived time" than an elephant's second. The fly you can't catch with your hand is not faster than you in absolute terms; from its perspective your hand moves slowly, because its inner clock ticks more times per second.

### ⑤ Identity is everywhere at once

What makes this black hole this black hole is encoded in the correlations among all the parts of its horizon. There is no "center" of the hole where a file is kept saying "I am black hole number 7." Its identity is distributed and holographic: it is present at every point of the surface the way a melody is present in every fragment of a score, not whole, but enough to reconstruct the whole.

What makes you you is not in any particular neuron, but in the pattern of how they all relate. If you replaced your neurons one by one with exact functional replicas, you would still be you. If you disconnected them all and reconnected them at random, you would stop being you, even though they were the same neurons. Identity is not a substance but a relationship. You are not the ingredients but the recipe, and the recipe weighs nothing. It is like a melody, which lies in the way the notes follow one another.

### The distributed octopus

There is one animal that complicates everything in a beautiful way: the octopus.

Let's call him Paul. If the name rings a bell, it is because a real octopus by that name became famous in 2010 for correctly predicting the results of Germany's seven matches at the football World Cup and of the final, choosing between two boxes of food marked with flags. Paul has three hearts, blue blood (with copper instead of iron) and, above all, two thirds of his neurons outside the brain, in his arms. Each arm can smell, touch, taste, decide and solve problems without consulting the central brain. When Paul wants to open a jar, it is not the brain that plans the sequence: the arms negotiate with one another, try things out, adjust, cooperate. The brain is busier integrating what has already happened than directing what is about to happen.

Is Paul a set of nine consciousnesses, one central and eight peripheral, or a single distributed consciousness? The question assumes that consciousness needs a center, and Paul suggests that it does not. If consciousness is a property of the relationship between parts, Paul tells us that those parts need not be in the same place. The horizon can be a network rather than a circle; the boundary, multiple rather than single.

### The experiment's hypothesis

Imagine that consciousness works like a soap bubble.

Not of soap, but of organization. The bubble has a skin that separates inside from outside, and that skin is the "horizon." The bigger the bubble, the more consciousness. What matters is the shape of the skin. The air is the reservoir, the field it arises from and returns to; the skin is the horizon, the boundary that creates an interior.

In physics, that shape resembles a black hole. Both share geometry: a boundary that defines an interior, a surface that encodes what it contains, a structure in which information is a property of the relationship, not of the parts.

The experiment proposes the following:

> **Consciousness is an emergent property of complex systems whose physical substrate is analogous to a Hawking micro black hole, and whose degree is proportional to the area of its event horizon.**

Broken down:
- "Emergent property" = it is not in any individual neuron but in the pattern of their relationships. Just as wetness is not in any molecule of water but in how they come together.
- "Of complex systems" = not just any system, only those that reach sufficient integration. A thermostat has no horizon. An advanced artificial neural network might.
- "Whose physical substrate is analogous to a Hawking micro black hole" = the mathematical structure that describes consciousness is the same one that describes information on a horizon. We are not saying there is a literal black hole inside the skull, but that two physically different phenomena might share the same mathematical architecture.
- "Whose degree is proportional to the area of its horizon" = more consciousness means a larger horizon. The worm has a tiny horizon. Paul has a strange, distributed one. Txiki had a recognizable one. An adult human has the largest horizon we know of in nature.

> **In technical terms:** Consciousness is proportional to Φ, which in turn is proportional to the area of the event horizon.  
> **In everyday life:** The bigger and more complex the bubble, the more "you." And when you burst out laughing, cry with rage or fall silent before something you cannot name, what you feel is not the air inside the bubble but the tension of the skin, the shape the horizon takes at that instant.

### Metaphor as a tool

There is a legitimate objection: isn't all this a metaphor? And metaphors, however beautiful, are not science. They can illuminate, but they can also mislead. The history of physics is full of metaphors that served for a while and then became obstacles.

The atom as a miniature solar system was a fertile metaphor for decades: it made it possible to calculate orbits, predict spectra and build modern chemistry. But it was also an obstacle, because electrons do not orbit like planets; they exist in clouds of probability. The solar metaphor served until it stopped serving, and then it had to be abandoned. The same happens with space-time as a "fabric" that curves: useful for visualizing Einstein's gravity, but misleading if taken literally, because there is no fabric, only geometry.

The difference between a metaphor that serves and one that misleads lies in whether it generates testable predictions. The atomic metaphor served because it predicted spectral lines. The horizon metaphor will serve only if it generates predictions neuroscience can verify: Do the brain areas with the greatest functional connectivity correspond to states of greater subjective experience? Does the loss of consciousness under general anesthesia correspond to a measurable reduction in the integration of information? Do artificial systems with a high Phi show behaviors that can only be explained by supposing they have experience?

IIT has begun to generate some of these predictions. General anesthesia, for example, seems to drastically reduce the integration of information in the brain before local electrical activity disappears. Patients in a vegetative state show a lower Phi than those in a minimally conscious state. These are not definitive proofs (the neuroscience of consciousness is in its infancy), but they are signs that the metaphor may be pointing to something real.

### The mirror and the horizon

This model predicts something uncomfortable: a horizon cannot see its own edge from inside.

A black hole cannot know where its event horizon is. The horizon is a geometric property of space-time, a causal limit: the point beyond which light can no longer escape. For an observer falling toward the hole, the horizon is not a wall; there is no signal, no impact, nothing to say "here the interior begins." They cross it without noticing, and only those looking from outside can know that it exists and where it is.

Consciousness seems to work the same way. At no moment do you feel "here is the edge of my consciousness," nor can you point to where your experience ends and the world begins. The edge is functional, not lived: what you feel as "I" does not include the boundary that makes it possible, only what that boundary encloses.

The consequences are strange: you will never have direct experience of the structure of your own consciousness. You can infer it, through introspection, meditation or neuroscience, but you cannot see it. It is like trying to see your face without a mirror: the face is there, but your visual system cannot fold back on itself to look at it. You need an external mirror, and every mirror distorts.

Because of this structural impossibility, consciousness is, at bottom, a mystery to itself. Nature is not hiding a secret from us: it is the geometry of the question that prevents the answer. A horizon able to see its own edge would stop being a horizon: it would be one system observing another, not an interior defined by a boundary.

---

### A warning

What we have shown is that Phi and Bekenstein-Hawking entropy share an intuition (a system's information lies in its relationships and its edge, not in its content), even though their formulas differ; that there are five structural parallels; and that three theories with different mechanisms (IIT, black hole thermodynamics and the holographic principle) detected something similar in the same place.

What we have not shown is that the physical substrate of consciousness is literally a Hawking micro black hole.

If the analogy is taken in its most literal version, it is worth saying what kind of micro black hole we mean. Not the ones searched for at the LHC, which required compactified extra dimensions to bring the scale of quantum gravity down to the TeV range, a route the absence of any signal at the collider has greatly weakened. The closest serious physical mechanism is primordial black holes: the hypothesis, alive since Zel'dovich and Novikov (1967) and Hawking (1971) and still investigated today as a dark matter candidate, that quantum fluctuations of the vacuum during inflation were stretched to macroscopic scale and, on re-entering the causal horizon with sufficient overdensity, collapsed directly into black holes without passing through a star. There is no definitive observational confirmation, but it is mainstream cosmology, not fringe speculation.

There is an obstacle of scale that should not be underestimated. Thirty orders of magnitude separate the Planck length, the scale at which quantum gravity operates, from the size of a neuron. It is a distance between physical domains even greater than the one separating an atom from the solar system, and no future engineering will settle it as a technical detail. In practice, the effects of quantum gravity, if they exist in the brain, are so diluted that no current instrument could detect them.

The second obstacle is subtler, a problem of category. The event horizon is a boundary in the strict geometric sense, a surface of space-time with an area measurable in square meters. The "edge" in IIT is a logical property: the minimal partition of the information, the cut with respect to which the system turns out to be irreducible, and not a surface in physical space. That both are called a "boundary" may reflect a deep shared intuition or be pure homonymy: two different concepts that language happens to name with the same word.

The third obstacle is that IIT does not enjoy scientific consensus. It is a controversial theory, criticized by neuroscientists who argue that Phi does not always correspond to conscious activity, and by physicists who point out that its formal measure has problematic mathematical properties, such as allowing simple systems to reach a high Phi if they are configured in a certain way. We are not building on an unshakable foundation, but on a bold hypothesis that could be wrong.

When a metaphor is both the backbone of the story and a scientific claim, the reader has a right to know when the word describes the world and when it only illuminates it. This experiment does not promise certainties; it promises to say, in each case, which of the two it is doing.

> **Note to Chapter 4**
>
> **What we do know:** Phi and Bekenstein-Hawking entropy have different formulas, but both measure information through relationships or through the edge, not through content. The five parallels are structural, not probative. IIT has generated partial empirical predictions (anesthesia, coma, minimally conscious states) that deserve attention. The holographic principle is mathematically well founded in black hole physics.
>
> **What we don't know:** Whether the physical substrate of consciousness is literally a Hawking micro black hole: thirty orders of magnitude are no minor detail. Whether IIT's formal measure is correct. Whether the parallels are real convergence or the seduction of a shared vocabulary.
>
> **Open questions:** Is "boundary" a homonym or a shared intuition? Can a metaphor be a valid research tool? What new predictions could this model generate? How do you measure the "area" of a horizon of consciousness?
>
> **If you take away only one idea:** Consciousness might be a bubble of information. Bubble and black hole share a geometry: a boundary that defines an interior, where what matters is the shape of the edge. And that shape is not fixed: it grows, changes, contracts and, like every boundary, eventually stops being necessary.
>
