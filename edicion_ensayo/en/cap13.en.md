Almost everyone who has lost someone they loved deeply knows an experience that ordinary vocabulary does not capture well: the feeling that the absent person still occupies a place in one's inner architecture. Not as a memory in the usual sense, but as a shape, as a space, as the geometry around which the horizon has organized itself for years or decades.

---

As conventional psychology has described it, grief is the gradual processing of that loss until the horizon reorganizes and the absence stops hurting. From the perspective of the experiment, that description falls short. The architecture of a horizon that has lived for a long time deeply entangled with another does not "reorganize" in the sense of returning to the state before the entanglement. Either it is rebuilt around the absence, and the absent person remains, transformed, as an active shape within the architecture, or it is not rebuilt at all, and the horizon sinks, sometimes for years, sometimes forever.

No way of *moving on* consists in undoing the other's inscription. The only question is what to do with the inscription that remains.

### What grief is not

Chapter 12 explained that the model of the other that persists after their death is a predictive system that keeps running without the data that calibrated it. The example was the neighbor: a hostile relationship and a model built out of vigilance, not love.

Grief proper is something else. When the entanglement has been loving, when, as we said in Chapter 13, the horizon recognized it and chose to keep it, what persists after the loss is not only a model of the other, but the part of one's own architecture that was built around that model.

In Chapter 15 we said that the bond between mother and child is not inference, but injury. Grief for someone deeply loved is structurally closer to maternal pain than to ordinary empathy. The horizon does not simulate the other in passing: it has built itself, over decades, around their presence. What is lost, therefore, is architecture.

### The architecture with a hole

When two horizons spend forty years entangled by love, besides keeping many memories of the other, each carries inside the geometry that presence has carved out, the way water carves its bed in stone over time. That geometry does not disappear when the water goes.

A widow who does not want her husband to dissolve, the protagonist of one of the stories that accompany this book, puts this intuition with a precision theory struggles to match: *it's the way I'm made inside. Every move I make, I make against that shape or with it.* She is not speaking figuratively. She is describing what the neuroscience of grief is beginning to document: the brain that has lost someone it was deeply coupled with does not process the world as it did before. The neural networks that had organized themselves around the other keep firing at everything that evokes their presence, their voice, their gestures. The horizon keeps predicting them, keeps making room for them, keeps reserving half its inner time and space for them for months or years after they have ceased to exist.

We can call it **asymmetry after dissolution**. The other has returned to the reservoir, and the part of the entanglement that was on their side has been redistributed through the field, into correlations no instrument can read as a story anymore. But the part that was on the survivor's side is still there, intact, without the complement that closed it: a riverbed without water, one half without the other, an architecture with a hole.

### The rites of grief

Over millennia, human cultures have developed technologies to contain grief; not to resolve it, but to contain it. Funeral rites, periods of mourning, dark clothing, temporary abstention from certain activities: none of this is superstition, but social engineering.

The rite does two things at once. First, it marks publicly that something has changed: the community acknowledges that the surviving horizon has undergone a structural reorganization and that for a while it cannot function normally. Second, it offers a provisional container for the chaos. Within the rite, grief has a recognizable shape: there are times to weep, to be silent, to remember and to take up life again. The rite does not speed up grief; it gives it form.

Cultures that have done away with the rites of grief, that expect people to "get back to normal" within days, produce something dangerous: grief without a container. The horizon that is not socially allowed to be broken tries to function normally, and the effort of keeping up appearances consumes the same resources it would need to reorganize. The result is frozen grief: an architecture that does not collapse, but does not rebuild either.

### The model that keeps running

The cognitive machinery responsible for this is relatively well understood. During sustained entanglement, the brain builds a complex predictive model of the other: how they move, what they say, what they will probably think in each situation. That model runs continuously, in parallel with the perception of the world, and anticipates the other's responses before they happen. That anticipation is what makes possible the synchrony of the bond we discussed in Chapter 10: two horizons that have been together for years do more than predict each other; they anticipate together what is about to happen.

When one of the two dies, the model of the other is not erased. It keeps running and keeps producing predictions, and each one, when it is not confirmed, when the expected response does not come, generates a prediction error that the system interprets as pain.

And, this is what neuroscience has been slow to recognize, the predictions don't stop because the system cannot stop making them without dismantling part of itself. The model is not a module that can be removed: it is distributed throughout the architecture of the horizon and involves memory, perception, emotional regulation and the sense of time. Switching it off would mean switching off much of what makes that horizon the one it is.

Seen this way, grief is the difficulty of going on being oneself without dismantling half of one's own architecture.

### Complicated grief

The clinical literature distinguishes, not without controversy, between normal grief and prolonged or complicated grief, and usually bases the distinction on duration and intensity. There is a more precise way of formulating it, consistent with what has been said so far: grief becomes complicated when the architecture of the horizon cannot reorganize around the absence without risking structural collapse.

For some people, the other was literally a large part of the structure. The bond had become an integrated support: the model of the other had taken over functions of emotional regulation, internal validation, orientation in time and in one's own story. On losing them, those horizons face not a wound in the architecture, but the possibility that it will crumble entirely.

In those cases, the system usually reacts by protecting a minimal integrity at any cost. It can do so in ways that seem contradictory: actively resisting acknowledging the loss (going on acting as if the other were there, keeping their things untouched, talking to them aloud) or, on the contrary, contracting until every emotion, every desire, every internal activity that might reactivate their model and hurt again is flattened. For the horizon, both are the same strategy: preventing the architecture from trying to reorganize, because reorganizing would mean risking collapse.

### Unrecognized grief

Not all grief is for a death. There are losses culture does not recognize as such, and those who suffer them are left without the rites that would give them shape.

Miscarriage is one of them. The mother's horizon had already reorganized around a presence that had not yet condensed, and the loss is no less real for being invisible to others. But culture has no rites to contain it: no funeral, no public mourning, no social acknowledgment that something has been lost. The mother goes back to work and no one knows that her architecture has reorganized around an absence she cannot name.

The death of a pet is another. To those who don't have one it seems exaggerated; to those who do, it is the loss of a horizon that, though not human, was deeply coupled to their own. The predictive model of the dog (when it wakes up, what it eats, how it moves) had been running for years, and the absence of that signal produces prediction errors as real as those of any human loss.

A breakup is also unrecognized grief. The other is still alive, but the entanglement the horizon had named as love has been cut. The architecture reorganized around that bond cannot "move on," because the inscription persists. And culture, which rewards "getting over it," pushes the horizon to recover quickly, without understanding that recovering requires reorganizing, not denying.

### Grief in the body

Grief is also physiological. Studies show that people in deep grief have weakened immune systems, altered heart rhythms and persistently elevated cortisol. The "broken heart" is not a metaphor: the stress of grief can trigger takotsubo cardiomyopathy, a disorder in which the heart changes shape under extreme emotional stress.

The grieving body has a regulatory system that has been disrupted by the reorganization of the horizon. Sleep fragments because the prediction system stays active at night. Appetite disappears or soars because the reward system has lost its main reference. The exhaustion is real: the brain spends resources maintaining a model that no longer has any data to feed it.

### Continuing to be two

What the clinical literature has begun to call, with some caution, *continuing bonds* offers a more precise formulation. The question is not whether the other remains in the architecture, because they will, they already do, and there is no way to prevent it, but how the relationship with that presence that no longer responds is reconfigured.

The widow in the story had sensed this without putting it into words. She does not want to dissolve him or forget him; she wants to carry him inside without his weight keeping her from moving. That formula, *carrying him and going on*, is what defines a viable grief. What happens is a reorganization: the architecture does not recover the symmetry it had when there were two, but it learns to sustain the asymmetry without collapsing.

It is a matter of recognizing that the dead person is there, without emitting, in the geometry of the living horizon, and of accepting that this geometric presence is the only one possible now and that, in a way, it is enough.

### The loss of a child

There is a case that defies everything said in this chapter. The loss of a child, especially a young one, does not fit the model of grief for a loving entanglement: it is something deeper.

Chapter 15 described motherhood as the manufacture of a reservoir. When the child dies, what is lost is not only an entanglement, but the very condensation the mother helped produce: the horizon she manufactured, which contained part of her own architecture and whose cells she carries in her body. The microchimerism that physically bound her to her child is still there after its death. The mother literally carries fragments of a horizon that no longer exists.

This grief has no name to fit it. More than "complicated grief," it is impossible grief. The model cannot predict how a horizon that has lost the condensation it manufactured reorganizes itself. It can only say that the reorganization, if it comes, takes decades, not months, and that often it does not come.

### The experiment does not console

The horizon hypothesis does not resolve grief; it allows it to be formulated more precisely, and that precision reaches something ordinary language does not: the difference between losing someone who was in the architecture (recognized love) and losing someone who was in it without anyone knowing (unnamed love, unresolved conflict, a relationship that never closed). The shape of the hole depends on the shape of the entanglement that left it.

The experiment cannot tell anyone how they should feel. It can tell them, with a little more clarity, what it is they feel: not the absence of something that was, but the presence of something that still is, in the form of an architecture that can no longer be updated. Grief is the name we give to the work of learning to live with an inscription that cannot be erased and no longer receives updates.

It is not a consolation, but it can be a companion.

> **Note to Chapter 17**
>
> **What we do know:** The DSM-5-TR (2022) included "prolonged grief disorder" as a diagnostic category, not without controversy. There are neuroscientific models of grief according to which the brain keeps waiting for the absent person (O'Connor). Continuing bonds are a recognized clinical model. Takotsubo cardiomyopathy is real. Unrecognized grief (miscarriage, pets, breakups) has documented effects.
>
> **What we don't know:** How long grief should last. Whether medicalizing extreme sorrow pathologizes it. How a horizon reorganizes after the loss of a child.
>
> **Open questions:** Are continuing bonds an adaptation or an avoidance? How can healthy reorganization be told apart from averted collapse? Why do some cultures heal grief better than others?
>
> **If you take away only one idea:** Grief is not forgetting, but learning to walk with a backpack you cannot take off. And sometimes the wisest thing is simply to keep walking.
>
