At this point the experiment has to change register.

So far, the reader has walked through the physics of horizons and integrated information theory. They have seen how a black hole encodes information on its boundary, how Phi describes the irreducible integration of a system and how a bubble, as it closes, suddenly creates an inside and an outside. It might seem that all this is still physics, but from here on it is no longer only physics.

What follows is a philosophical interpretation that uses those concepts as scaffolding. The central thesis of this book is this:

> *Consciousness is the interior of an encapsulated informational domain.*

It is neither a metaphor nor a poetic way of talking about the brain: it is the hypothesis that organizes everything that comes after.

---

A conscious system would not simply be an information processor observable from outside, but an architecture with two levels of access. From the outside we see only its public interface: behavior, language, neural activity, temperature, movement, response. From the inside, by contrast, there is a private state: the unrepeatable way that information is integrated for someone. That private state is the inside that appears when information reaches a sufficient degree of integration and is protected by a boundary.

Classical neuroscience does not find thought under the microscope for the same reason an outside observer cannot directly inspect the interior of an event horizon. Thought exists, but it is not public. What the scanner sees are correlates, surfaces, emissions, changes of flow, electrical signals: the interface. Subjective experience is the system's private state, and asking for it to appear as an external object is to confuse levels of observation.

> **In physics this is called:** access dualism; public interface vs. private state.
> **In everyday life it's like:** seeing the outside of a lit house from the street: you can see there is light inside and shadows moving, but you cannot know what is being said inside without going in, and once you go in you are no longer an outside observer.

## Encapsulation as a cosmic law: from nature to engineering

When software engineers coined the term *encapsulation* and the principle of *information hiding* (David Parnas, 1972), they were not inventing a design rule out of nothing: they were formalizing, for artificial systems, the same principle nature has been using for billions of years to build its **hierarchy of abstraction**.

In the real universe, complexity is not managed flat: physics and biology handle it by packing states and behaviors into sealed layers. Quarks are encapsulated in protons; protons are packed into atoms; atoms form molecules; molecules, organelles; organelles, cells; and cells, organisms.

Each level of this hierarchy works as a boundary of abstraction. A cell processing glucose does not calculate the quantum states of the electrons in its carbon atoms; it has no need to, because that immense lower-level complexity is hidden behind a simplified interface (chemical gradients and molecular signals). If the universe did not encapsulate information in this way, reality would be an unstable, noisy quantum soup: any fluctuation at the Planck scale would immediately propagate destructive effects to the higher levels, and no stable structure could arise. Thanks to encapsulation, scales can exist and interact safely.

When software engineering reached its own crisis of complexity in the twentieth century (the problem of flat, tightly coupled code prone to global failures), system designers were forced to imitate this natural order. They discovered that to build complex, stable systems (operating systems, networks, applications) they had to draw boundaries of abstraction: group related logic and data inside an object or module, hide its implementation details behind a public API and forbid direct access to its internal variables (`private`).

Consciousness, according to the horizon model, is the last level of this natural hierarchy of abstraction. The brain is an enormously complex biological system, with trillions of chemical fluctuations and neural firings every second, and it encapsulates all that low-level parallel computation behind a single simplified interface: the first-person perspective, the self. The first-person perspective is what that private state is when seen from inside; the public API is everything else (behavior, words, gestures) through which the horizon deals with its surroundings while hiding the cellular implementation, and that lets it make decisions in real time without drowning in its own execution data.

## The hard problem reformulated

The hard problem of consciousness (why is physical processing accompanied by subjective experience?) begins to be reformulated here. Perhaps it is not a matter of explaining how a mental substance miraculously arises from matter, but of recognizing that our expectation of a completely public universe was mistaken. If reality admits encapsulated domains, if there are regions where inside and outside are not symmetrical, then subjectivity is not an anomaly but what an encapsulated informational domain is when seen from inside.

We do not live, then, in a dualism of substances. We live in an **access dualism**.

This is something different, and more manageable. Substance dualism holds that there are two kinds of things in the world, matter and mind, radically different and with a mysterious interaction. Access dualism holds that there is only one kind of thing, but that certain of its structures can be accessed in two mutually exclusive ways: from outside, as an object; from inside, as a subject.

---

From outside, the horizon is a boundary. From inside, it is a world.

And the interior of that horizon, that inside no one can inspect from outside without destroying its condition as an interior, is what we call consciousness.

There is no literal black hole inside a head. But perhaps there is something just as strange: an informational boundary that, as it closes, defines an interior. And that interior, while it lasts, says: **I**.

> **Note to Chapter 3**
>
> **What we do know:** That encapsulation is a standard design principle in software engineering (Parnas, 1972). That neuroscience measures correlates of experience, not experience itself, is a point on which very different authors agree (Nagel, Chalmers). That access dualism does not require postulating any new substance.
>
> **What we don't know:** Whether every domain with a high Phi is conscious, or whether encapsulation is a necessary but not sufficient condition. Whether the public/private distinction is fundamental or emergent.
>
> **Open questions:** Can an encapsulated system be conscious without a high Phi? Where is the threshold of encapsulation? Does current AI have inaccessible private states, or only hidden ones?
>
> **If you take away only one idea:** Subjective experience is not an anomaly in a public universe, but what an encapsulated informational domain is when seen from inside. The chasm between matter and mind is not ontological, because there are not two substances; it is architectural: there are two levels of access to the same structure.
