## [ILLUSTRATION TP: "The limits of the horizon"]
*A circular horizon blurring at its edges, like wet watercolor. Inside the circle, sharp landscapes: a city, a family, a face. At the edge, the shapes dissolve into drops that fall toward the void. Indigo and gold tones, an atmosphere of farewell.*

---

There is a way of getting lost that is worse than death. Sometimes it doesn't even hurt more; the terrible thing is that the horizon fades while it is still there. Death, at least, has the decency to be an event, with a before and an after, a moment when someone ceases to be there and those of us who remain can weep, remember, close. Alzheimer's is a process, and what it destroys is organization. The building does not collapse; the blueprints are erased, brick by brick, room by room, while the occupants go on sitting in the living room without knowing that there is no longer a kitchen, or a bedroom, or a way out.

I have seen whole families bend under the weight of this progressive forgetting. A sudden loss at least sets its own grief in motion; this one repeats every morning: the husband who asks where his wife is while she stands in front of him, the mother who does not recognize the son who looks after her. A small death every day, every day a slightly narrower horizon, until the world shrinks to a room, a window, a ray of light that comes in and means nothing because there is no longer any story to give it context.

Alzheimer's forces us to ask what consciousness is if it can crumble like this, little by little, without a total collapse. If memory is the armor of the self, what remains when the armor rusts? If identity is a building made of stories, who lives in the ruins?

## The blurring edge

In the early stages, the forgetting seems ordinary: where did I leave the keys, what's that actor's name, what did I come into this room for. We all have such lapses, and almost always the name comes back an hour later or the keys turn up in the pocket of a coat we don't remember putting on. In youth, these lapses are anecdotes; in middle age, they bring a twinge of anxiety we cover with a forced laugh. In the first signs of Alzheimer's, they are omens the patient senses but does not name, like someone who hears the foundations of their house creak and prefers to think it's the wind.

The difference is that in normal aging the horizon gets them back: the information was still there, only access was slow; the synapse worked, but the path was clogged. In Alzheimer's, the information ceases to be there. The bookcase stays where it is; it is the books that disintegrate. Beta-amyloid accumulates in plaques between the neurons like an invisible mold. The tau protein unfolds and tangles inside them into neurofibrillary tangles, as if the internal structure of each neuron had decided to abandon its shape. The neurons do not die all at once: they lose their axons, their dendrites, their capacity to communicate with their neighbors. Tissue stops being tissue and turns into silence.

Normal forgetting is a library with a broken catalog: the book still exists, it is just hard to find, and sometimes it takes the librarian, a clue or the smell of paper that evokes the title. In Alzheimer's, the library itself disintegrates. The bookcase keeps its hollow shape for a while, but the book is gone. And the cruelest thing is that the librarian, who is the patient himself, keeps walking the aisles in search of something he no longer knows, feeling that something essential is missing without being able to name it.

From the horizon model, normal aging is a slowing of access; Alzheimer's, a degradation of the architecture itself. Not all the neurons die at once: the connections die. The horizon does not switch off; it tears and loses area. The boundary that separated inside from outside becomes porous, irregular, full of holes through which what used to be contained escapes: the daughter's name, the address of the house where he lived for forty years, the meaning of the word "hunger." The horizon becomes an oil slick on water that breaks up into smaller and smaller drops, each isolated, each a world without context.

In systems design terms, Alzheimer's is a **catastrophic failure of encapsulation**. In programming, an encapsulated object guarantees that its internal state is protected from outside modification and maintains its structural invariants. If its boundary degrades, the private state begins to become corrupted and to leak. System calls try to use broken pointers or areas of memory that have already been freed. The public interface (the API that allowed interaction with the patient) returns errors or becomes opaque, while the internal "librarian" walks aisles whose indexes have been wiped from the allocation table.

There is something especially heartbreaking about the intermittent lucidity of the early stages. The patient knows that he forgets, knows that something is slipping away. It is like trying to hold water in your hands: you feel there is something there, but every time you close your fingers there is less. That awareness of forgetting, what neuropsychologists call metamemory, is the last guardian of the horizon and the first to go out. When it goes, the patient stops knowing that he does not know. The horror gives way to a kind of blind peace, but first it leaves a deep mark on those watching from outside. Watching someone lose themselves is witnessing a dissolution that should not be visible.

## The returning reservoir

In Chapter 6 I described birth as a condensation: the reservoir closing in on itself to form a horizon. Alzheimer's is the reverse movement, but not a symmetrical one. What happens is a dissolution from the edges, uneven, an erosion that advances at different rates depending on the region of the brain, very far from an orderly opening toward the reservoir that would undo what was done at birth.

Episodic memory, the story of the self over time, is the first to go. Autobiographical memories fade in reverse order of acquisition: first the recent, then the intermediate and finally the oldest, as if the horizon were unrolling backward and undoing the story of the self in the opposite direction. The patient stops remembering what he had for breakfast, then what year it is, then that his brother died ten years ago. Sometimes he relives conversations from his youth with surprising clarity while failing to recognize the person in front of him. The past is not erased all at once, but from the present backward, like a tide going out and leaving only the oldest foundations exposed.

Then semantic memory goes: what an apple is, what "husband" means, what a church is, what a car is for. The world loses its labels. The objects are still there and still seen, but they are no longer *things with names*, only pure presence without category. In intermediate stages, the patient may still recognize an apple as food, as something round and red, but the word "apple" no longer floats beside it. Language comes away from the world like a seam coming unstitched. And without language, the world becomes immense and unintelligible, a chaos of nameless shapes in which everything is new and, therefore, nothing is familiar.

Then language itself falls. Not only the words that designate things, but grammar, syntax, the capacity to join subjects and predicates. First concrete nouns disappear, then complex verbs, then subordinate clauses. In the end there are monosyllables, gestures, sounds. The horizon does not contract toward the center, but from the edges, like ice melting at its ends. The image is of a territory losing its borders at random, not of a sphere shrinking while keeping its shape: isolated islands of ice remain in waters that no longer remember winter.

What remains in the final stages is not "nothing." That is the most common mistake, the one that leads some people to speak of the patient as if he were already dead. What remains is a minimal horizon: perception without story, present without past or future, sensation without a name. The patient can feel cold, hunger, restlessness, calm. He can look at a face and feel something he cannot call "love," but which works like love in his body: the pulse that slows, the breathing that calms, the pupils that dilate in a non-declarative recognition. It is a horizon so reduced that from outside it seems empty, but from inside, from the perspective of the system itself, it is still a here, a now, a something.

Some patients in advanced stages still respond to the music they heard when young, to a child's voice, to the touch of a familiar hand. Not because they "remember" in the ordinary sense, since explicit memory no longer works: there is no mental image of the song or association with a specific moment of youth. Those frequencies resonate in the residual horizon, in the correlations that have not yet broken. It is like a guitar with only two strings left: the whole melody cannot be played, but the remaining strings vibrate with the implicit harmony of the entire song. The body remembers what the mind has forgotten. Some networks hold out much longer than others: those of musical and procedural memory, those that sustain the emotional response to a familiar voice, the brainstem that keeps setting the rhythm of life; and that despite the fact that the hippocampus, the gateway to episodic memory, is among the first to fall.

This is what distinguishes Alzheimer's from death. In death, the horizon evaporates as a whole; it is a continuous process, but it keeps its internal coherence to the end, and the information is scrambled as a unit. In Alzheimer's, some parts persist while others are gone. The patient can be "closer to the reservoir" at some moments than at others; the boundary fluctuates. Sometimes, for seconds or minutes, a familiar face clears the fog and the horizon expands for a moment, recovering connections lost months ago. Then it closes again. It is a door that opens and shuts at random, gives a momentary glimpse of the garden that was, and returns us to the empty room.

## Time that no longer flows

When episodic memory dissolves, time stops being a river and becomes a puddle. There is no yesterday or tomorrow; only this instant, and this one, and this one, each without cause or consequence, without history. The patient does not live in the present like the mystic who has reached timelessness through discipline, but because time has broken in two for him.

From outside, the patient's time looks like a tragedy of repetition: he asks the same question every five minutes, wants to see someone who died years ago, doesn't understand what he is doing in that house. But from inside, every question is new, every anguish is the first, every confusion is original. The patient does not suffer the repetition because he does not register it. We who watch and see the loop suffer it; he lives a spiral he does not know is closed.

This loss of narrative time bears an unsettling resemblance to certain advanced contemplative experiences. Mystical texts speak of the "eternal now" as a liberation. But the difference is radical: the mystic chooses to let go of the story, and does so from an intact horizon; the patient with Alzheimer's loses it without choosing, from a horizon that is crumbling. The timelessness of the enlightened is an ocean; that of Alzheimer's, a shipwreck. Both are in waters without shores, but one swam there on purpose and the other fell overboard.

This raises an uncomfortable question about the conscious "now." Is the present a window that needs the frame of past and future to exist as experience? Or is the pure present, sensation without story, a legitimate, if impoverished, form of consciousness? The horizon model suggests that the pure present is still a horizon, only of minimal area: the consciousness of a newborn, of an animal without inner language. It is consciousness in its most reduced form, the hard core that holds out when everything else has gone.

## Language as the last dike

Language is the last bastion of the human horizon, and its fall is the quietest of catastrophes. When a patient loses the capacity to name, he loses not only labels, but the possibility of sharing his world. Pain that cannot say "pain" is still pain, but without a bridge to another horizon. Joy that cannot say "how good to see you" is still joy, but it bounces off the walls of a self that no longer has windows. They are two lighthouses facing each other across a foggy bay: both are still lit, but the beam of one no longer reaches the other.

The aphasias of Alzheimer's are especially cruel because they often preserve the music of speech while destroying its meaning. The patient may go on speaking with intonation, rhythm and expressive pauses, but the words are substitutions ("thingy" for "spoon," "the praying place" for "church") or outright gibberish that almost sounds like a language. It is like hearing a symphony with every note in tune, but in random order: the musical form persists, but the information has fled. The horizon keeps emitting signals, but they no longer point to anything.

When language is reduced to monosyllables and gestures, a strange enforced intimacy arises. The caregiver has to learn to read what used to be said: a gesture of the hand, a tilt of the head, the tone of a moan, everything becomes vocabulary. It is like going back to the infancy of the species, to communication before words. But this regression is a forced rupture that leaves the caregiver translating between a lost language and a silence that does not want to be silence.

## The caregiver as an external horizon

When the horizon itself can no longer sustain identity, another can do it on its behalf. The caregiver, especially if he or she has shared decades of entanglement with the patient, does not merely stand in for functions; they are not an assistant who changes diapers and hands out medicines. They are the custodian of the model the other had of himself: they keep alive, in their memory, in their language and in their affection, a version of the patient the patient can no longer maintain.

In Chapter 15 I described how the mother builds the child's horizon before it exists: she talks to it during gestation, tells it who it is, passes on a world to it before it has eyes to see it; she is, literally, the reservoir that condenses into a child's horizon. Here the reverse happens: the adult child sustains the mother's horizon when she no longer can. It is a different operation. The mother manufactured a horizon that was going to close and gain autonomy; the child cares for a horizon that is opening, losing its boundaries and receding toward the reservoir.

The caregiver who knows the patient well can anticipate his needs before he expresses them, interpret gestures incomprehensible to a stranger and keep active, through resonance rather than instructions, the correlations that still work. They know that when the patient walks back and forth three times between the kitchen and the living room it is not restlessness, but that he is looking for the bathroom and has forgotten where it is. They know that if he refuses food it is not a whim, but fear of choking, because he no longer controls his swallowing well. They know that the vacant gaze toward the window is sometimes the only way to process a world that no longer makes sense.

It is an entanglement that works in only one direction: the caregiver models the patient with increasing precision while the patient loses the capacity to model the caregiver. In physics, entanglement is always mutual; this is not. In life, it is the most unequal love there is: giving without receiving recognition, holding without being held, remembering for two.

This raises an uncomfortable question: to what extent does the patient's self go on existing in the space between the two horizons? If identity is holographic, if it is distributed in the correlations among all the parts of the horizon, when some of those correlations pass into the caregiver's horizon, does part of the self go with them? If my wife reminds me who I was, if my son tells stories of my youth, if my caregiver anticipates my fear before I feel it, where exactly is the limit of my horizon? Does it end at my skin, or does it extend to the horizon of the one who cares for me?

I have no answer. I have only the question and the certainty that those who have cared for someone like this know what I mean without needing it explained. They have felt, between exhaustion and love, that the patient lives, halfway, like a ghost, but lives, in the space they share. They have felt that their memory of the other is not just recollection, but dwelling; that the model they keep in their head is not a replica, but an extension, a territory in which the patient's self still holds citizenship.

And there is something more: the caregiver is transformed too. Caring for someone who is dissolving is a mirror that anticipates our own possible decline. Each day of care is a lesson in the anatomy of the horizon, a live demonstration of how fragile everything we take for granted is. The caregiver learns, in body and soul, that identity is a contingent loan. And that learning, though it hurts, is a form of wisdom that cannot be acquired any other way.

## The memory that doesn't know its name

Between the loss of episodic memory and the disappearance of language there is an intermediate stage that is especially disconcerting: the world is still recognizable, but can no longer be named. The patient knows what a chair is, because he sits on it, but cannot say "chair." He knows the person in front of him is important, because his body calms and his face lights up, but he cannot find the word "son" or his name.

This stage reveals something important about the architecture of knowledge: knowing how and knowing what do not live on the same floor of the building. A patient may go on playing the piano if he learned as a child, go on walking around his neighborhood or using kitchen utensils, all without being able to say his own name. The body keeps maps the mind can no longer read aloud. It is the most compelling proof that consciousness is a federation of subsystems that can fail separately.

For the model, this means that the horizon does not degrade uniformly. Some regions, the oldest, the most practiced, the most automated, hold out longer than others. The horizon is a territory with fortresses and porous borders. Procedural memory is a besieged stronghold that holds out when everything else has fallen, and its resistance tells us that the horizon has layers, strata, a geology of consciousness in which the deepest is the last to disappear.

## Death as closure

In the final stages of Alzheimer's, death stops being the enemy and becomes the closing of something that had already gone long before. Relatives who have watched someone disappear piece by piece sometimes experience the death not as a loss, but as the acknowledgment of a loss that had already happened. In those cases, grief does not begin with death: it ends with it. They have been weeping for years, fragment by fragment, and death only confirms what already was.

From the horizon model, complete evaporation is preferable to partial dissolution. A horizon that evaporates returns to the reservoir as a whole; its information is scrambled, but the process is coherent. It is not lost in pieces, but transformed all at once, and keeps some form of unity, even if only that of the process. A horizon that dissolves from the edges never finishes leaving or staying. It is an intermediate state without a name, an informational limbo with enough structure to suffer, but not enough to understand.

It is the worst of worlds: enough consciousness to suffer and not enough to understand why. In the intermediate stages, the patient can feel fear without knowing what he fears, anguish without an identifiable cause, a nameless unease. It is suffering without an object, a pain the sufferer cannot diagnose. The caregiver interprets it, names it, contains it, but the patient lives it raw, like a wounded animal that does not understand its wound.

This is not an argument for euthanasia; that decision belongs to other domains, ethical, legal, personal. It is only an observation about the geometry of suffering. There are forms of destruction of the horizon crueler than total destruction, degrees of being worse than not being. Alzheimer's forces us to consider that death, far from being the worst fate, can sometimes be the last mercy of an irreversible process.

But there is another side, and it would be false to keep quiet about it. Some caregivers describe moments of strange tenderness in the final stages: the patient who no longer knows who you are, but smiles at you with a smile that seems genuine, free of all baggage, all history, all resentment. It is like meeting someone for the first time every day and, every day, that first impression being one of trust. The reduced horizon has less room for rancor: there is no memory of offenses and no scars in the story. In its extreme impoverishment it reaches a kind of terrible innocence, a purity no saint would choose, but which exists, which can be observed and which is a way of being, even if none of us would wish for it.

## What Alzheimer's reveals

If consciousness were a substance, Alzheimer's could not happen this way. A substance is either there or not, the way alcohol makes you drunk or doesn't, or poison kills or doesn't. But the horizon is an architecture: it can lose area, connectivity and correlations and still be a horizon, smaller and smaller, poorer, more fragmented, but a horizon all the same. It is still a limit, a boundary between inside and outside, even if the inside is an unfurnished room and the outside, pure indifference.

Alzheimer's shows that consciousness does not work like a switch, but like a continuous gradient that can be traveled in both directions, and the descent is not symmetrical with the ascent. Birth is condensation: the reservoir organizes itself, takes shape, differentiates into parts that correlate. Death is evaporation: the horizon returns to the reservoir as a whole, becomes disorganized, but keeps its unity to the end. Alzheimer's is erosion: the horizon loses pieces without losing its status as a horizon, degrades by sectors, becomes a map with blank areas where there used to be inhabited territory.

And erosion teaches something condensation and evaporation do not: that the horizon is fragile not because it can be destroyed, since everything can be destroyed, but because it can be destroyed piecemeal while keeping in each piece the illusion that there is still a whole. That is the cruelty of Alzheimer's: not annihilation, but simulacrum. The patient seems to be there, and in a way he is, but that "there" is ever narrower, ever hollower, ever more incomplete. It is a horizon that deceives about its own extent, that keeps up a facade of integrity while behind it the walls are crumbling.

Alzheimer's is, in short, a lesson in humility for any theory of consciousness. It does not let us think of the mind as a simple entity, an indivisible soul or a program running on hardware. It forces us to think of it as an ecosystem, a network, a fabric that can thin out until it almost disappears without ceasing to be, for years, a way of being in the world. Each patient shows that consciousness is emergence, not essence: that it can be built slowly, over decades, and dismantled slowly over as many again; that there is no single moment when it "switches off," but a long dusk in which each sunset lasts months.

When I see an advanced patient look at a flower without knowing what it is, but smile at its color, I think of the smallest possible horizon: perception without knowledge, affection without history, presence without identity. And I think that this minimal horizon is still, in some way, a form of being conscious. It is not the consciousness any of us would choose, but it is consciousness: the proof that the phenomenon can be reduced almost to the bone and still stand.

Alzheimer's shows us the floor. It strips away the furniture, the curtains and the paintings until only the structure is left, and then it begins to remove beams. But as long as one remains, as long as a single correlation remains that relates an inside to an outside, there is a horizon, a world, however small, and someone, however diffuse, who is having an experience.

That is the ultimate limit of the model: not the question of why there is consciousness, but the realization of how much it can lose without ceasing to be. Alzheimer's is the antithesis of birth: if birth is the miracle of condensation, Alzheimer's is the tragedy of dissolution. But both teach the same thing: that the horizon is not a given, that it is a construction, a contingent work and therefore a work that can be undone. That consciousness is a loan that one day, in some way, is repaid.

> **Note to Chapter 18**
>
> **What we do know:** Alzheimer's progressively destroys neural connections before it destroys the neurons themselves. Episodic memory, tied to the hippocampus, is usually the first to degrade; procedural and musical memory hold out longer.
>
> **What we don't know:** Why some advanced patients respond to familiar stimuli without functional explicit memory. Where the exact threshold lies at which a horizon ceases to be one.
>
> **Open questions:** To what extent does the self of a patient with Alzheimer's reside in the entanglement with their caregivers? Is it possible to measure the "mass" of a contracting horizon?
>
> **If you take away only one idea:** Consciousness is not a switch that turns off, but an architecture that can lose its bricks one by one and still hold up the sky with less roof every day.
>
> **Further reading:** Jacobsen et al. (2015), "Why musical memory can be preserved in advanced Alzheimer's disease"; Sacks, O. (2007), *Musicophilia*; Kitwood, T. (1997), *Dementia Reconsidered*.
