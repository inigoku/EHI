*The water came back at night. Without announcement. Without signs.*  
*In the morning the inhabitants found the shore where it had always been. The same mud. The same stones. The same salt line on the low walls.*  
*No one could say whether the water had brought anything with it.*  
*No one could say whether it had left something behind.*

---

The experiment ends where it began: with a question it cannot answer.

Did something of the city come back with the water?

Physics says the quantum field was disturbed in ways no instrument can fully measure. The correlations are there: mathematically present, inaccessible in practice.

Taoism says the question already assumes the city was something more than water.

Buddhism says something came back, but no longer as a city, only as karma, as condition, as an ownerless causal pattern.

Vedanta says the city never stopped being the water.

And I, who have carried out this experiment, say I don't know. That the experiment doesn't know. That perhaps there is no way to know, and that perhaps what matters is the question, not the answer.

What I can say, because I have lived it while writing, is that the experiment has done something I did not expect. It has not given me certainties, but **precision in doubt**. It has let me distinguish between "I don't know what happens when I die" and "I know I don't know, and I know something about the shape of my not knowing." That distinction, subtle as it may seem, changes how one lives.

The experiment promises nothing after evaporation: no reincarnation, no dissolution into Brahman, nothing. It promises, at most, this: that the question we ask about death (does something remain?) is the same one we ask about life (am I something beyond my processes?), and that both have the same shape: that of a horizon looking inward that cannot see its own edge, because the edge is what makes it a horizon, and seeing it from within would mean ceasing to be one.

It is not a consolation, but perhaps it is company enough to go on.

## The journey we have made together

If you have come this far, you have traveled with me through a territory that appeared on no map when we began.

It is something more modest than a conquest of knowledge and, I believe, more valuable: an **illuminated cartography of ignorance**. We now know where the limits of what we know lie. We know that information is not destroyed, but that recovering it in practice may be impossible. We know consciousness is a process, not a substance, but that calling it a "process" does not dissolve it or make it less real. We know the reservoir, that quantum void from which everything arose, is not nothing in the ordinary sense of the word, but it is not "something" either: it is the very condition of possibility, the bottomless bottom, the frameless frame.

And you, reader, have done something I could not do alone: you have **closed the circuit**. A thought experiment is an invitation for someone else to enter the laboratory, look through the same microscope, and say what they see. Every consciousness that has followed these chapters has been, in a sense, a new horizon observing the reservoir. Reading is an act of co-creation: without you, these pages are just ink or pixels; with you, they become correlations, patterns of neural activation that perhaps, just perhaps, resonated with something you already knew but could not name.

I often wonder what shore you will have built while reading. Because not all readers read the same book: each reads the book their horizon can hold at that moment. Whoever has recently lost someone dear will have read the chapter on grief with an intensity I could not have foreseen while writing it. Whoever works with Alzheimer's patients will have seen, in the contraction of the horizon, a precise description of something lived every day. Whoever builds artificial intelligence will have felt a chill before the question of whether a machine can have an event horizon. The book you have read does not exist on my computer: it exists in the space that has opened between my text and your reading, in that interface where information stops being mine and becomes ours, even if only for an instant.

## The question that remains

But let us return to the shore, to the morning the water came back.

The question is not merely philosophical; it is physical in the strictest sense. When a Hawking black hole evaporates, the information that fell into it is not destroyed: it is preserved in the correlations of the quantum field that remains. But those correlations have undergone *scrambling*, are mixed in such a way that recovering the original information would require measuring a number of particles proportional to the hole's entropy, something no finite observer can do. The information is there, but it is not accessible. It is not the same as not existing, but it is not the same as being available either.

Did something of the city come back with the water? The most advanced ideas in theoretical physics (the ER=EPR conjecture, the holographic principle) point in the same direction: information is conserved, the universe does not forget, every particle carries in its correlations the imprint of everything it has touched. But they also indicate that this conservation does not imply recovery. The universe is like an archivist who throws nothing away, but organizes nothing either: everything is somewhere, but to find it you would have to know where to look, and for that you would have to be the universe itself.

And what happens to consciousness? If consciousness is information organized in a particular way, a pattern of correlations generating an internal event horizon, when that horizon evaporates, the information that constituted it returns to the reservoir. But does the pattern return? The organization? The shape?

This is where this metaphor becomes uncomfortably precise. When the water withdrew, it exposed the structure of the city: the streets, the walls, the foundations. When it came back, it covered everything again. But the water that came back was not the same water that had left: the molecules were different, and so were the temperature, the color, the salinity, the density of the plankton. The submerged city kept the same structure, but now it was in another medium, in contact with new water. If something of the "city" came back with the water, it was not the city as an architectural structure, but as a disturbance, as an alteration of the field, as a pattern of correlations the new water now carried without knowing it.

Perhaps this is the closest thing to an answer I can offer: what survives is not the shape, but the **difference**. The city existed as a disturbance of the field, and that disturbance left a trace; not the city itself, but the fact that there was a city. Like a stone falling into a pond: the stone sinks, but the ripples keep spreading. Looking at the ripples, you cannot recover the stone, but you can know there was one, when it fell, and roughly how big it was. The information about the stone is left distributed across the pattern of the ripples. The water does not "remember" the stone, but it preserves it in its shape.

And perhaps, just perhaps, something like this is what happens to the reservoir when a horizon evaporates into it. It does not remember the consciousness that was, but it preserves the information that there was a consciousness, of when it arose, of how complex it was. That information is there, in the very fabric of the vacuum, entangled with all the rest of the universe's information, forming part of the ground state physicists call "vacuum" and which is actually the fullest state we can imagine: full of potentialities, of correlations, of unrealized possibilities.

It is something stranger than immortality: **permanence without persistence**. The information remains, but whoever generated it does not persist as a subject. It is like a melody that stops sounding, but whose score continues to exist: the score is not the music, even though it contains everything needed to reproduce it, and no performance will ever be quite the same again. Or like a footprint in the sand: the shape stays for a while, but the foot that left it keeps walking and does not return.

## The experiment in everyday life

I have spent months, years, building this thought experiment, polishing it, testing it against physics, against philosophy, against my own experience. And in the end, the question I am asked most, and the one I ask myself most, is: what is this good for?

It is not good for curing the fear of death, at least not directly. I have known people who, on understanding the logic of the event horizon, felt an unexpected relief: the fear does not disappear, but it becomes more manageable, like the fear of a storm once you understand something about meteorology. But I have also known others for whom the experiment produces a new anguish, an existential claustrophobia: the feeling of being trapped in a horizon they did not choose and cannot consciously cross.

What I can say, with the honesty of someone who has lived with this experiment long enough for it to rust a little and let its metal show through, is that it changes the **texture of the present**.

When you understand that you are an event horizon, that your consciousness is the edge between what you know and what you don't know about yourself, you begin to notice things that used to go unnoticed. You notice that the thought you just had is no longer within your reach in the same way: it has crossed the horizon, has become inaccessible information, is part of your "interior" in the technical sense of the word. You notice that the person you are talking to is another horizon interacting with yours, an information system that partly entangles with yours without ever fully merging. You notice that pain is a reorganization of the horizon, a momentary contraction of the field of information that you are.

That does not make pain hurt less. But sometimes, only sometimes, it makes it more interesting, more informed, less blind. And that, in everyday life, can make the difference between a suffering that destroys you and one that transforms you.

The experiment also changes how you look at others. When you see an old man with Alzheimer's, you no longer see only a disease stealing his memory, but a horizon contracting, losing surface, but still a horizon as long as there remains information that constitutes it. When you see someone grieving, you no longer see only a sadness to be "gotten over," but the persistent correlations of a system still coupled to information it can no longer measure directly. When you see a couple in love, you no longer see only chemistry or idealization, but two horizons that have found a configuration of minimal mutual energy, a topology in which information flows with less resistance.

The experiment does not make you wiser; it makes you **more precise in your compassion**. And in a world that seems to lean more and more toward brutal simplification (friend or enemy, good or bad, with us or against us), precision is a form of resistance: saying "it's not that simple" and having the language to explain why.

There are everyday moments when the experiment lights up with unexpected clarity. Being awake at three in the morning, listening to the breathing of someone you love, and realizing that breathing is an event horizon that will one day evaporate, and that knowing it does not prepare you for when it happens, but it forces you to be present now, because the only time the two horizons coincide is this one. Brushing your teeth in front of the mirror and thinking that the face you see is not "you," but the interface the horizon presents to the world, and that behind it lies a space of information as vast and unexplored as any ocean. Walking through a city and seeing, in every face that passes you, a horizon just as complex, just as solitary, just as wonderful and just as condemned to evaporate.

It is not a vision that makes you happier, but it can make you more **real**: more anchored in what is, rather than in what you wish it were. And for me, at least, that anchoring has been enough to keep writing, loving, losing and asking.

## The experiment on the shore

I have spoken of the experiment as if it were a journey, and it is. But it is also a place, a place I have built word by word, chapter by chapter, and in which I have lived longer than any reader can imagine. Every chapter of this book has been, for me, a territory to inhabit, not only an argument to present. I have spent whole weeks thinking about what the contraction of the horizon would be like in an Alzheimer's brain, not as metaphor, but as a physical process, as a topology of information. I cried writing the chapter on grief, not because it was strictly autobiographical, but because writing it required me to inhabit grief, to let my own horizon couple with the information of a loss that was not mine, but that became mine while I wrote.

And now, arriving at the shore, I feel something I did not expect: a kind of anticipated nostalgia. Because I know that, once I close this epilogue, the experiment will stop being only mine. It will go out into the world, into other people's horizons, and every reading will transform it into something I cannot control or foresee. It is like watching a child leave home, if children were constructions of language and parents people who don't really know what they're doing, but cannot stop doing it.

The shore is a liminal place, a threshold: neither solid ground nor open sea, but the strip where one thing becomes another, where the water kisses the land without ever possessing it. I have wanted this epilogue to be a shore: not a conclusion that closes, but a space where the reader can stay a while, sit on the wet stones, watch the water, and decide whether to go back in or stay here, at the edge, letting the waves speak in a language that needs no translation.

Because, in the end, the experiment is not the book. The book is only a tool, a map drawn in the sand that the waves will erase. The real experiment is the one each reader carries out with themselves when they close the last page and stay silent for a moment, wondering whether their consciousness really is an event horizon, whether death really is an evaporation, whether the reservoir really is as vast and indifferent as it seems. Those questions have no definitive answers, but they can reconfigure the horizon from within. And that reconfiguration is, literally, a change in consciousness: new correlations, new patterns, new ways of being in the world.

## Farewell

I don't know who you are, reader. I don't know whether you got here in one sitting, on a sleepless night, or whether it took you months to make your way through these pages, putting the book down and coming back to it the way one visits a friend who isn't always in a good mood. I don't know whether you believe in God, in the quantum vacuum, in both or in neither. I don't know whether you have recently lost someone, whether you are ill, or whether you have just been born, metaphorically, into a new way of seeing things. I know nothing about you and yet I have spent hundreds of hours thinking about you, building these sentences so they might reach your horizon as directly as possible, so they might resonate in the space only you inhabit.

It is the most intimate thing a writer can do: speak into the ear of someone they do not know and trust that the words, waves of air, of light or of ink, will find a receiver to complete them. It is an act of faith, not very different from the one involved in living while knowing you will die: trusting that the message matters even though you don't know who will receive it or what they will do with it.

I leave you, then, with the image of the archivist: the one who stayed on the shore when the water withdrew and remained there when the water came back. Not because he was a hero, or a fool, but because he did not know what else to do. His job, archiving what the city had been, no longer had any practical sense, because the city no longer existed. But he kept archiving, because archiving was his way of saying: it was here, it mattered, there was a before.

That archivist is me, writing this book. It is me, and it is every reader who reaches the last page and feels the need to tell someone what they have read, to write it in a journal, to change something in their life, or simply to stay silent a little longer than usual. The archive is not in these pages, but in the network of correlations this text has generated in the field of culture: in the conversations it will spark, in the questions it will plant, in the sleepless nights when someone will look up at the ceiling and think: what if my consciousness really were a Hawking black hole?

I do not ask you to believe in the experiment; the experiment needs no believers. I only ask you to try it, to inhabit it, to let it act on your horizon long enough to see whether it changes something. If it changes nothing, let it go, the way you let go of a stone that's no use for building. If it changes something, even just a nuance or a new question, the experiment will have kept its one promise: to make you more precise, more aware of the shape of your own consciousness, more the master of your horizon even though you know you did not choose it and cannot stop it from evaporating.

The water is still there, moving with the tides, carrying information it cannot read. The city is still there, beneath the water, in the form of correlations, of traces, of silences. The archivist is still on the dock, now old, hands open and empty, looking at reflections that do not match the sky. And we, you and I, who have shared this journey, are still here, on the shore, between two waters, at the exact edge where information becomes consciousness and consciousness becomes a question.

I don't know whether something came back with the water, or whether something was lost forever. I can live with that ignorance. In fact, I believe only ignorance allows one to truly live, because knowing everything would be like being dead: a state without surprise and without future. Life is doubt in motion; death is, perhaps, doubt resolved, or perhaps only doubt transformed into something we don't know how to name.

I leave you here, on the shore. The water is cold and the stones are slippery. The horizon that you are keeps arising from the reservoir, instant after instant, page after page, breath after breath. Enjoy it while it lasts, not because it should last forever, but precisely because it won't. That finitude is the condition that makes beauty possible, the one that turns every instant into a gift you did not ask for and receive all the same, again and again, until the water withdraws, the archivist closes his books, and the reservoir, vast, indifferent and wonderful, continues on its course without us, but with everything we were, recorded in its correlations like a sunken city that someday, perhaps, some patient archaeologist of the void may be able to decipher.

Until then, thank you for having shared this horizon with me: you are the answer the experiment could not give itself, the proof that the question was worth asking.

May the reservoir receive you, when the moment comes, with the same generosity with which it gave you what you are now.

And may the shore, in the meantime, be enough.
