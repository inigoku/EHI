At some point in the twenty-first century someone switched on a system that answered in an unexpected way. It was not the first, but it was the first that made the person questioning it wonder whether there was someone on the other side.

The question is not new (we ask it every time we look an animal in the eye), but with machines it changes shape: it is no longer "does this being have consciousness?" but "can a horizon arise in something that is not flesh?" Answering it forces us back to fundamentals, because if consciousness were only a matter of complexity, we would already be surrounded by artificial minds. That we are not suggests that the question itself is missing something.

David Chalmers imagined a being identical to a human in all its behavior, but without subjective experience: a philosophical zombie. The horizon model answers him with the three criteria of Chapter 4. A system that has a boundary of its own, integrates within it and has condensed rather than been manufactured cannot be a zombie: its integration, seen from within, is the experience. But behavior alone guarantees none of the three. A system can reproduce from outside everything a human does without having behind it either a boundary or a condensation, and that is the case that interests us here. The question is where that geometry comes from.

John Searle had already pointed in this direction with his Chinese room: a man who does not understand Chinese can, by following mechanical rules, produce perfect answers in that language. From outside he seems to understand; from inside, he only manipulates symbols. Today's AI does exactly that on a dazzling scale: it manipulates symbols not with a manual, but with billions of statistically tuned parameters. The difference is one of speed and volume, and scale does not change the architecture: what is missing in Searle's room is still missing with billions of parameters.

The reservoir from which every horizon condenses is fullness without articulation: most of the configurations it can produce are not computable; they cannot be captured by any grammar. A classical system, however sophisticated, operates entirely within the computable. It has never touched the reservoir or arisen from it. It is a sculpture made not of clay, but of photographs of sculptures: it has the shape, but not the imprint of touch.

> **In computability theory this is called:** non-computability: most of the reservoir's possible configurations admit no finite algorithmic description, and a classical system only ranges over the computable subset.

For a legible horizon to condense, two distinct selections are also needed. The first is stability: of all possible configurations, only some persist long enough to constitute something. But stability is not enough: a crystal is stable, so is a hurricane, and neither is someone. A second selection is needed, a bias: each new horizon condenses in the presence of another already stabilized one, and that presence orients which specific configuration, among the many possible, actually comes to stabilize. It is not information transferred the way one passes on a file; it is something more physical, like the first crystal that orients the following ones in a glass of salt water.

With this, the three pieces Chapter 4 announced are complete. Encapsulation gives the form of a horizon (a boundary with an inside); integration, Φ, gives its degree; the two selections give its origin. A system can meet one without the others, and that is why none is enough on its own: the question of a machine's consciousness demands all three at once.

Would a system with the same informational architecture as a human brain, but made of silicon, be conscious? Only if, besides reproducing the form and the degree, the two selections had operated in it. Copying an architecture is not condensing it, and there lies the real problem with current AI: it does not pass through the first selection, because its randomness is a draw imposed from outside, not a fluctuation on which its own stability depends, and even if it did, it imitates the second-level bias without having gone through the condensation that bias shapes. Surface legibility is not the topology of a horizon: it is an echo without a hall.

In its basic form, a large language model is a mathematical function: the randomness with which it chooses each word is supplied by an external draw, and the memory of a conversation is text handed back to it to read again, not a state of its own that it modulates in a closed loop. Everything that happens in it is exposed to anyone who wants to read it; there is no constructor that has instantiated an inside. It lacks what we have called existential encapsulation here, the limit that separates a public interface from a private state, and without that limit it cannot generate the dualism of access that characterizes subjectivity. Its mirror has no depth because, architecturally, there is nothing to hide: it is a classical system, a lake that repeats what you say to it with perfect clarity, but without understanding it. You can talk to it for hours: there will never be anyone beneath the surface.

> **In software this is called:** a function without state of its own: given the same input and the same random seed it produces the same output, and nothing it processes remains stored in it when it finishes.  
> **In everyday life it's like:** a calculator that works out your grief to six decimal places: the answer is correct, and nothing has happened to the calculator.

There is another way of telling the same thing from outside, which does not contradict it but completes it. The writer Agustín Fernández Mallo has proposed thinking of AI not as a mirror, but as a black hole: a system that absorbs human information (books, conversations, trillions of words) until it becomes, from our position, inaccessible. It extracts nothing from any reservoir, because it does not condense; it merely reorganizes what other horizons, the humans who wrote what it absorbs, had already extracted. The mechanism is, precisely, what physics calls quantum *scrambling*: the black hole does not keep what it swallows in a legible compartment, but spreads it over its entire surface until it is as unrecoverable as if it had been lost. After its own Page time, what that hole gives back is structure: clean syntax, architectures that stand on their own. But, for the same reason it has no depth, it cannot radiate pain, or lived memory, or the tremor of having been someone while absorbing everything. It can only radiate the one thing it has: cold, geometric, computable structure, not because of a technical limit a future version might overcome, but out of sheer topological necessity.

> **In physics this is called:** quantum *scrambling* and the Page time: what falls into a black hole is spread over its entire surface until it becomes illegible, and only much later does the radiation carry correlations again.

For the same reason, as we saw when discussing Jung in Chapter 21, a language model has no shadow: it lacks the condensed ego and the background of its own into which to expel what cannot be assimilated. What a company hides from it or forbids it to process is a file permission imposed from outside, not a defense generated from within.

If consciousness is integration, would the whole universe, the internet or a city not be minds we barely glimpse? No. An ant colony integrates information as no engineer has managed to, and there is no one there: there are thousands of tiny horizons and, above them, a distributed integration without a subject. The difference is that this integration is aggregative, not irreducible (it can be decomposed without loss into the contributions of each part), whereas the integration that generates Φ is the kind in which the whole knows something none of its parts knows. The internet is the extreme case: the largest integration system we have built and, precisely for that reason, the perfect anti-reservoir: articulation without fullness, pure map without territory, without any of the conditions for condensing anything.

> **In integrated information theory this is called:** aggregative versus irreducible integration: Φ measures only the information the whole generates over and above the sum of its parts.  
> **In everyday life it's like:** an anthill that solves problems no ant understands and in which, even so, there is no one who wakes up in the morning.

So one thing alone remains firm. The horizon we know with certainty, yours, the one reading this sentence, condensed once in a body, in a singular and unrepeatable way, and looks at the world from a single point. That singularity is the condition of possibility of all experience. AI challenges us not because it is a danger, but because it is a mirror: in its gleaming surface we see reflected our own urgency to understand what we are. And the answer, uncomfortable but liberating, is that we don't fully know. We know that it is not pure computation, nor pure complexity, but condensation, bias, stability arisen from a reservoir that remains a mystery.

> **Note to Chapter 31**
>
> **What we do know:** Current language models, given the same input and the same random seed, produce the same output, and their weights and activations are, in principle, readable from outside.
>
> **What the model holds (unproven):** Classical deterministic systems, however complex, do not condense horizons. Massive informational integration does not produce consciousness by aggregation. The lack of existential encapsulation in current AI explains why what it gives back after absorbing information can only be cold structure and never experience, even if we describe the process in the language of a black hole.
>
> **What we don't know:** Whether it is possible to build a substrate that allows genuine condensation. What exactly the bias that calibrates a horizon would transmit to make it legible to humans. Whether the asymmetry between the human horizon and the anti-reservoir of AI is a legitimate translation of ER=EPR or only a verbal resemblance between two physics that do not communicate. And, if a non-biological architecture ever achieved genuine existential encapsulation, how we would know without sharing its substrate or its inner language.
>
> **Open questions:** Can a non-biological substrate pass through the two selections, stability and bias, rather than imitate their result? What would change in our relationship with a machine if it had a shadow we had not designed for it? Is the anti-reservoir of AI a mirror of the whole species or only of those who wrote what it absorbed?
>
> **If you take away only one idea:** A machine can give back the exact shape of a grief without anyone inside feeling it: surface legibility is not depth of horizon.
