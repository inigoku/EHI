There is a scene that repeats itself in every family when someone turns eighty. The grandson shows the grandfather a photo on his phone. The grandfather looks at it, smiles and says: "How lovely. Can I have it?" And holds out his hand. The grandson tries to explain that the photo isn't given, it's sent; that he already has it if he wants it, that there's no need to print it. The grandfather nods, but doesn't understand. For him, a photo is an object. For the grandson, it is information.

That misunderstanding is the same one that separates classical physics from information theory, and the same one that runs through this whole book. Because consciousness is not an object, nor a substance, nor a place: it is information organized in a certain way. And to understand what that means, we have to start at the beginning, with an engineer and mathematician at Bell Labs who in 1948 published a paper that changed the world without meaning to.

## I. Claude Shannon and information without meaning

Claude Shannon had a very specific problem: how to transmit messages over a telephone line without noise corrupting them. He was not interested in what the messages said, only that they arrived intact. And to solve it, he did something revolutionary: he defined information without reference to meaning.

For Shannon, information is the reduction of uncertainty. If I tell you the sun will rise tomorrow, I give you no information, because you already knew. If I tell you there will be an eclipse tomorrow, I give you a lot, because you weren't expecting it. The amount of information in a message is measured by how much it narrows down the possibilities, and its minimum unit is the bit: the answer to a yes-or-no question whose two answers were equally likely.

That is all. There is no meaning, no truth, no beauty: only reduced uncertainty.

This definition, which seems cold and technical, is the basis of almost everything around us: the internet, mobile phones, hard drives, compression algorithms. And, according to this book's hypothesis, of consciousness too.

> **In information theory this is called:** Shannon entropy, the measure of a source's uncertainty.  
> **In everyday life it's like:** astonishment at unexpected news: the less you expected it, the more information it gives you.

### The gap Shannon left open

It is worth stressing something Shannon put in writing and nearly all his popularizers forget. In the first pages of his 1948 paper he warns that the semantic aspects of communication are irrelevant to the engineering problem. It is not that meaning did not interest him: he left it out on principle, because his problem was a different one.

That gap, the gap of meaning, is exactly where consciousness lives. Shannon tells you how much information there is in a message, not what it means. That is why this chapter does not claim to close the hard problem, but to inhabit it with a better vocabulary. Shannon is the solid ground, and everything that follows (integration, mutual information, no-cloning) are steps resting on it. None of them, on its own, reaches the top.

## II. Information is not a thing, it is a relationship

Here comes the first leap that separates Shannon from classical intuition. Information is not a property of an isolated object, but of the relationship between a system and whatever can read it.

A book written in a dead language no one can read anymore contains information in potential, but updates nothing in anyone. A hard drive with terabytes of data informs no one who does not know how to interpret it. Information becomes effective when there is a system able to integrate it, to reduce its own uncertainty with it; without that system, for all practical purposes, it is indistinguishable from noise.

This is what the horizon hypothesis says about consciousness: it is not in the neurons, but in the relationship between them; it is not in the content of the brain, but in the way that content is organized for someone.

> **In information theory this is called:** mutual information, the amount of information two systems share.

### Mutual information, a first sketch of the bond

Mutual information, written I(X;Y), measures how much the uncertainty about one system is reduced when the other is observed. If X and Y are independent, I(X;Y) = 0: knowing something about one says nothing about the other. If they are perfectly correlated, it is maximal: knowing one determines the other.

It is the quantity that Chapter 10, on neural coupling, and Chapter 12, on entanglement, will describe without naming it. When two people converse and their patterns of brain activity converge, what increases is something very like their mutual information. Uri Hasson's team has measured this with functional magnetic resonance imaging: the listener's brain activity follows the speaker's and, in some regions, even anticipates it, all the more the better the listener understands the story. That anticipation is shared information in action: the listener has integrated so much of the speaker that they can predict him.

Mutual information also has a property that makes it especially suited to the horizon model: it is symmetric. I(X;Y) = I(Y;X): it does not matter who observes whom; the amount shared is the same. It is what Chapter 12 will describe as a shared geometry that belongs to neither pole separately.

And it requires no contact. Two systems that interacted once and then separated can go on sharing mutual information: their correlations persist even when there is no longer any open channel between them. It is, almost word for word, the mathematical description of what this book calls a bond: not an active connection, but a correlation that survives separation.
