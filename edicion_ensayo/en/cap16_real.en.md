So far we have talked about individual horizons: how they are born, how they become entangled, how they break. But there remains a question that, once asked, cannot be postponed.

What happens when the asymmetry is not circumstantial, as between mother and child or in grief, but structural? When a system prevents two horizons from meeting on symmetrical terms, not for a day, but for generations?

A clarification before going on. The horizon model does not oblige us to talk about politics, but it allows it. What follows is a decision to extend the experiment into softer terrain. Readers who prefer to keep consciousness and politics apart can skip this chapter without losing the thread.

Those who decide to stay will find something they had, in a way, already sensed. Injustice is, besides a poor distribution of resources, a disturbance of the very geometry of encounter: the impossibility of two consciousnesses resonating on an equal footing because one of them is surrounded by walls the other does not see.

The child who grows up in a tower of brick and glass and the one who grows up in a windowless room do not just have different goods, but different universes of signal. Their horizons are formed from incommensurable materials. And when, decades later, they meet (in a courtroom, at a job interview, in the supermarket line), the original asymmetry keeps acting like an invisible force that decides who can speak and who will be heard.

## Power as a restriction of coupling

Power is not brute force. From the geometry of the horizon, it is the capacity to modify another's rate of integration without that other being able to return the gesture in equal measure.

Under conditions of symmetry, the arrow goes both ways: each horizon can resonate, couple and uncouple. When there is power, the arrow is one-way: one horizon decides what information reaches the other, at what speed and with what urgency. It can saturate it with noise or deprive it of signal.

The other cannot respond, not because it doesn't want to, but because the architecture of the relationship prevents it.

A prison, a relationship of domestic violence or a childhood of institutional neglect share that geometry. The subjugated horizon is prevented from responding: its return signal is attenuated, intercepted or punished. Whoever wields power receives the other's signal as information to be processed or discarded, without their own architecture being exposed.

But asymmetry does not always come in uniform and with a baton. Sometimes it wears a tie and presents itself as efficiency. The precarious worker who cannot negotiate his schedule, the nurse who has no time to listen to the patient, the student who passes by memorizing without questioning anything: all live under forms of power that go by other names, but share the same topology. The signal rises, is diluted and gets lost in bureaucratic networks designed so that the response never arrives.

Imagine a company where the boss decides a team's strategy without consulting it. The employees can mutter, suggest, even protest, but the architecture of the relationship guarantees that their signal does not weigh as much as that of the person who signs the contracts. The boss need not be wicked: the system is built so that his horizon absorbs the other's information without being obliged to integrate it as his own. The pain of the person laid off does not pass through the wall of his office; the precariousness of the temporary contract does not disturb the stability of the permanent contract of the one who decides.

When coupling is one-way for years, something deeper than a one-off injustice happens. The subjugated horizon learns to modulate its own signal: to speak more quietly, to anticipate rejection, to translate its experience into the vocabulary of the powerful before expressing it. This is what is sometimes called internalized oppression: the asymmetry is internalized to the point where the oppressed collaborate in their own silencing, not out of cowardice, but because their predictive model has learned that the authentic signal will not be integrated.

## Poverty as deprivation of signal

Subjective time, the density of integration, depends on the richness and variety of the stimuli the horizon receives. Something direct follows: for an impoverished horizon, the environment offers less integrable information per unit of time.

The child who grows up among books, adult conversation, musical instruments and trips to the countryside receives high-density stimuli. The child who grows up in multiple deprivation (cramped space, limited vocabulary, few new stimuli, chronic stress that is noise rather than novelty) sees his horizon expand less than his biology would allow. His brain has the same capacity; it is the environment that gives it no material to integrate.

The difference is topological. The poor child's horizon has a different architecture: fewer long-range connections, a reward system calibrated for urgency, a chronic permeability to threat.

Two twins separated at birth, one raised in a deprived neighborhood and the other in a well-off one, will not just accumulate different knowledge: they will build different *ways of being conscious*. The first will learn to detect danger before it shows itself, to save energy, not to trust continuity. The second will learn to defer rewards, to explore, to take for granted that the world will cooperate with his plans. Both are rational adaptations to their environment, and neither is superior in the abstract. But the world is designed by and for the second, so the first's adaptation becomes, in that context, a "deficit."

The notion of "deprivation of signal" makes it possible to separate two things that are usually confused: material poverty and informational poverty. A child can have food and a roof, material deprivation solved, and grow up in an environment where no one speaks to him in complex ways, where television is his only varied stimulus, where fear of eviction or domestic violence consumes all the available attention. In that case, the horizon is still impoverished, not for lack of calories, but for lack of the kind of stimuli consciousness needs to gain complexity.

This is not determinism. Plasticity exists, but it has a cost. Getting out of poverty requires reconfiguring an architecture built over years under adverse conditions.

And here the horizon model sheds particularly clear light on something: the cost of that reconfiguration is also informational. Whoever escapes poverty has to learn to process signals they never had to integrate, to inhibit responses that used to be adaptive, to trust stabilities their predictive model does not anticipate. It is like learning a language at thirty: possible, but costly, and never with the fluency of someone who learned it as a child.

## Racism as a denial of legibility

Racism is not only prejudice. It is also a systematic failure of legibility: the inability or refusal of one horizon to model another with the precision its complexity demands.

When a judicial system attributes hostility to gestures it would read as neutral in another context, or when an employer cannot interpret the signals of a candidate of another ethnicity, there is more than ill will: there is a predictive model degraded by the bias of its training environment.

The brain models others with a precision that varies with familiarity. If the social environment is segregated, if the media overrepresent certain groups as a threat, if real interactions are scarce or loaded with asymmetries of power, the predictive model degrades. The horizon is not bad: the corpus it was trained on is biased.

A precise consequence follows: racism is, in part, a collective informational failure, and like every collective failure it is not corrected by individual goodwill alone, but by modifying the training environment: real, sustained interactions, under conditions symmetrical enough for predictive models to recalibrate.

The literature on intergroup contact has already shown this empirically. What the horizon model adds is an explanation of the mechanism: repeated contact under symmetrical conditions rebuilds legibility because it forces the model to correct its bias with new data. Without symmetry of coupling, contact recalibrates nothing, and can even reinforce the bias.

Think of the security guard who follows a young Black man through the aisles of a shopping mall. His predictive model, trained on hundreds of media portrayals, assigns a high probability to hostility, and the real signals (the way of walking, the tone of voice, the gaze) are read through that filter. A neutral gesture becomes suspicious; an irritated response to being watched, confirmation of danger. The loop closes: the model generates the behavior it predicts, because the prediction itself modifies the behavior of the person being observed. It is what happens to someone who believes their partner is cheating on them and, by spying on them, creates the distance they then interpret as proof.

The denial of legibility has effects that go beyond the single interaction. Whoever is systematically mismodeled learns to model themselves with the categories of the one who looks at them. The young man whom society reads as a threat may internalize that reading, not because it is true, but because it is the only version of himself that others give back to him integrated. The other's horizon works, in a way, like a mirror: if every mirror gives you back a distorted image, you end up believing that your true face is the distortion.

## Democracy as a design of coupling

If power is sustained asymmetry, justice is the aspiration that no horizon be systematically prevented from resonating. Politics, then, is not exhausted by the management of resources: it is the design of the geometry of collective coupling.

A functioning democracy guarantees two things. The first, channels for upward signal. The vote, a free press and protest are technologies of signal. They do not guarantee that the signal will be heard, but they are a necessary condition for it to be heard.

The second, the capacity to respond: mechanisms of accountability, periodic elections, public scrutiny. This distinguishes democratic asymmetry from authoritarian asymmetry: in a democracy, the subordinate horizon can modify, slowly, partially and in aggregate, the architecture of the horizon that wields power.

We should not exaggerate. A society is not a horizon: it was not born and did not cross any threshold of emergence. It lacks the integrated unity that defines a horizon and, therefore, a consciousness of its own. Attributing a collective Φ to a society would be confusing the measure of integration with the existence of a subject.

What can be said, modestly, is that democracy tends to maximize the flow of information among the horizons of a network. Democracies tend to be more stable and innovative not because their citizens are better, but because their geometry lets more signal circulate from more nodes.

When the channels for upward signal atrophy (abstention, disinformation, media concentration) or the capacity to respond is corrupted, the geometry degenerates into a de facto oligarchy. The system is still called a democracy, but its topology is that of a soft dictatorship.

Here the horizon model offers a useful distinction: not every asymmetry is tyrannical. A doctor knows more than her patient, and a judge more than a litigant, but those asymmetries are functional, not structural. The doctor has to respond to the patient's pain; the judge, to give public reasons. There is a return channel, even if unequal, that prevents the asymmetry from crystallizing into domination. Democracy does not demand perfect symmetry, which is impossible in any complex organization, but that no asymmetry be shielded from the return signal.

Today's problem may not be the classic dictatorship, but diffuse asymmetry: algorithms that decide what news we see, platforms that monetize attention without being accountable, companies operating in jurisdictions where no one can ask them for explanations. The citizen faces not an identifiable oppressor, but a network of asymmetries so entangled that the signal of protest dissipates before reaching a destination where it could be integrated.

That diffuse asymmetry now has a concrete case study in artificial intelligence. Coeckelbergh has analyzed how algorithmic systems amplify discrimination and erode democratic quality without the citizen having a channel of upward signal equivalent to the vote or a free press. Hao has documented, through investigative journalism, that this power is not distributed but concentrated in a handful of horizons (companies, not people) able to impose their geometry of coupling on the rest of the planet, sustained by poorly paid labor in the Global South and by chips, water and energy that rarely enter the public debate on AI. An algorithm needs no inner life to wield power in the sense of this chapter: it is enough for it to modulate the signal of millions of horizons without any of them being able to modify its own in return.

## Collective trauma as shared geometry

Individual trauma is encapsulated information that deforms the geometry of the horizon. Collective trauma (wars, genocides, slavery) works analogously, but on the scale of entire populations.

When a society has lived through something that overwhelmed the integrating capacity of all its horizons at once, what remains is not just a set of traumatized individuals, but a field of damaged correlations that persists for generations.

The children of survivors and the grandchildren of the enslaved do not inherit the trauma biologically (although epigenetics points to possible mechanisms), but they do inherit the environment of coupling in which they grow up. Silences, overprotection, hypervigilance: all this configures the architecture of the new horizons before they can question it.

Imagine a community that lived through a dictatorship. The adults who survived it learned not to trust, not to name, to read between the lines. That caution, rational at the time, is passed on to the children not as doctrine, but as atmosphere. The child grows up in a house where joy is always a little guilty, where trust in institutions is a naivety that costs dearly, and where the father's silence at dinner says more than a thousand warnings. He did not have to live through the repression to inherit its geometry: it was enough to grow up in the gravitational field that repression left behind.

Truth commissions, memory museums and public rites of reparation are operations of collective de-encapsulation: attempts to make information that was trapped circulate, so that it can be integrated instead of going on deforming things from underground.

The difficulty is that opening the capsule has a cost and a risk: it can flood the system with more information than it can integrate at once. That is why processes of transitional justice are so fragile and fail so often. The model offers no recipes; it offers a way of understanding why they fail.

Some collective traumas are born not of a single event, but of a prolonged condition. Colonization was centuries of sustained asymmetry that reconfigured the horizons of colonizers and colonized so deeply that it persists after the flags have changed. The enslaved person lost not only freedom: he lost the possibility that the system around him would integrate his signal. And that denial, repeated over generations, built architectures of consciousness that legal abolition cannot dissolve overnight.

> **In physics this is called:** hysteresis: a system keeps the imprint of its history even when external conditions return to their original values.  
> **In everyday life it's like:** a wooden door that swelled with the damp and still sticks even after the weather has changed.

## Care as repair of coupling

Chapter 18 spoke of the caregiver, the one who sustains another's horizon when it contracts. Here we can broaden that intuition. If injustice is a sustained asymmetry of coupling, care is the practice of restoring symmetry, even if only locally and temporarily.

The caregiver does not correct the structure: they cannot abolish poverty, end racism or heal collective trauma. But they can create, in the space of the relationship, a different geometry: making their horizon available to receive the other's signal with the same intensity with which it emits its own.

This is what a good teacher does in an impoverished neighborhood: not merely transmitting content, but offering a horizon that resonates with the child's and gives him back his own signal faithfully enough for him to integrate it. It is what a community therapist, an intercultural mediator, a social worker who listens before classifying all do. These are figures who do not solve structural injustice, but who repair, relationship by relationship, the possibility of coupling.

The horizon model allows us to say something political rhetoric sometimes forgets: justice is not only a distribution of goods, but also of attention; the possibility that your signal reaches another horizon and is integrated, instead of being processed as noise. In this sense, the caregiver is the prototype of what a politics of the horizon might aspire to: not equality of outcomes, but equality of resonance.

## What this chapter is not

For the most part, this chapter discovers nothing: it changes the framing. That poverty damages development, that intergroup contact on an equal footing reduces prejudice, that collective trauma is transmitted through the environment: all this was already known to developmental psychology, social psychology and memory studies. What the model offers is a common vocabulary, a way of seeing power, poverty, racism and trauma as variants of a single phenomenon, the geometry of coupling, rather than as separate problems.

On a few points it risks something more: the distinction between material deprivation and deprivation of integrable signal, and the prediction that it is symmetry of coupling that allows intergroup contact to recalibrate models. If those claims are false, the model is superfluous here; if they are true, it contributes something.

There are no experiments that measure the flow of integration in a society, nor studies relating asymmetries of coupling to inequality. What there is, is a way of ordering questions that until now have been posed in moral or economic vocabulary and that might benefit from an informational one.

The wounded horizon is not only the individual with a trauma or an addiction. It is also the community that could not process its history, the class prevented from emitting its signal, the group that was not modeled with the precision it deserved. The experiment does not heal those wounds; at most, it names them differently. And naming, though not enough, is sometimes where something begins.

Naming allows us to see patterns where before there was only moral chaos. It allows us to ask, for example, whether a public policy should not redistribute, besides income, signal: whether children from impoverished environments do not need, besides more food, more conversation, more rhythms, more spaces in which their horizons can resonate with others that are not in survival mode. It allows us to ask whether racial justice does not consist, besides punishing discrimination, in redesigning the training environments in which predictive models are formed. And whether repairing a collective trauma is not, besides remembering, creating the conditions for trapped information to circulate without destroying the system that receives it.

These are questions without easy answers. The horizon model does not resolve them, but it makes them visible as problems of geometry, not only of morality. And in a world where morality has so often become a weapon to throw at others, it may be useful to have a language that describes before it prescribes and measures before it condemns.
