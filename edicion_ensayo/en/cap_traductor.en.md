There is a kind of horizon cultural biography documents again and again without ever finding a precise name for it: the genius who cannot find the bank. The composer unable to sign a contract without help. The painter who, at seventy, would not know how to heat up soup without burning the pot. The world-class violinist who never learned to drive, because the hours others spent learning he had spent, since he was five, with the violin under his chin.

---

This book has described the permeability of the horizon: how much of another's signal it lets in. This reading deals with something different and, in a way, inverse: how much of a horizon's own signal it manages to get out without another's help.

Every horizon needs some way of translating itself into the outside world: the part of oneself able to talk to a clerk, sign what has to be signed, remember to eat. In most horizons that function coexists, more or less integrated, with the rest of the architecture. In some it does not, and then another person usually ends up taking it on.

### The mind that steps outside the skull

In 1998, the philosophers Andy Clark and David Chalmers proposed a thought experiment that has proved extraordinarily fertile. They imagined Otto, a man with early Alzheimer's who always carries a notebook where he writes down every address and every piece of data his biological memory no longer retains, and Inga, whose memory works normally. Otto's notebook, they argued, fulfills the same role as Inga's biological memory: it is always available, he trusts it without question, and he acts directly on what it says. If so, the notebook is part of Otto's mind.

Clark and Chalmers were talking about notebooks and calculators. But a notebook has no agenda of its own, no fatigue, no inner life. When the function of translation is fulfilled not by an object but by another complete horizon, the resulting system is a mind expanded by another mind, willing, for years or decades, to subordinate much of its own activity to the first one's.

### Memory split in two

In the mid-1980s, Daniel Wegner gave this phenomenon a name that has proved fruitful far beyond social psychology: transactive memory. He observed that long-term couples do not store the same information each on their own; they specialize: one remembers the dates, the other the addresses; one handles the money, the other the relationships. Neither possesses the complete system alone: the complete system is the couple.

Wegner later documented what happens when that system breaks: the loss of a long-term spouse deprives one not only of company, but also of operational access to capacities that, although they never resided in one's own brain, were an active part of the survivor's everyday cognition. It is like a two-partner company in which only one knows how to deal with the banks: if that partner leaves, the company does not lose an employee, but an entire function.

### Gala: the economic translator

Few cases are as well documented as the marriage of Salvador Dalí and Gala. They met in the summer of 1929 in Cadaqués: Dalí was twenty-five and lived, according to his biographers, trapped between phobias, obsessive rituals and an extreme shyness that would have made it almost impossible for him to sustain the public life his future fame would demand. Gala, who left Paul Éluard and their daughter to be with him, over time took on a role that went far beyond that of muse: she managed the contracts, set the prices, negotiated with the gallerists and demanded advances with a firmness her own interlocutors described as legendary. Dalí came to sign much of his later output as "Gala Salvador Dalí," an almost literal acknowledgment that his work could no longer be separated from her management of it.

But Gala's translation did not stop at the system's output. In 1941, as her biographer Ian Gibson documents, it was she who convinced Dalí that his surrealist period had already given all it could give, and that something greater awaited him if he was willing to leave it behind. The following years brought the shift Dalí himself would call classicism and, later, nuclear mysticism: Renaissance perspective, the golden ratio, religious themes, a geometry that replaced the overflow of the paranoiac-critical method with a form the eye could follow from beginning to end. Gala did not merely sell that shift once it had happened: she proposed it before it happened.

That moves the translator's function from the system's output to its input. It is not just that Gala turned the already-made work into contracts and galleries, but that at some point she began to intervene in the grammar by which that work would be made: which part of Dalí's inner overflow deserved classical form and which remained, simply, noise. An output translator transmits the signal as it arrives. An input translator decides, before that, what counts as signal. It is the difference between an editor who corrects an already-written manuscript and one who, before a single line is written, decides which book is worth writing.

There is no clean way to separate, in this case, commercial strategy from aesthetic calibration. Postwar classicism sold better in the American market than 1930s surrealism, and Gala knew it. But it was also a more contained form, more governed by external rules, for a horizon whose inner turbulence may have needed exactly that containment in order to keep producing without burning out. In this case, the commercial landing and the formal one did not compete with each other: they were probably the same operation seen from the two ends of the same channel.

### Gala in her own words

Almost everything said so far we know through Dalí, through his biographers or through those who negotiated with Gala, never through her. There is a partial exception. Gala kept up a correspondence throughout her life with Dimitri Gomberg, the Moscow lawyer who married her mother when she was a child and whom she always called father. In a 1943 letter, already settled into her role beside Dalí, she describes herself in terms that might seem like traditional resignation (she says that, like every Russian woman, she tries to help her husband in everything) and then explains what that help consists of: she poses as a model, acts as secretary in the practical side of life, because he is completely submerged in creative work and incapable of dealing with such things. And she closes with a line that could be lifted from this very chapter: that she is not especially brilliant, but that both of them work for the one thing that truly matters, that a talent be able to express itself.

It is the only time, among everything that survives, that Gala explains her function in her own words, not before a gallerist or before posterity, but in private, to a stepfather on the other side of a world war. And there is a second piece, this time on the input side of the system, that Dalí himself left in writing. On finishing *The Persistence of Memory* in 1931, he watched Gala's face as she looked at the completed painting, and saw in it, almost in his own words, the unmistakable contraction of astonishment. That, he said, was what convinced him the image worked, because Gala never erred in judging the authenticity of an enigma.

It is not a minor detail. An output translator need not have an opinion about the painting, only sell it. But here Dalí describes something different: a verdict, painting by painting, on whether the signal had become what it was supposed to be before anyone else saw it. Gala did not only decide the general direction of the turn toward classicism; she certified, work by work, whether that direction had been fulfilled.

It is worth resisting, all the same, the temptation to turn this into a story of silent renunciation. Besides the letters to her stepfather, Gala left a diary of her own written in French, a language adopted after leaving Russia, which remained unpublished until 2011, almost thirty years after her death. The exhibition the National Art Museum of Catalonia devoted to her in 2018, the first dedicated to her alone, carried an eloquent title: *A Room of One's Own in Púbol*. The castle was also the only place where she practiced, unwitnessed, her own writing.

This story should not be flattened into tenderness. Gala's own biographers disagree about how much of her role was devotion and how much a partnership from which she herself benefited: she managed, after all, an estate she depended on as much as he did. Dalí gave her a castle, Púbol, which, under the pact between them, he could only enter with her written invitation. Translation into the outside world was, here too, a long-term contract between two horizons with different needs.

What is unambiguous is what happened when the system broke. Gala died in June 1982. Dalí stopped creating almost entirely and sank into a silence he barely emerged from until his own death, seven years later. He had lost half the system that turned his inner intensity into something the outside world could receive.

### Ángeles de Castro: the domestic translator

Miguel Delibes documented a much more private variant of the same structure, though indirectly: not in a biography, but in a novel that took him almost two decades to write after living through the event that gave rise to it. In *Señora de rojo sobre fondo gris* (1991), Nicolás, a painter, addresses a long monologue to his daughter, imprisoned for political reasons, while reliving, in time jumps, the sudden illness and death of his wife. The novel is a barely veiled transposition of Delibes's own life: his wife, Ángeles de Castro, died on November 22, 1974, at fifty-one, of a brain tumor, after giving him seven children. Delibes waited almost twenty years to publish what he himself described as a tribute to his better half.

The title is not an invented metaphor. *Mujer de rojo* is a real portrait of Ángeles de Castro painted by Eduardo García Benito in 1962, which Delibes hung behind his desk, where it stayed for the rest of his life. He wrote, quite literally, under her gaze.

Ángeles de Castro's translation, both in Delibes's telling and in what is known of the real relationship, operated above all on the domestic register: she sustained, organized and translated the everyday life of someone whose energy was almost entirely committed elsewhere. In the novel, it is she who restores houses so that he can work in silence, who manages his schedule without needing to consult him, who, in words Delibes himself put in Nicolás's mouth, cleared the path so he could work unburdened.

### The angels that were not angels

But *Señora de rojo* does not describe only an output translation. In the novel, Nicolás attributes his ability to paint to angels who come down, or don't, depending on the day. When his wife falls ill, the angels stop coming down: he faces the blank canvas, destroys brushes, turns down invitations to give lectures because, he says, a painter who cannot paint has no right to talk about painting. Only at the end, in a nighttime scene in which she finds him going through his own paintings, does Nicolás admit what he had spent months avoiding: that they were never angels, that it was her; that his wife's faith impregnated him because creative energy was, somehow, transmissible, and that creative activity is impossible if no one is pushing from behind.

This does not stay in fiction. Delibes acknowledged in an interview that the same thing had happened to him as to Nicolás: that he wrote for her, that when he lost her judgment he lost his point of reference, and that for a long time he found it hard to write again. And there is a fact that predates the illness, not follows it: Ángeles was the only person who read the manuscript of *La sombra del ciprés es alargada* before Delibes submitted it for the 1947 Nadal Prize, the prize that, as he himself admitted, decided whether he would keep writing. In that anecdote there are no angels, only a first reader and an editorial decision, even before the rest of the world had anything to read.

### A translation less private than it seems

It is worth qualifying, however, the label "domestic translator." There was also a public register, though of a different kind from Gala's: not contracts, but recognition. Delibes signed his first cartoons in *El Norte de Castilla*, even before marrying, with the pseudonym MAX: Miguel, Ángeles, and an X representing the future they would build together. His book *Diario de un emigrante* (1958) has carried, from its first edition and in every one since, the dedication "To Ángeles de Castro de Delibes, the balance; my balance."

And in 1975, a year after Ángeles's death, Delibes was inducted into the Royal Spanish Academy. His induction speech, a public act the Academy itself later published, does not begin by talking about literature, but about her: he says she was, along with their children, the axis of his life and the stimulus for his work, the point of reference for his thoughts and activities, and that with her disappearance the better half of himself died. His friend Julián Marías answered him, also in public, with a line that has endured: that Delibes's work could not be understood without the company of that woman whose mere presence lightened the weight of life. It is the official response of a fellow academician to an induction speech, delivered on the same day and before the same audience.

In those same years of silence, José Ortega Spottorno offered Delibes the editorship of *El País*, Spain's first great daily of the democracy. Delibes turned it down: he said his mind could no longer take in such ambitious projects, that he would settle for something smaller and more familiar. It is a public, dated professional decision, as concrete as any contract Gala negotiated for Dalí, and it occurs precisely in the temporal gap in which, according to this chapter, the translator was missing.

Fifty years after her death, the Miguel Delibes Foundation organized an exhibition devoted entirely to her: *Ángeles, el equilibrio de Miguel Delibes*. Their children insisted on a distinction worth keeping: it was not a tribute to their mother, but the last, posthumous tribute of their father to her. Even the institution that vindicates her half a century later does so through his gaze.

### George Henry Lewes: the reversed translator

Almost every time this structure is told, the genius is a man and the translator a woman. The case of Marian Evans, whom the world knew, and still knows, as George Eliot, reverses the roles without reversing the structure.

Evans met George Henry Lewes in 1851, in the same London editorial world where she worked as a translator and editor of the *Westminster Review*: he was already, in his own right, an established literary critic, philosopher and man of science. They went to live together in 1854 and lived as husband and wife until his death, in 1878, never able to marry, because Lewes, separated from his legal wife, could not divorce.

It was Lewes who, around 1856, convinced Evans to leave translation and criticism, the register in which she already moved with ease, and try fiction, ground she had never set foot on. It was also Lewes who proposed the male pseudonym: partly to shield the work from prejudice against women novelists, and partly, according to her biographers, to protect Evans, whose reputation already carried the stigma of living with a married man, from a second scandal. The decision of what to try to write, and under what name to sign it, came before a single page of *Adam Bede* existed.

From then on, Lewes acted as something very close to what today would be called a literary agent: he negotiated contracts and advances with the publisher John Blackwood, managed the household finances and hosted a weekly salon that served, among other things, to keep Eliot's name visible in the right circles. And he filtered in the other direction too: he read the reviews before she did and hid the negative ones from her, while feeding publishers themselves the idea that their author had such an extreme sensitivity that the slightest criticism might make her stop writing forever.

Not all recent criticism accepts this image of the devoted protector without question. The literary historian Beverley Park Rilett has argued that much of that story (Lewes as the indispensable shield of a fragile Eliot) comes largely from Lewes's own letters and memoirs, and may exaggerate both his intervention and her fragility. It is the same discussion, with the genders reversed, this chapter already noted regarding Gala: how much of the documented help was real necessity and how much the story the translator himself built around his own function.

There is, however, an asymmetry this case does not share with the previous two. Lewes was not only Eliot's translator: he had his own career, his own work and his own reputation as a critic, philosopher and physiologist, with a biography of Goethe still cited today. Unlike Gala or Ángeles de Castro, whom we know almost solely through the genius they translated, Lewes left an archive of his own, independent of hers.

Lewes died on November 30, 1878. Eliot never wrote another novel. She spent the two years of life left to her editing his unfinished manuscript, *Problems of Life and Mind*; she married John Walter Cross in May 1880 and died seven months later.

The parallel with Dalí or Delibes should not be forced here. Eliot was not left paralyzed: she completed and published Lewes's unfinished work, managed his legacy and, less than two years later, decided to remarry. None of that is paralysis. What she lost, to be precise, was not executive capacity in general, which kept working, but the specific register Lewes had helped her find: fiction. She stopped writing novels, not living. It is a nuance worth not overlooking just because it fits less neatly than the other two cases.

### What the myth doesn't tell

All three stories tend to be told as love legends. It is worth resisting that reading, at least in part. A structure in which one horizon concentrates almost all its energy on one register and delegates the rest to another is not, in itself, admirable or condemnable: it is an architecture with concrete costs. And the cost is rarely shared equally.

About Gala we know, thanks in part to her own effort to manage her reputation too, considerably more than we usually know about a domestic translator: she had lovers, kept a castle of her own, left documentary evidence of her ambition, and even a diary she wrote only for herself, which no one read until thirty years after her death. About Ángeles de Castro we have recovered more than the myth acknowledges (a public pseudonym, an academic speech, an institutional exhibition), but each of those pieces names her through someone else's voice: the husband who signed, the husband who spoke, the husband whose children organized the tribute. The asymmetry is not that we know more about one and less about the other, but that Gala, at some point, wrote for herself knowing no one would read it yet. Ángeles de Castro never had that notebook of her own, or, if she did, it has not reached us. We know her almost entirely through Nicolás's memory, that is, through Delibes's memory. Half a century later, the couple's story is still the one he told.

About Lewes, by contrast, we know almost as much as about Eliot, and through the same channel: he wrote and published under his own name throughout his life, and his work (philosophical, scientific, dramatic) survives independently of hers. That breaks, at least in this case, the rule the first two examples seemed to establish: archival asymmetry does not depend only on who translates and who is translated, but on whether the translator also had a voice of their own the world was already listening to before it met the genius. Gala and Ángeles de Castro did not. Lewes did.

Sustaining another horizon's translation for decades (negotiating their world, organizing their time, absorbing their social clumsiness) is work, even if the language of romantic love prefers not to call it that.

### When the translator disappears

The structure described in this chapter is an extreme version of the entanglement this book called love, extended from the purely affective register into the executive one. When the translator disappears, what is lost, besides company and emotional regulation, is the part of the system that literally knew how to turn the inner note into something the world could receive.

That explains, more precisely than any tale of genius and muse, why Dalí barely painted again after Gala's death (his last painting, *The Swallow's Tail*, is from 1983), why Delibes's Nicolás, like Delibes himself in all likelihood, is left paralyzed before the most basic tasks of daily life in the weeks that follow the loss, and why Eliot, without being paralyzed, never wrote a single novel again either, in the two years left to her after Lewes's death. It is the structural description of what happens when a horizon suddenly loses the part of its own cognition that resided, by full right and without metaphor, in another person.

And if there really was, in all three cases, input translation as well as output translation, the loss is even more complete than the image of a piano missing half its keyboard suggests. It is not only that the remaining keys cannot play the whole piece, but that, for a time, no one knows which piece is worth playing. When Ángeles died, Delibes's writing did not just dry up: in those same years he turned down the editorship of *El País*, the most ambitious project offered to him in his entire career, because his mind, he said, could no longer take in anything so big. He had not lost the capacity to run a newspaper, but the person who, for thirty years, had told him which projects were his size.

What this reading describes is a lens that magnifies grief: when what was lost was not only companionship but a shared functional architecture, grief is not only the absence of someone, but the loss of part of one's own capacity to be in the world.

> **What we do know:** Gala's management of Dalí's career and contracts, and Dalí's creative silence after her death in 1982, are extensively documented by his biographers. Ian Gibson documents that in 1941 it was Gala who actively drove Dalí's turn toward classicism, leaving his surrealist period behind. Gala's letter to her stepfather Dimitri Gomberg (1943) and Gala's unpublished diary, published in 2011, are catalogued at the Centre for Dalinian Studies and were central to the MNAC's 2018 exhibition. The death of Ángeles de Castro, Miguel Delibes's wife, on November 22, 1974, and the autobiographical relationship between that loss and *Señora de rojo sobre fondo gris* (1991), are confirmed by Delibes himself and his biographers, as are his induction speech at the Royal Spanish Academy (1975), Julián Marías's public reply at that same event, and his refusal of the editorship of *El País* in those years. Wegner's transactive memory and Clark and Chalmers's extended mind are established frameworks in social psychology and philosophy of mind, respectively. The relationship between Marian Evans (George Eliot) and George Henry Lewes, his role in Evans's move from translation and criticism to fiction, the choice of the male pseudonym, the management of contracts with John Blackwood, and Lewes's death in 1878, are documented in the standard biographies of both and discussed specifically in Rilett (2017).
>
> **What we don't know:** How much of Gala's role was devotion, shared economic strategy, or both at once (her own biographers disagree). Whether Gala's diary, written in a third, adopted language, reflects her experience unmediated or is also, in its own way, a construction of herself for a posterity she at some point imagined. How Ángeles de Castro would describe, in her own words and not in those of the speech, the dedication or the exhibition that name her, the function this chapter attributes to her: that notebook of her own, if it existed, has not reached us. How much of the narrative of Lewes as the indispensable shield of a fragile Eliot reflects the real relationship and how much was constructed largely by Lewes himself in his letters and memoirs: this is the central question of the Rilett article cited below.
>
> **Open questions:** Is it possible to sustain so asymmetric a translation without the translator losing part of their own horizon in the process? Does this structure change when both members of the couple are, each in their own way, geniuses of a narrow register? Is input translation (deciding what counts as signal before the work exists) a different function from output translation, or are they the same mechanism applied at two points in the cycle? Does the archival asymmetry depend on the gender of the one who translates, or simply on whether the translator already had a recognized voice of their own before taking on that function? Is the loss of the translator always general paralysis, or can it manifest as the loss of one specific register while the rest of the system keeps functioning?
>
> **If you take away only one idea:** Behind many horizons the world calls brilliant, there is another horizon, almost always less visible, who learned to translate them. Losing that translator is losing part of one's own system.
