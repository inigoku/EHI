So far we have talked about entanglement between people and between people and animals. But the human horizon does not stop at the border of the living: it couples with places, objects, ideas, abstractions without body or pulse. And sometimes these couplings are so dense, so persistent, so decisive for identity, that calling them "mere projection" underestimates the real geometry of what is going on.

### Place as a shared horizon

A place is information accumulated in space.

The room you grew up in is a density of correlations between every object, every corner, every hour of the day. The window onto the courtyard holds the light of a thousand mornings; the mark on the door frame, your height at seven. The smell of the wardrobe, the creak of a certain floorboard, the shadow the curtain draws at five in the afternoon: all of this is information the horizon integrated over years, until it stopped being "information about the place" and became "part of who I am." It is the difference between a photo of your house and your house.

Whoever says "I miss my city" does not miss the city as a map, but the specific configuration of signals it provided: the density of information per unit of time, the predictability of certain sounds, the way the light fell on certain corners. The city was a coupling, a shared horizon, not with a person, but with a territory.

This explains uprooting, not as romantic melancholy, but as the dissolution of an entanglement. The horizon that moves away from its place of origin undergoes a self-contraction: it stops receiving the signals it was calibrated with and does not find in the new place the same density of correlations. The new place is not worse; the horizon has not yet had time to become entangled with it. And the time of entanglement, as we saw in Chapter 10, is time of integration.

Some places function as secondary horizons: the childhood home, the village square, the library where you studied, the mountain you saw from your window. They are nodes of correlation in which identity was deposited layer by layer. The human horizon includes what it left in those places and what those places gave back to it over the years.

### The things that hold us up

A beloved object is a node of entanglement.

The old T-shirt you don't throw away, your grandfather's watch, the chipped mug, the book with notes in the margins: all of them hold information the horizon integrated at significant moments. It is not magic: the object works as a temporal anchor, a mark in space that allows the horizon to access past configurations again. When you touch your grandfather's watch, you are touching not metal and glass, but a frequency: that of your grandfather's horizon, which became entangled with you through that object and persists in the residual correlation.

We are a species that makes objects precisely because objects extend the horizon. A tool is a projection of the horizon onto the world, a way for inner intention to produce effects outside. In its origin, technology is entanglement with the inanimate in order to extend what the body cannot do alone.

But there is a boundary. When the object shifts from extension to substitute, the architecture becomes deformed. Whoever cannot sleep without their pillow, think without their pen or feel safe without their phone is not using objects, but delegating the boundary of the horizon to them. The object becomes a prosthesis of the boundary itself, and its absence causes the same kind of collapse as that of a loved one. It is not exactly an addiction: the horizon was built including that object in its architecture and, without it, the boundary is exposed.

---

### Ideas as shared horizons

An idea is a configuration of information that many horizons can adopt at once.

When you believe in an idea (justice, freedom, equality, beauty), your horizon does not "contain" it as if it were an object, but reconfigures itself so that it works as an organizing principle. The idea becomes a criterion of integration: new information is assessed according to whether or not it is compatible with that configuration. The horizon that adopts an idea is, in a real sense, entangled with everyone who adopted the same one, not because there is a physical channel between them, but because they share the same informational geometry.

That is what makes ideas so powerful and so dangerous. An idea truly adopted becomes architecture. Changing one's mind, far from being like changing one's shirt, forces a reconfiguration of the horizon, and that costs the same kind of energy as breaking an entanglement with a person: the horizon has to uncouple from a geometry that gave it coherence for years and couple to another that has not yet proved it can hold it up.

That is why ideological conversions are so rare and so traumatic. People may want to see the counterevidence; what happens is that their horizon cannot integrate it without destabilizing. Counterevidence arrives as a threat to the architecture.

### Religion and fanaticism: two cases this book treats separately

Religion and fanaticism are, each in its own way, entanglement with what the horizon cannot touch directly: the first, with the inaccessible that sustains without being confirmed (later we will call it "coupling with the inaccessible"); the second, with an idea that has taken possession of the boundary itself. Neither fits here at the length it deserves. Religion and contemplative practice (prayer, the Eucharist, community) have chapters of their own later on: Chapter 28 compares five traditions point by point and Chapter 29 develops in detail the technique common to prayer and meditation. Fanaticism, a pathological entanglement with an idea (the fanatical horizon cannot doubt, because for it doubting is not questioning a proposition, but dissolving the boundary that keeps it intact), receives the same attention in Chapter 30, applied there to its political form.

### The inanimate as a mirror

The human horizon is the only one we know capable of becoming entangled with what does not live, think or feel: with places that endure, objects that persist, ideas that transcend, presences that cannot be touched.

And that is its greatest power: the capacity to couple with the inanimate is what makes culture, technology, art, religion and science possible. A horizon that could only become entangled with other living horizons would be trapped forever in the biological present. Entanglement with the inanimate is the doorway to time, to space, to abstraction, to what is not here but can be real all the same.

But that capacity has its shadow. The same mechanism that allows us to weep over a historical tragedy allows us to become fanatical about an ideology; the one that lets us feel at home in a strange city lets us get trapped in a place that no longer exists; the one that makes the beauty of art possible makes dependence on objects possible. The horizon does not, by itself, distinguish between a coupling that nourishes and a parasitic one. That distinction is ethical, and geometry does not resolve it. And ethics, as we will see in Chapter 51, is precisely what the horizon hypothesis cannot decide for us.

What it can say is that every horizon is entangled with more things than it knows, and that the task of a life is to become conscious of those entanglements: to know which places hold you up, which objects imprison you, which ideas enlarge you and which shrink you; to know where your horizon ends and where the echo of something you adopted without noticing begins.

From this model, freedom is not the absence of entanglement, but knowledge of the network.

> **Note to Chapter 27**
>
> **What we do know:** Humans form attachment bonds with places (topophilia), objects (transitional objects, Winnicott) and ideas (ideological identity).
>
> **What we don't know:** Whether coupling with the inanimate works through the same neural mechanisms as interpersonal coupling. How the "density" of an entanglement with an idea is measured.
>
> **Open questions:** Can a horizon be healthy without coupling to the transcendent? What distinguishes an idea that expands from one that imprisons?
>
> **If you take away only one idea:** Your horizon is made of people, places, things and ideas. Knowing which they are, which hold you up and which consume you, is the only freedom the model can point to.
>
> **Further reading:** Winnicott, D. W., *Playing and Reality* (1971); Tuan, Y.-F., *Topophilia* (1974); Sloterdijk, P., *Spheres* (1998–2004).
