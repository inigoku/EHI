If the horizon hypothesis does not solve the hard problem, does not predict causal mechanisms, does not answer ethical questions, does not console us before death, and does not explain love, what is it for?

For this: **ordering experience so that it becomes more habitable**.

Not more true: more habitable.

---

When someone says "time passes faster the older I get," the horizon hypothesis offers a way of understanding it that is neither a simple metaphor nor a simple cliché: the horizon's rate of integration decreases because the patterns are consolidated and every new stimulus finds less uncharted surface. Time does not "pass" faster: less happens per hour.

There is something deeper here, something the everyday complaint about time conceals. As children, every day brought never-before-seen patterns: the first contact with the sea, the first fall from a bicycle, the first kiss, the first betrayal. The child's horizon is a territory in constant expansion, a map drawing itself in real time. Every new experience creates connections and possibilities for integration, and that is why a summer day at eight years old can contain more "events" of consciousness than a whole month at forty. It is not nostalgia that makes childhood time seem denser: it really was, measured in integrated information per unit of time.

But it is not an irrevocable sentence. Recognizing that subjective time is density of integration opens a practical door: within certain limits, we can recover slowness. Not by denying aging, but by deliberately introducing genuine novelty into the horizon. Learning a language at sixty is not mere entertainment: it forces the horizon to chart unknown territories and deploy resources of integration we thought were lost. Traveling to a place where nothing works the way it does at home, where even ordering a coffee demands full attention, is training for the horizon.

When someone says "I miss my father, but I don't know exactly what I miss," the hypothesis offers an answer: you don't miss his specific acts, but the geometry your horizon had when he was there to confirm it or call it into question. The gap is a shape that is no longer updated.

Seen this way, grief stops being a process of "getting over it" and becomes a process of cartographic reorganization. For years, maybe decades, your horizon learned to predict that person's presence: their voice in certain tones, their silence at certain moments, their way of entering a room. All of that was part of the predictive landscape of your experience, of the topography that shaped your days. When they die, the model does not disappear at once: it persists and keeps generating predictions that are no longer confirmed in the world. Every time you get home and expect to hear their voice, every time you have news to share and your hand reaches for the phone on its own, you experience the prediction noise of a model that no longer has a referent.

The cruelty of grief lies not only in the absence, but in the persistence of presence as expectation. The horizon keeps drawing the outline of someone no longer there, and that phantom shape hurts more than any emptiness. But recognizing it, naming it as an architecture of the horizon and not as simple emotional weakness, changes something. It does not cure grief, because grief is not an illness to be cured, but a geological transformation of the terrain of experience. Naming it as a reorganization of the horizon allows, at least, no longer blaming yourself for "not getting over it," for still missing them after years, for discovering that some days the ghost returns with the intensity of the first.

When someone says "I can't stop thinking about that person even though I know it's not good for me," the hypothesis answers that your horizon is calibrated to integrate their signal, and when the signal is missing, the system generates prediction noise. Obsessive thought is an architecture waiting for stimuli that no longer arrive.

Here the horizon hypothesis contributes something popular psychology usually gets wrong. It is not about "stopping thinking" by force of will, because will itself operates within the horizon, not from outside it. Every attempt to suppress the thought paradoxically generates more activity in the predictive circuit: the horizon detects the suppression as novelty, integrates it, and thereby reinforces the network containing the object of the thought. It is the old dilemma of "don't think about a pink elephant": denial demands representation.

What the model suggests, instead, is another strategy: not suppressing the thought, but filling the horizon with alternative stimuli that compete for the same resources of integration. It is not enough to "get distracted": you have to deliberately introduce signals of high novelty (learning, environments, new bonds) that demand so much processing that the persistent predictive model gradually loses its dominance. It is not eliminated: it is diluted into a broader horizon.

These formulations cure nothing, but they can **change the relationship with the experience**. Moving from "something is happening to me and I don't know what it is" to "what is happening to me has a shape I can name, even if I can't change it" is already, in itself, a relief.

### Anxiety as premature contraction of the horizon

Anxiety has many faces: catastrophic anticipation, objectless fear, hypervigilance, paralysis before trivial decisions. But, from the horizon's perspective, they all share the same mechanism: a premature contraction of the field of integration. The anxious horizon does not integrate what is happening, but anticipates what could happen, and does so with such intensity that threat predictions eclipse present stimuli.

The problem is that prediction consumes resources, whether or not it is accurate. When the horizon devotes almost all its capacity for integration to future scenarios of danger, little is left for the present, and the present, unintegrated, becomes ghostly, insubstantial, a mere transition between the anxious now and the feared later. The anxious person does not live in the future, as is sometimes said: they live in a present so saturated with negative predictions that the real present never gets to be integrated.

In the face of anxiety, the practice of the horizon does not consist in denying the predictions, but in deliberately broadening the field of integration so that the present regains density. The "sensory grounding" technique (what do you see now? what do you hear? what do you feel in your feet?) is not magical distraction: it forces the horizon to integrate concrete, current sensory information that competes with the abstract predictions. It does not eliminate anxiety, but it can change the ratio between integrated present and imagined future.

### The body as embodied horizon

We have spoken of the horizon as if it were something purely cerebral, a function of information in neural networks. But experience does not happen in an isolated brain, but in a body that breathes, that has weight, temperature, posture, a heart rate. And that body is, itself, part of the horizon.

When the body is sick, when pain occupies the center of the sensory field, the horizon contracts around that signal. Everything else (thoughts, relationships, projects) loses integrating density. A mild toothache can make all the philosophy in the world seem irrelevant, not because philosophy has changed, but because the horizon is occupied.

But the body can also be an avenue of expansion. Bodily practices, such as tai chi, mindful walking or swimming, do not only modify the body, but the distribution of integrated information in the horizon. By paying deliberate attention to movements that are usually automatic, novelty is introduced into the sensory field: the horizon has to re-chart territories it thought it knew, and in that new cartography, spaces sometimes emerge where before there was only automatism.

### Meditation as modification of the horizon

If subjective time is density of integration, any practice that modifies the rate of integration modifies time. Meditation, in its more concentrated forms, reduces external stimuli and increases attention to internal processes. The result is that the same clock hour contains more "events" of integration, because every instant breaks down into more differentiated components, even though no more things happen; in memory, that hour weighs more. It is like looking at a leaf through a magnifying glass: the leaf hasn't changed; the attention has.

States of deep absorption (jhana, samadhi) take this to the extreme: external stimuli are reduced so much that the horizon feeds only on itself. What advanced meditators report about time (that an hour seems to last minutes, or that time disappears entirely) is consistent with the model's prediction: with no new stimuli to integrate, the horizon stops accumulating differentiated "nows," and both the lived time and the remembered time that Chapter 10 distinguished fade away together.

But here the experiment meets another limit. Meditation, besides modifying the rate of integration, is a **relationship with the reservoir**, with the field the horizon arises from and returns to. The contemplative traditions that describe the dissolution of the self do not describe (only) a change in integration, but something the horizon's vocabulary does not capture: the experience that the horizon, with all its content, is the "projection" of something more fundamental that has no horizon shape.

The experiment can describe it as the limit at which integration remains high, as the interlude suggested, but the boundary stops separating an inside from an outside; that description is not the experience, and in this territory what counts is the experience.

There is more to say about meditation as a practice of the horizon. Many people abandon it because they expect it to "calm the mind" and discover that sitting in silence only amplifies the inner noise. From the horizon's perspective this is foreseeable: by reducing external stimuli, the horizon has more resources to integrate the internal processes that usually stay in the background. The mind does not become noisier; the noise that was already there becomes audible.

Over time, something changes. The noise is still there, but the horizon learns not to integrate it as a threat. Thoughts, sensations and emotions pass, and the horizon stops treating each one as a stimulus demanding immediate response. A kind of "slack" develops in integration: a space between signal and reaction, between stimulus and response. That slack, small at first, almost imperceptible, is perhaps the closest thing to freedom the horizon can experience from within.

### Creativity as reorganization of the horizon

Creativity consists in reorganizing what is already in the horizon so as to produce new patterns. The painter does not create colors that did not exist: they reorganize the perception of colors into configurations the horizon had not integrated before. The musician does not invent sounds, but relationships between sounds.

From the horizon's perspective, creative block has a clear explanation: the horizon uses its integration resources on predictive patterns so consolidated that it has no capacity left for alternative configurations. "Inspiration" is the moment when some unexpected stimulus (a dream, a conversation, a mistake) forces the horizon to chart outside its usual routes.

Creative practices (writing, drawing, improvising music) are, in this sense, ways of training the horizon to tolerate the uncertainty of reorganization. Every blank page, every empty canvas, is an act of faith in the horizon's capacity to generate patterns with no prior map. And every finished work, however imperfect, proves the horizon can do something it had not done before.

### Memory as cartography of the horizon

We have discussed memory in earlier chapters, but it is worth returning to it. Memory is an active reconstruction: every time we remember something, the horizon reintegrates it, and in that reintegration it can modify it. That explains both the plasticity of memory and its fragility.

But there is another aspect. Memory does not only record the past; it configures the present. What we remember, and how we remember it, determines which predictive patterns are currently active in the horizon. Two people in the same place at the same moment can live radically different experiences because their horizons bring different memories that filter present stimuli and give them meaning. It is like two people looking at the same cloud: one sees a ship, the other, a storm.

This has practical consequences. We cannot change what happened to us, but we can, to some extent, modify how those facts are integrated into the present horizon. Therapies that work with memory reconsolidation do not erase the past: they reorganize its weight in the current topology of the horizon. And that, sometimes, is enough for the present to stop being a prisoner of the past.

### The experiment as a serious game

I have called this a thought experiment, a game. But there are games that are not frivolous. Chess is a game, and yet it trains planning and working memory. Meditation is a game, in the sense that it pursues no external goal, and yet it changes the structure of the brain.

The horizon experiment is a game in that sense: it has no direct practical consequences. It does not cure diseases, does not build bridges, does not predict the weather. But it orders experience in a way that lets us see patterns that were not seen before, and seeing patterns is a necessary, though not sufficient, condition for any intelligent action.

If someone who has read this experiment discovers they can talk about their grief with a more precise vocabulary, understand their addiction without unnecessary moral guilt, or recognize in their relationship a geometry that belongs neither only to them nor only to the other, but to the two together, then the experiment has served some purpose.

Not to discover the truth, but to inhabit the question with more clarity.

And perhaps that is its deepest value: not in the answers it gives, but in the questions it allows us to formulate precisely. For centuries, human experience has been described with vocabularies drawn from religion, morality, pathology or economics. Each has its use, but also its blind spots. The vocabulary of the horizon does not claim to replace them, but to add one more perspective: that of an information system that arises, sustains itself for a while, and dissolves.

When someone who has been through an extreme experience (an accident, a loss, a revelation) says "I came out a different person," the horizon's vocabulary allows us to understand it literally: their horizon of integrated information underwent a reorganization so deep that the continuity with the previous "self" is functional, not substantial. It is the description of a physical process, even though the physical here is information, not matter.

### The experiment as company in solitude

There is a solitude born of a lack of understanding, even in the presence of other people. Existential solitude, the kind that company does not cure, arises when the horizon encounters experiences its usual vocabulary cannot integrate. The death of a loved one, the encounter with one's own end, the revelation that nothing we thought solid is solid: these experiences can leave the horizon facing a nameless territory.

The horizon experiment does not fill that solitude. It is not human company, or an embrace, or a presence. But it offers something extreme solitude desperately needs: a language. Not to explain the inexplicable, but to inhabit it without it being total chaos. Calling death "evaporation toward the reservoir" does not make it less final, but it can make it less absurd. Calling love "mutual expansion of the horizon" does not make it safer, but it can make it more understandable when it fails.

Every thought experiment eventually runs into its limit in the flesh of experience. The horizon can name death, but not die for us; it can name love, but not love for us; it can name meditation, but not sit on the cushion for us. It is in that space between the name and the thing, between the model and the experience, that life happens. The experiment does not claim to occupy that space; it claims, at most, to light it enough for us to walk through it with a little more awareness, a little less fear, and a little more wonder.

> **What we do know:** Sustained meditation practice is associated with measurable changes in brain structure and activity. Naming an emotion reduces amygdala reactivity (affect labeling, Lieberman et al., 2007).

> **What we don't know:** Whether meditation is a "modification of the horizon" or something more. Whether the relief given by vocabulary is lasting.

> **Open questions:** Can the experiment be a "practice" without being a therapy? Where does the game end and life begin?
>
> **If you take away only one idea:** This book promises no answers. It promises, at most, more clarity in the question itself.
