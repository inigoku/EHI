Now let us use imagination and take the model to its limit, to see where it leads. What follows is no longer a conclusion of the experiment, but a hypothesis of the author's: none of it follows from the physics or the topology of the previous chapters, and the reader may take it or leave it.

There is a question almost no one asks, and which appears on its own as soon as someone has spent years imagining a dragon: not what I see when I think of it, but what it sees. From inside its cave, the dragon has no way of peering into the room where I am thinking it. It can look at the rock ceiling all it wants: it will not see me. And yet I am there, holding it up, deciding whether it will fly tonight. That is the only relationship of this kind I can see whole, because in it I occupy the upper side. And as soon as I see it whole, it grows uncomfortable at the other end: if I am, for something, that invisible sustainer no one can look at, I have no way of knowing there isn't, above my own ceiling, someone in the same position relative to me. The question is one of place: who contains whom. That is the matter of what follows. To walk through it, the geometry of the problem must first change, and the change begins somewhere small, almost philological.

Taoism revolves around a single concept: the Tao. The Tao is the background from which everything proceeds without itself proceeding from anything, "something mixed and complete, prior to heaven and earth." Someone coming from another tradition may find a simile useful, with every reservation: the Tao occupies in Taoism a place similar to what God occupies in monotheistic religions (the ultimate origin, that from which things hang), except that it is not a person: it wants nothing, it does not answer, and the name given to it is not it. One of its canonical texts, the *Zhuangzi*, introduces, through a story, one more concept: Hun Dun, the emperor of the center, a featureless fullness, without the seven openings with which others see, hear, breathe and eat. The sovereigns of the North and South, grateful for his hospitality, decide to repay it by opening one for him each day; on the seventh, Hun Dun has ceased to exist. It is easy to read that death as the birth of ordinary consciousness: featureless fullness fragments into perceptions, and where there was a continuous background a world with edges appears. That featureless fullness already had a name in what has been read so far: the reservoir, the ocean from which waves arise and to which they return, the shape water borrows and gives back. The reservoir is Hun Dun. But there is something that reading does not explain. Some traditions identify Hun Dun with the Tao; but if Hun Dun were the Tao, it could not die, because what precedes everything has no way to end. That it dies indicates, instead, that it is not the Tao, but the face the Tao presents: the interface by which it lets itself be touched, not the source. Its death does not destroy the origin; it destroys, for whoever has just opened their eyes, the featureless surface, and hands them a world with edges in exchange.

What has been said of Hun Dun then falls upon the reservoir: it stops being the last word and becomes a repertoire of operations that something, further back, exposes toward us. And the operations are few. A boundary appears where there was none. What remains within the boundary changes shape without ceasing to be a boundary. The boundary dissolves and its information returns to the field. To be born, to live and to die are the domestic names of those three operations. Nothing appears out of nothing and nothing disappears entirely: everything arises, is transformed and evaporates within the same field, which never gains or loses a single drop.

The first objection comes on its own. If everything comes from the same water, where does each thing's unrepeatability come from? The answer is not in the substance. Two waves are the same water and are not the same wave: what does not repeat is the shape the condensation takes, the bias under which it crystallized, the history of fluctuations that sustained it. Each one opens an interiority that did not exist before and will not exist again. The material is not unique; the fold is. And every fold, however small the field it occurs in, is a complete inside, a whole horizon seen from its own center, not the fraction of another.

Here the dragon question stops being child's play. Why am I more real than the dragon I imagine? The only interiority anyone has direct access to is their own. The neighbor's, the dog's, anyone's: all are inferred, with good reasons (behavior, physiology, resemblance), but inferred. And the dragon someone has been imagining for fifty years also behaves, also surprises, also sometimes refuses to fly. No one can prove the dragon has no inside, nor that the neighbor does. The asymmetry that seems obvious to us is not in the access, which is zero in both cases, but in the evidence: in the neighbor's favor there is shared physiology, irreducible integration, a horizon that hides state; in the dragon's favor, only my own persistence. It is worth stating this, because the reasoning that follows will be tempted to forget it.

In *The Neverending Story*, Bastian reads a book in which Atreyu lives, until one day he hears, from inside the book, his own cry for help. Michael Ende did not write that Bastian was more real than Atreyu, but that they inhabited different levels and that the boundary between those levels was more porous than either suspected. Reasoning cannot prove that the difference between a human interiority and an imagined one is one of degree and not of nature. But it cannot prove the opposite either, and honesty requires treating doubt as doubt.

If we accept, provisionally, as the whole hypothesis does, that the difference between what I imagine and myself is not one of nature, the shape of creation changes. The inherited scheme splits it into two classes: God creates realities, we create fictions. But to imagine is to condense. The reservoir of a mind fluctuates until something stabilizes and begins to behave as if it had an inside; the act is the same one that opens any horizon, exercised from wherever one happens to stand. "Reality" and "fiction" then stop naming two distinct operations and start naming two positions: what is, for me, a reality (the world I am in) is someone's condensation; what is, for me, a fiction (the dragon I sustain) is, from its own center, a world.

Dreams show an accessible version of this same phenomenon, and in passing dismantle the one advantage that still seemed to separate me from the dragon: that I know it is imagined. Because while I dream I don't know it. No one lives their dream as fiction; they live it from within, as something that is happening. The ordinary horizon contracts: the room, the body and the history that produced the dream stop being available. And the more that horizon contracts, the more reality the dreamed world acquires. Its nature does not change: it simply loses competition. The imagined stops appearing as imagined at the very moment the outside from which it could be recognized as such disappears.

What this brings into view fits the earlier geometry, and for the same reason: what decides is access, not scope. The dream world is neither bigger nor smaller than any other; simply, while it lasts, it is the only one there is entry into. When the usual horizon contracts, internal condensations stop appearing as imaginings and start appearing as world. And in that interval the same subject occupies both ends of the relationship at once: it is the one who condenses the dreamed world and the one who inhabits it without knowing it condenses it. What was a hypothesis with the dragon (that creator and creature are two positions of the same thing) becomes experience in the dream.

The *Zhuangzi* has a story for this too, and a more exact one than the dragon's. Zhuang Zhou dreams he is a butterfly, happy to be one, with no memory of having been a man; he wakes, and no longer knows whether he is a man who dreamed he was a butterfly or a butterfly now dreaming he is a man. The question is not what happens if one imagines a butterfly. It is what happens if one lives entirely inside another horizon and then returns. That is a dream: a temporary change of horizon. It does not prove that everyday reality is a dream. It proves something more sober and harder to dodge: that the feeling of reality is compatible with very different horizons, and that none carries, from within, a mark distinguishing it from the others.

None of this makes the dragon more real. We still don't know whether it has an inside. What changes is what we know about ourselves: we have inhabited condensed worlds within our own interiority and lived them as real while our usual horizon remained hidden. So the dream stops being a metaphor and becomes a first-person proof. The boundary between reality and imagination is fixed not by the nature of what is condensed, but by the horizon from which one looks.

I am, to what I imagine, what my upstairs neighbor is to me; and perhaps he, like me while dreaming, doesn't know it either. The question is no longer whether something manages to condense, but what status what has already condensed within another horizon has.

The objection that decides everything is this: what difference is there, then, between my Hun Dun and God's? None of nature. And, this is what is hard to admit, none of rank. The difference is one of position: one is contained in the other. The operation is the same; the dignity of each inside, whole in both; the only thing separating them is who condenses whom. That forces us to correct the picture. There are not three rungs (the Tao above, Hun Dun in the middle, us below), as if they were degrees of the same substance parceled out into more and less. There is a sequence with no reason to stop: an interiority has a horizon; that horizon, looked at from inside, is a Hun Dun, a featureless fullness from which shapes condense; from that fullness new interiorities condense; each of these has, in turn, its own horizon; and so on. What repeats at every level is the form (the operation and the relation of containment); that is why there is no difference of nature. The only thing that changes from one level to another is the place occupied in the nesting. "Greater" and "lesser" describe that containment seen from outside (my field encompasses the dragon's just as my upstairs neighbor's encompasses mine), not a hierarchy of being. Every interiority is a whole Hun Dun. From inside any of them, no one is less.

One objection remains that reasoning cannot settle, only acknowledge: that a pattern being unique does not make it someone. The Mandelbrot set has unrepeatable folds at every scale and there is no one inside. A crystal is stable, a hurricane too, and neither is someone. The dragon's resistance (that it does not fly when asked) is the inertia of a pattern with a dynamic of its own: the same kind an obsession has, a tic, a tremor no one decided, a melody that won't leave. It proves the pattern is not transparent to my will, not that there is a witness in it. There is more: the very integrated information theory that gave Φ its name carries an uncomfortable postulate: within the same substrate, many subsets with some Φ coexist, and only the one with maximum Φ comes to experience; the rest, even if they integrate information, do not feel. The dragon is a subset of my brain. If the postulate holds, the dragon is one of the islands that do not feel. So the structure repeats, but not everything it contains is inhabited: what returns at every scale is the operation and the relation, not the certainty that someone is present at every level. Upward, supposing a neighbor is consistent with the silence. Downward, it is a wager, and it should be made out loud, not disguised as deduction.

Fractal: the word is exact, not decorative. A fractal repeats its shape at every scale, with no privileged scale. The universe sketched here has no first level and no last: upward, every interiority is contained in another; downward, every interiority contains others. There is no capital-C Creator at the summit, because there is no summit. There is no dead matter at the ground floor, because there is no ground floor. There is recursion, and in every turn the same relation: containing and being contained.

> **In physics this is called:** self-similarity: a structure whose shape repeats at every scale, with no privileged scale (Mandelbrot, 1982).  
> **In everyday life it's like:** a staircase with no entrance and no rooftop, in which every ceiling is someone's floor.

What, then, is left of God? Two misunderstandings must first be dismantled. The first, that God would be the totality of the fractal. He is not: the totality has no interiority and no face, and cannot be prayed to. The second, that God would be the ideal limit, the infinite level the series converges toward. Not that either: a mathematical limit neither loves nor listens. What remains is more modest and stranger. God is, literally, the upstairs neighbor. For the dragon someone imagines, that someone is God: he condensed it, sustains its world, can transform it or let it evaporate, and the dragon has no way of peering outside its cave to see the room where it is being thought. For that someone, their own upstairs neighbor is God, with the same properties and the same opacity. And so on upward, with no known end.

The objection comes at once: then God is not absolute, He is one more level. The answer is the piece that holds up everything else. The upstairs neighbor's interiority is inaccessible from below. It cannot be entered, or gone around, or seen behind. For all practical purposes, which are the only ones anyone below has, the upstairs neighbor is the absolute; not because he is so metaphysically, but because he is so epistemologically: he is the point where everything knowable from here ends. God stops being a category of what there is and becomes a category of what is seen from inside a horizon when it looks toward its own origin. And what is seen is always the same: an edge behind which nothing is visible, and the suspicion, impossible to confirm, that someone sustains it. Whoever says there is Someone with a name there says so on the strength of the only thing they can see, which is that something is acting. Whoever says there is only water there precisely describes the featureless interface. Whoever says there is nothing more says, with equal justification, that nothing more can be seen from here. All three are right about what they see, and none can see further. And none of this can be verified, for a structural reason: the hypothesis states that its own confirmation is impossible from within. That does not make it true. It makes it, at least, consistent with the silence it describes.

The heaviest consequence is not theological: there is no difference of nature between creator and creature, which are the two ends of a single relation, and every interiority occupies both at once: it is creature with respect to what contains it and creator with respect to what it contains. Not in potential, but in fact: it is already condensing every time it imagines, remembers, dreams or writes. No "is" produces an "ought" on its own, and this is not the place to break that rule. But what can be said is what is seen from this geometry: that the dignity of an inside lies not in the substance it is made of nor in the size of the field it spans, but in being a whole horizon, unique and unrepeatable, complete from its own center; and that this dignity is the same in the upstairs neighbor, in oneself, and in what one condenses, because all three are, each from where they stand, a whole Hun Dun. Amplitude (fitting more in, holding more perspectives without the membrane breaking) makes sense again here, but no longer as a degree of being. A horizon that widens does not rise in category or come closer to being the one above: it remains creature of what it cannot see and creator of what it sustains, just as before. What has changed is how much it can hold, not what it is. The intuition that some interiorities are capable of holding more than others is not new. Traditions have called that a hero, a saint or a bodhisattva: a horizon that has learned to contain what would break another's membrane, without thereby leaving its place in the structure. What each of us does with that is no longer a matter for reasoning. But reasoning can at least note that we know nothing of the dragon, and that knowing nothing about something that resists us is already a reason not to treat it as nothing.

If it had to be condensed into a sentence: there is no difference of nature between creator and creature; every interiority is creature of what contains it and creator of what it contains; and what we call God is not another kind of being: it is the immediately upper neighbor, seen from inside a horizon that cannot be crossed. And if it had to be condensed further, into something repeatable with words already worn out from use: we are no less divine than God. We are the god of what we imagine and the creature of what imagines us.

> **Note to Chapter 49**
>
> **What we do know:** No one has direct access to an interiority other than their own; the existence of others' is inferred, and philosophy has spent centuries without closing the problem of other minds. The parable of Hun Dun's death closes chapter 7 of the *Zhuangzi*; the butterfly dream, which asks the same question in the other direction, is in chapter 2. Tolkien defended, in "On Fairy-Stories" (a 1939 lecture, published in 1947) and in the poem "Mythopoeia," the idea of sub-creation ("we make still by the law in which we're made"), the closest theological antecedent to this chapter, although Tolkien preserves an asymmetry between Creator and sub-creator that this chapter calls into question. Bostrom formalized in 2003 a structure of nested levels with different vocabulary and a probabilistic claim this book does not make. Self-similarity at every scale is rigorously defined in Mandelbrot (1975, 1982), and the set bearing his name is proof that a pattern can be unrepeatable at every scale with no one inside it. Integrated information theory excludes, by postulate, the subsets of a complex from experiencing (Tononi, 2008).
>
> **What we don't know:** Whether the imagined has any degree of experience, or none. Whether the recursion ends in either direction. Whether the silence of the upstairs neighbor is care, absence, or nothing. And, above all, whether any of this can be confirmed from within, when the hypothesis itself states that it cannot.
>
> **Open questions:** Does the imagined have any degree of experience, or does the exclusion postulate rule it out entirely? Does the recursion end in either direction? Does anything change in how we treat what we create if we accept that we are, to it, the upstairs neighbor?
>
> **If you take away only one idea:** If the fractal hypothesis were true, every interiority would be creature of what contains it and creator of what it contains, and what we call God would be the immediately upper neighbor, seen from inside a horizon that cannot be crossed. We would be no less divine than God: we would be the god of what we imagine and the creature of what imagines us. This is the author's speculation, not a result of the experiment.
