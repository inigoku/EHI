## Three verbs

Let us forget chronology. Let us forget the museums, the wars, the catalogues raisonnés. The Spanish twentieth century was not fought in the trenches of the Ebro or under the bombs of Guernica: it was fought in the curved space of consciousness, there where the gravity of the self bends the light of the world until it becomes unrecognizable.

Dalí, Picasso and Miró were not painters. They were astronomers of the subjective abyss.

Each built his own event horizon: the boundary beyond which information (desire, trauma, memory) can no longer escape outward. Everything that happened to them, an executed friend, a beloved woman, a lost war, fell toward that center and was subjected to its laws. And each solved the same problem in his own way: what to do with that mass of obsessions that bends one's own biography into a singularity.

Dalí sealed it. Picasso inhabited it. Miró dissolved it.

These three verbs form a typology of destinies. Sealing, inhabiting and dissolving are the three possible responses to the weight of an inner world that threatens to swallow everything. That is why this chapter is not about painting, but about what a consciousness does with its own mass.

It is a map of those three singularities, and of what remains when someone dares to look inward.

## The outer collapse (1936–1939)

Before mapping the three systems, we must register the disturbance that passed through all three. No inner horizon is immune to a shell.

The Civil War was, for these three horizons, the same external gravitational field with three different responses. Dalí saw it coming and turned it into flesh: *Soft Construction with Boiled Beans*, painted months before the uprising, is a body tearing itself apart, premonition made anatomy. An entire country reduced to a figure strangling itself with its own hands. Afterward he dodged it: neither trench nor banner, but flight to Italy and America, and a political silence that was another way of sealing the capsule, a silence that over the years shaded into flirtation with power and that his old surrealist companions never forgave him. The war did not enter Dalí: the method digested it and gave it back transformed into a monster.

Picasso did the opposite: he threw his horizon wide open, once. *Guernica* is the moment when outside information (the bombing, the bodies, the horse) crosses the membrane without being transformed into autobiography. For once, the Picassian self stepped aside to let through the scream of others. The painting toured the world as ambassador of a dying republic and did not set foot in Spain until 1981, by which time both the dictator and the painter were dead: the information took forty-four years to return to its origin. Picasso never went back to Franco's Spain; his exile was a stable orbit around a country that by then existed only within him.

Miró answered with *The Reaper* and the poster *Aidez l'Espagne*, and then with retreat: Varengeville, Palma, the withdrawal into the minimal. His *Constellations* are born right there, while Europe burns: twenty-three small-format gouaches, easy to carry, painted by a man fleeing with his family from one war into another. It is resistance by concentration. His horizon did not close with the war: it grew finer and more taut, like a rope.

Three reconfigurations of the same blow: Dalí digests it, Picasso expels it, Miró compresses it.

## I. Salvador Dalí: encapsulation

Dalí built a bunker around his psyche.

His painting is a sealed capsule where the outside world does not enter without first submitting to the paranoiac-critical method: a machine that multiplies images, that folds reality over itself until the original and its reflection can no longer be told apart. That encapsulation is survival.

> **In physics this is called:** an event horizon. The boundary separating what can escape outward from what is trapped forever; nothing crosses it without first being transformed by the interior physics of the system.

The method has its manifesto work: the *Metamorphosis of Narcissus*. A figure crouched by the water is, at once, a self-absorbed youth and a stony hand holding an egg from which a flower sprouts. There is no trick: both readings are simultaneous and complete, and the eye cannot hold both at once. Dalí has built an image in superposition, and every viewer collapses it in their own way. Freud, who received him in London in 1938 with that painting under his arm, admitted to the young Spaniard what he denied to the rest of the Surrealists: that there was a serious psychological problem there. It was a diagnosis.

## [ILLUSTRATION il_cart_narciso: "The image that does not collapse"]
*Original illustration. A sepia-toned silhouette against a starry sky: the profile of a face bent toward the water is, at the same time and with no trick, the outline of a cracked stone vessel. Both readings share exactly the same outline, so the eye cannot hold both shapes at once.*

Faced with chaos (the war, the exile, old age), Dalí builds a self-referential universe where his phobias (madness, death, impotence) and his fixations (Gala, religion, money) orbit like planets in a closed system. The paranoiac machine works even backward through art history: for years, Dalí subjected Millet's placid *Angelus* to his method until he extracted from it a sepulchral drama (the two peasants, he claimed, were not praying over the harvest but over the grave of a dead child), and he went so far as to demand the canvas be X-rayed to confirm it. That the X-ray in fact revealed a repainted shape beneath the basket is beside the point: what matters is that, for Dalí, no image in the world was safe from being reabsorbed by his system. His own repertoire confirms it: the crutches propping up the soft flesh, the ants announcing decay, the drawers opening in torsos as if the body were a filing cabinet. None of it is decoration; it is the nomenclature of a private planetary system, stable enough to be recognized from one painting to the next across fifty years. What enters Dalí does not come back out intact: it emerges distorted, stretched, turned into the echo of a self that no longer knows where the world ends and where its own shadow begins.

### The tremor as evaporation

There is a work that anticipates this ending by decades. In 1952, after Hiroshima, Dalí returns to his most famous painting and paints the *Disintegration of the Persistence of Memory*: the soft clocks of 1931 now float above a grid of separating blocks, the whole world decomposed into discrete units that no longer touch. It is his nuclear-mystical period: matter, he has discovered, is made of particles that do not brush against each other, and he paints that discontinuity with the same fervor with which he used to paint flesh. Without knowing it, he is rehearsing his own future: twenty years later, disintegration will leave the canvas and enter his hand.

Because the degenerative tremor of his final years is his inner horizon cracking apart. Fractal analysis of his late brushstrokes detected alterations predating his clinical diagnosis; they are the self losing cohesion.

If the horizon separates the observable from the unobservable, Dalí's illness was the evaporation of his own identity: that slow, unavoidable leak physics predicts for every black hole. Information is not destroyed; it is transformed. Dalí's tremor is information disintegrating: the self radiating its matter outward in the form of unstable lines, of figures that can no longer sustain their own myth.

And yet even the evaporation was staged. Dalí did not merely suffer the tremor and old age: he incorporated them into the repertoire. The old man in a golden robe receiving the cameras, the tube in his nose displayed to the press, the theater-museum in Figueres built as a mausoleum during his own lifetime: the last Dalí turned his own decline into material for performance. The capsule was losing information, yes, but he was still deciding the angle from which the leak should be seen. It is the character's final paradox: a self disintegrating without ever giving up the direction of the scene.

### Gala: the singularity

Inside that convulsing capsule, Gala is the anchor point that prevents total collapse: the node that keeps Dalínian consciousness above the threshold of fragmentation.

It is enough to follow her transmutations to measure her mass. In *Galarina* she is serene flesh with a bared breast, painted, he said, the way Raphael painted his Madonnas. In *Atomic Leda* she is a suspended goddess, touching neither swan nor pedestal, in a world where nothing brushes against anything: love rewritten as a geometry without contact, where union no longer depends on touch but on the relative position of bodies in space. In *Corpus Hypercubus* she no longer even takes part in the scene: she watches the crucifixion from below, dressed as a lady of Toledo, turned into the witness whose gaze sustains the miracle. Over the years, Dalí even came to sign with both their names, "Gala Salvador Dalí," as if the signature itself were a binary system.

But, like every singularity, Gala is not directly observable. We do not see her in the paintings as she really is: we see her transmuted into Leda, into a virgin, into architecture. She is the invisible mass that curves Dalínian space, and she manifests only through the gravity she exerts on everything else.

> **In physics this is called:** dark matter, or a black hole itself before its first photograph: a mass never observed directly, only inferred from the curvature it imposes on everything around it.

The definitive proof arrived through absence. When Gala died, in 1982, the system was left without a center: Dalí shut himself away in the Púbol castle (which he had bought for her and which, while Gala lived, he could visit only with her written invitation), stopped eating, suffered burns in a nighttime fire and practically stopped painting. The singularity had disappeared, and the horizon, with no mass to sustain it, began its final collapse. Dalí's last seven years are not merely biography: they are the empirical confirmation of his own cosmology.

### Lorca: the retained information

The truncated friendship with Lorca is the redacted zone of the map. Lorca embodies the desire and the identity Dalí needed to bury in order to build his public character: an absent presence that returns as a blurred face in the early paintings, information trying to escape the horizon, repressed again and again by the gravity of the official self.

The Cadaqués summers of the mid-1920s were the one period when the Dalínian horizon came close to not closing. In *Honey Is Sweeter Than Blood*, the lost 1927 painting, the severed heads of Lorca and Dalí himself lie close together on the beach, among devices and elongated shadows: two beheaded, contiguous identities, neither fused nor separate. It is the document of an entanglement in the instant before its rupture. Soon after came distance, public sarcasm and Gala. And in 1936, the execution: the information remained retained forever, with no possibility of a reply.

Dalí took decades to name the obvious. At the end of his life he repeated that Lorca had been the only man ever able to fascinate him, and he confessed the poet's passion with a mix of pride and evasiveness that was, still, the old censorship at work. We cannot see Lorca directly in the mature work, but his effects disturb every Cadaqués landscape Dalí painted afterward. The curvature of that space never went flat again.

### The clocks

Dalí does not fragment for aesthetic reasons, but because his horizon is a battlefield, and multiplying images is his only way of not collapsing. His soft clocks demonstrate that time stretches with desire and shrinks with terror. He himself gave the domestic key to the prodigy: a Camembert melting one night in August. That the emblem of subjective time was born from a melting cheese is the method in its purest state, the trivial elevated to cosmology by the pressure of a mind that draws no hierarchy between panic and dinner. In his capsule, past and future coexist in a viscous present, suspended in the membrane of the horizon.

## II. Pablo Picasso: superposition

If Dalí locks himself in a high-gravity well, Picasso inhabits a prism in perpetual rotation.

Look at the two-headed portraits of the 1930s and 40s: the blonde placidity of Marie-Thérèse fused with the sharp edge and weeping of Dora Maar in a single impossible face. In *The Dream*, Marie-Thérèse sleeps with her head split into two halves that are profile and moon; in *Weeping Woman*, Dora's face breaks into green, acidic crystals around a handkerchief that is also chewed glass. Picasso does not paint women: he paints superimposed states of affect, and he forces the viewer to process love, destruction and desire in a single glance.

## [ILLUSTRATION cart_llorona: "Weeping Woman"]
*Pablo Picasso, oil on canvas, 1937. Tate, London.*

That double face is the key to his entire body of work. Picasso does not have a single horizon, but many superimposed. His self is a wave encompassing every possible state until the act of painting collapses the wave and materializes a style, a temporary identity. He himself put it, without physics, before the portrait of Gertrude Stein: when told she did not look like the mask-like face he had painted, he answered that she would. It was the conviction that the painting anticipates a state rather than recording it: that observation produces reality instead of copying it.

> **In physics this is called:** quantum superposition and the collapse of the wave function. Before measurement, a particle does not occupy a single state: it occupies all of them at once, until the act of observing forces the system to settle on one.

The first collapse has a date and a corpse. In 1901, his friend Carles Casagemas shoots himself in a Paris café over a woman. Picasso, nineteen years old, buries his friend and then paints the entire world blue for three years: beggars, the blind, wasted mothers, Casagemas himself, shrouded, with a hole in his temple. The Blue Period is the first demonstration that a trauma can tint the whole of the perceptual spectrum, that the mass of a single death can curve all the light of a system. Then came the pink (the acrobats, the itinerant tenderness) and then the axe blow: between the pink acrobats and *Les Demoiselles d'Avignon* there is no narrative transition, only a leap. The five women of the brothel on Carrer d'Avinyó, with their Iberian- and African-mask faces, do not evolve from anything before them: they erupt, the way a particle erupts where classical physics did not expect it.

## [ILLUSTRATION cart_avignon: "Les Demoiselles d'Avignon"]
*Pablo Picasso, oil on canvas, 1907. The Museum of Modern Art, New York.*

Changing style was, for him, a vital necessity. Each period is a capsule he inhabits with a predator's voracity; once he has exhausted its information, he abandons it and founds a new universe with new laws. Unlike Dalí, Picasso does not suffer the capsule: he devours it.

Cubism takes this logic to its limit. Painting a face from the front and in profile at once is a topology of consciousness, one that perceives every perspective integrated in the same instant: the maximum integration of information that fits into two dimensions, the highest Φ ever captured on a canvas. And it has two phases that are two inverse operations: analytic cubism grinds down the violin, the bottle, the face, until they are reduced to a dust of ochre planes where the object almost disappears; synthetic cubism reconstructs them with scraps of newspaper and oilcloth, with fragments of the real world glued to the canvas. First it disintegrates information; then it proves it can reintegrate it with foreign matter. A complete cycle of destruction and recomposition of the world, executed in under a decade.

At the center of all his orbits lies a constant mass: the minotaur. He appears in the engravings of the 1930s as rapist, as victim, as a blind beast led by a girl with a dove. He is the self-portrait Picasso could not make head-on: desire as a domestic monster, guilty and moving at once. His time does not move in a straight line: it orbits, cyclically, around the minotaur, around sex and death.

And in the end, the method becomes explicit. In 1957, Picasso shuts himself away and paints fifty-eight variations on Velázquez's *Las Meninas*: the same painting again and again, decomposed, recombined, inverted, reduced to sign. He does not copy: he systematically works through the possibilities of a single image, as one works through, one by one, the solutions of an equation. Superposition has stopped being a trait of his painting and become his program. That he chose precisely *Las Meninas*, the painting in which a painter paints himself looking at whoever is looking, is no accident: it is the old predator recognizing, in Velázquez's mirror, the problem of the observer his own work had been posing for half a century.

## [ILLUSTRATION cart_meninas: "Las Meninas"]
*Diego Velázquez, oil on canvas, 1656. Museo del Prado, Madrid. The original painting Picasso took apart in 1957 into fifty-eight variations.*

His fragmentation, far from Dalí's viscous panic, is a surgical operation. He dismantles the figure to understand its skeleton. And so on to the last day: the old musketeers and painters of his final years, resolved in urgent brushstrokes by a ninety-year-old who no longer corrects, are the wave refusing to fully collapse: a self that would rather go on being all its possibilities than accept a final position.

## III. Joan Miró: dissolution

Miró is the anomaly of the system.

While Dalí suffers his horizon and Picasso analyzes it to exhaustion, Miró dissolves the boundaries, but not the self. The anomaly should be stated precisely: Miró does not annihilate himself into the cosmic; he expands into the cosmic without ceasing to be a self. The proof is that his universe turns out to be unmistakable: no one signs the void the way he does. His journey toward abstraction is the dilation of a subject up to sidereal scale. By abandoning the weight of figuration, he tears down the walls of the capsule and turns the canvas into a firmament where the stars are thoughts and the lines are breathing.

The starting point is the earth, not the sky. *The Farm*, painted over nine obsessive months between Mont-roig and Paris, contains the entire family farm: every crack in the wall, every leaf of the eucalyptus, the snail, the watering can, the goat. It is realism taken to an impossible density, an inventory in which nothing outweighs anything else. Hemingway, who bought it, said it held everything one feels for Spain when far away. It is already there, in figurative code, the program of an entire life: the ant is worth as much as the star. All that remained was to let go of the ballast.

He let go of it violently. "I want to assassinate painting," he declared around 1927, and he meant it: against inherited craft, against the easel painting as an object of possession, Miró undertook a systematic demolition of everything painting had of property. What remained after the murder was an alphabet. Star, woman, bird, eye, ladder. Signs that no longer represent things, but summon them.

The process, moreover, had its own thermodynamics. Miró worked like a farmer: he rose early, kept regular hours, let canvases rest for months and even years before giving them their final mark, cultivated dozens of paintings at once like someone tending an orchard. The spontaneity of his signs is a mirage: every stroke that seems to have fallen from the sky was awaited with geological patience. It is Dalí's discipline inverted: where the man from Empordà manufactured delirium with a watchmaker's precision, the Catalan manufactured precision with the appearance of delirium.

Here the black hole metaphor reaches its limit and inverts: Miró's horizon does not trap: it lets light through. There is no mass of trauma curving space around it. His consciousness travels light, without capturing the light of others.

That inventory does not disappear when Miró abandons figuration; it is translated. The same gaze that in *The Farm* gave equal care to the snail, the goat and the mountain reappears, twenty years later, in the *Constellations*, only now the snail has become a dot and the mountain a line tying that dot to a star. The principle (nothing weighs more than anything else) holds; what changes is the scale at which it applies: from a farmyard in the Camp de Tarragona to the entire cosmos. Between one painting and the other there is no rupture, only the same equation, solved first for the small and then for the infinite.

His *Constellations* encapsulate infinity in a matchbox: bubbles where gravity does not exist and the elements float in a vibrant void. That encapsulation is not defensive, as in Dalí, nor analytic, as in Picasso, but uterine. The painting is a womb where the cosmos folds back in order to be born again.

And his entanglement is horizontal, democratic, total. His fine black lines tie a star to a woman, the woman to a bird, the bird to a primordial stain. There is no hierarchy: an ant from *The Farm* is entangled with the most distant star. If Dalí entangles to survive and Picasso to possess, Miró entangles to celebrate.

> **In physics this is called:** multipartite entanglement. Not a bond between two particles, but a network where measuring the state of any one node instantly alters that of all the others, with no hierarchical center.

But let no one mistake lightness for anesthesia. The pain of the war is in his lines, even if as contained tension: in the black of *The Reaper*, in the violence of the wild paintings of the 1930s, in the taut stroke of a set of *Constellations* painted while fleeing. The rope that ties the star to the woman is stretched by history; it simply does not break.

Miró's old age is the exact reverse of Dalí's. Where one disintegrated against his will, the other emptied himself by his own choice. The three canvases of *Blue I, II and III* are almost bare fields: a trail of dots, a red line, an expanse of blue that Miró himself compared to the preparation of a rite. It had taken him a whole lifetime to earn the right to paint almost nothing. And when the world wanted to turn him into a classic, he answered by burning and slashing his own canvases (the *burnt canvases* of 1973, exhibited with the burns on display): at eighty, the lightest of the three still refused to let his universe become heritage. Dissolution, too, has to be defended.

He also destroys the arrow of time. His time is that of wonder, where a minute before an insect lasts an eon; that of nature, bound to the cycles of the moon and the tides. His forms do not age because they inhabit a continuous present, stripped of the weight of history.

## IV. The three-body problem

These three systems did not orbit in isolation: they disturbed one another.

> **In physics this is called:** the three-body problem. In celestial mechanics, three masses mutually attracting each other have no closed-form solution: each trajectory is permanently altered by the presence of the other two, and none goes back to orbiting alone.

Dalí needed Picasso the way one needs a gravitational field to measure oneself against. He visited him in Paris in 1926, before the Louvre ("I have come to see you before the Louvre"; "You did quite right"), and never stopped orbiting him after that. It was Picasso who paid for his first passage to America, the trip that launched him to fame; Dalí repaid it for decades by sending postcards and tributes that the man from Málaga, a communist offended by the other's open Francoism, never answered. A perfect entanglement in its asymmetry: Dalí could not stop emitting; Picasso had no need to receive. Decades later, the man from Empordà was still defining himself by contrast: "Picasso is Spanish, so am I. Picasso is a genius, so am I. Picasso is a communist, I am not." Dalínian encapsulation was built, in part, as a shield against that greater mass: to avoid being absorbed by the Picassian field, Dalí had to relentlessly increase the density of his own myth.

Picasso and Miró, by contrast, became entangled without collision. The man from Málaga protected the young Miró in 1920s Paris and kept his *Self-Portrait* all his life. Two systems exchanging information without deforming each other, perhaps because they were not competing for the same region of space: Picasso possessed the body; Miró, the firmament.

And between Miró and Dalí, the initial debt: it was Miró who vouched for the young man from Figueres before the Paris Surrealist circle, who wrote recommending him. The gifted disciple ended up expelled from the group; the guarantor, expanded into his own cosmos. When Breton turned Dalí's name into an accusatory anagram, *Avida Dollars*, Miró said nothing, as he almost always did: his way of not being dragged along by another's gravity. The web joining them was never symmetrical.

Three bodies, three unequal masses, a single space curved by all three at once. None of the three horizons can be understood without the gravity of the other two.

## V. The viewer

A third element is missing from this mechanics: the observer.

Before being looked at, these paintings exist in pure potentiality. In the Dalínian delirium, the form is face, landscape and threat all at once. In the cubist fracture, the plane is front, profile, violin and void. In the Mironian cosmos, the stroke is eye, star and seed. Until the viewer's gaze lands and supplies the structure of their own inner horizon, the work does not collapse into experience.

Velázquez knew this before physics did: in *Las Meninas*, the painter looks at us from inside the painting, and the painting he is painting, the one we will never see, is us. Picasso devoted fifty-eight canvases to that trap. Dalí built double images that do not exist until an eye decides which of the two to see. Miró left so much emptiness in his last canvases that the viewer has no choice but to inhabit it. All three, by opposite paths, reached the same conclusion: the painting is not finished in the studio. It is finished in each gaze, and differently in each one.

And the viewer does not measure aseptically: they interfere. By observing, we do not merely resolve the painting; we alter our own. Dalínian neurosis calibrates our fear. Picassian fracture restructures our gaze. Mironian lightness expands our wonder.

The painting does not show us what they saw: it shows us what we are capable of sustaining when someone gives us the coordinates of the abyss.

## VI. Conclusion: what remains

Dalí taught us that encapsulation can be a prison, that entanglement is a life preserver that suffocates, and that time melts under the weight of terror. His work is the diary of an inner collapse that constantly leaked the radiation of his own fears. And his ending (the empty castle, the tremor, the still-directed scene) shows that even disintegration can be a work of art.

Picasso showed us that we can encapsulate ourselves in successive dimensions, that the entanglement of passions creates the very structure of the real, and that the self is a field of superimposed forces. He died painting: the wave still poised against a final position.

Miró revealed to us that encapsulation is not mandatory, that total entanglement dissolves anguish, and that subjective time can be an eternal dawn. His work is proof that we can expand the horizon until it stops being a boundary, and float in the fabric of the universe without fragmenting.

Three answers to the same problem, and none transferable. You cannot choose your own horizon the way you choose a style: the mass of each biography decides for us. What can be chosen, and this is what all three prove, is what to do with the curvature. Seal it and turn it into theater. Inhabit it and turn it into method. Dissolve it and turn it into a firmament.

Faced with the devastation of a century of gunpowder, these three men left us not oils, but cartographies of consciousness. And one last unresolved question: who observes the observer? Who collapses these three demiurges?

The answer lies in the one who remains. In the one reading these pages and feeling that the gravity of their own horizon has fluctuated; that an invisible line has just tied their memory to a star; that a soft clock marks a time that no longer belongs to Chronos.

The observer is not outside the system. The observer is the system.

> **What we do know:** Fractal analysis of Dalí's late brushstrokes (Forsythe, Williams and Reilly, 2017) detected alterations preceding his clinical diagnosis, although the diagnosis itself (parkinsonian syndrome rather than confirmed Parkinson's) remains debated. The chronology of the three painters is extensively documented: the paranoiac-critical method, the genesis of the soft clocks and the relationship with Gala (Dalí, *The Diary of a Genius*, 1964), the truncated friendship with Lorca and the meeting with Freud in London in 1938 (Gibson, 1997), Picasso's periods and the Casagemas episode (Richardson, 1991–2007), and Miró's statements about the universal within the intimate and the "assassination of painting" (Miró, *Selected Writings and Interviews*, 1978).
>
> **What we don't know:** Whether the event-horizon metaphor adds anything beyond geometry to an artist's biography, or whether it is just an elegant way of naming what art criticism already knew how to say in other words. Whether integrated information theory (Tononi and Koch, 2015) can be applied, even loosely, to a pictorial style and not only to a brain. A black hole, according to the information paradox (Hawking, 1988; Bekenstein, 1973), threatens to destroy what falls into it; the work of art does the opposite (it transforms information into perceptible structure, never annihilating it), and that asymmetry is what this chapter never quite resolves.
>
> **Open questions:** Does the reader of these pages seal, inhabit or dissolve their own horizon? Is it possible to choose one's own answer, or has the mass of each person's biography already decided it in advance? And if the viewer is never external to the system they observe, what part of these three painters do we go on carrying, without knowing it, every time we look at one of their paintings?
>
> **If you take away only one idea:** There is no single correct way to survive one's own inner mass. Sealing it, inhabiting it or dissolving it are three equally valid answers to the same problem, and none is transferable from one biography to another. The only thing that truly matters is not pretending there is no curvature.
