## [ILLUSTRATION 18: "The limit of the experiment"]
*An old map unfolded. Territory drawn with precision at the center. At the edges, the territory dissolves into blank paper. A compass opened to its widest span. A handwritten note: "The map is not the territory"*

---

Every thought experiment eventually finds its limit, not because imagination runs out, but because the question it asks demands tools the experiment does not have.

Not knowing something yet is not the same as knowing that something cannot be known from where we stand. The first case is an invitation: forge ahead, invent new instruments, wait. The second is a glass wall: you can see it, describe it, feel its cold against your forehead, but crossing it would require ceasing to be who you are. It would require a point of view the universe, in its current architecture, does not seem to offer creatures like us.

This chapter is an honest description of the points at which the horizon hypothesis stops being useful, contradicts itself, or simply has nothing to say.

Recognizing the limits of a tool does not weaken it; on the contrary, it is the only way to use it without breaking it. A surgeon who does not know where his scalpel ends and the optic nerve begins is not bold, but dangerous. A thinker who does not know where their conceptual framework ends and mystery begins is lost.

Epistemological honesty, knowing what we don't know, is the last form of care we owe an idea we have followed throughout this book.

### First: scale, or why this is a map and not an engine

Between the scale at which quantum gravity operates and that of a neuron there are about thirty orders of magnitude.

That distance is a gap between worlds.

Quantum physics operates at scales where spacetime itself may not be continuous, where information behaves like geometry and geometry like information, where a particle can be in superposition before the universe's clock ticks even once. Neuroscience operates at scales where body temperature undoes most superpositions in much less than a microsecond, where millions of sodium and potassium ions cross membranes like the crowd at a train station, and where humidity, thermal noise and the complexity of metabolism cause most relevant quantum phenomena to dissipate before a single synaptic signal completes its journey.

This does not invalidate the experiment, but it bounds it.

Think of it as a map. A road map does not tell you what trees line each curve, whether the asphalt is slippery this morning, or what song is playing on the radio of the car passing you. But it lets you plan the trip: it tells you that if you take the northern highway for a hundred and fifty kilometers, you will reach the port where the ferry waits. The map is not the journey, or the ferry, or the sea breeze that will hit your face when you climb onto the deck. But without the map, you might never have left home.

The horizon hypothesis is a formal map: a structure that organizes questions and suggests connections between domains that don't usually speak to each other. It is not a causal mechanism: it does not predict which neuron will fire or when, it does not allow intervention in a brain to modify its "Hawking temperature," and it offers no experimental route for detecting a "consciousness horizon" on a functional scanner. There is, as far as we know, no measurable signal on an electroencephalogram that directly corresponds to the curvature of an information horizon.

David Hume, in his *Enquiry Concerning Human Understanding* (1748), had already seen this with a clarity contemporary neuroscience sometimes forgets: we never perceive the very fabric of causality, only the constant conjunction of events that follow one another. There is no visible thread joining cause and effect, only the reliable repetition of A preceding B. In the same way, there is no observable bridge between the electrical discharge of biological tissue and the private texture of a thought. The horizon hypothesis does not claim to find that missing thread, but, like every formal map, to show us the geometry of the room we are locked in, not the mechanism that turns on the light.

This may seem like a weakness. But remember that Shannon's information theory does not predict which transistor of a computer will heat up at what moment either, and yet it has made the internet possible. The theory of evolution does not predict which fly will land on which flower at three in the afternoon, and yet it explains why flowers and flies exist. A framework can be immensely useful without being immediately operational.

It serves as a map, not as a tool, and that is fine. Not every map has to be a key, and not all understanding has to translate into control. Sometimes, understanding that two territories that seemed separate are joined by a bridge is progress enough for a generation of thought.

### Second: the hard problem, or why we don't know why we feel

The hypothesis can talk about geometry, information, entanglement, gradients of entropy rising and falling like the tide. But it cannot talk about **what it is like** to be that geometry.

It cannot say why the pain of an ingrown nail feels like a stab and not like the color blue, why dark chocolate is a delight on some people's tongues and pure bitterness on others', or why a sunset produces in some a warm expansion in the chest and in others a dizzying anguish at the passage of time.

That is the "hard problem of consciousness": why there is subjective experience. Why we are not machines processing information with no one "inside." Why there is someone watching. Why the universe is not a film projected with no theater and no one to watch it.

Imagine an empty room. You can describe its dimensions, the color of its walls, the air temperature, the acoustics. You can predict how sound will bounce if someone bangs a pot and model the air current if a window opens. But none of that tells you why there is someone inside, sitting in the armchair, watching the light coming through the gap in the curtains. Physics can describe the room with exquisite mathematical precision, but it cannot say why there is a witness.

The experiment does not solve this, and no current theory does. We can talk about integration of information, reentrant causal loops, gamma oscillations, predictive networks minimizing free energy. All of that describes the room; none of those concepts explains why there is someone sitting in it.

It should be said plainly: the horizon hypothesis **does not explain the hard problem**. It is a framework for talking about its correlates with a richer vocabulary: a better-lit room, with better curtains and a more precise thermostat, but still with no answer to the fundamental question.

The hard problem remains there, intact, at the center of everything, like a stone in the shoe of Western thought; like the question every child asks at some point ("why am I me?") and that most adults learn to forget, not because they found the answer, but because they discovered the question unsettles more than any answer reassures.

#### The reformulation of the problem and Hume's "bundle"

This is where the encapsulation perspective proposes a reformulation, not a solution.

We already saw, in the interlude, Hume's finding: searching for himself, he never found a permanent subject, only a bundle of shifting perceptions, a theater actors enter and exit.

The encapsulation model (Chapter 3) picks up Hume's intuition, but describes its geometry. The self is not a mysterious substance hidden inside the head, but neither is it a simple, unmoored fiction: it is the *inside* generated when the bundle of perceptions couples and integrates with enough strength (with a high Φ) within a boundary.

This topology allows us to reformulate Chalmers's hard problem. The question is no longer "how does physical matter produce the intangible quality of mind?", a formulation that presupposes an unbridgeable dualism of substances, but a question about accessibility: "how do certain configurations of matter establish boundaries of encapsulation, asymmetries of access?"

Subjectivity is what an encapsulated, integrated informational domain *is* when accessed from its own internal perspective. Hume was right not to find a permanent substance when he looked inside the room: there is no "spectator" separate from the show. What there is, is the system's private state (the bundle of perceptions) protected by the boundary of the horizon. This does not solve the hard problem, as said above; it displaces it. If the reformulation is correct, part of the gap between mind and matter would be a consequence of the architecture of encapsulation: the universe admits regions with asymmetric read access. What remains unexplained is why one of those accesses, the inside one, feels like something.

### Third: ethics, or why this book cannot tell you what to do

The hypothesis can describe what happens when a horizon condenses, becomes entangled or evaporates. It can describe the geometry of the bond, the asymmetry of loss, the gradient toward death. What it cannot say is what **should** happen.

Is it better for a horizon to condense or not to? Is it better for two horizons to become entangled or to stay separate? Is it better for a horizon to evaporate slowly or all at once? Is it morally acceptable to "switch off" an artificial horizon, should it ever have one? Should we respect the limits of a deteriorating horizon, or do we have a duty to intervene?

Hume, in the eighteenth century, noticed something uncomfortable: no logical argument allows an "ought" to be derived from an "is." You can observe that human beings suffer when deprived of sleep: that is an "is." But it does not follow that you "should" let your roommate sleep. That "should" comes from somewhere else: from your values, your culture, your capacity to imagine another's suffering as your own.

The experiment has no answer for this. It only offers structural information: here there is a horizon, here there isn't; here there are two sharing geometry; here there is one contracting. What to do with that information belongs to other domains: ethics, politics, law, personal choice, the human conversation that never ends.

The experiment is not neutral (no conceptual framework is), but its bias is of a specific kind: it tends to **homogenize** what it describes. If every system meeting Chapter 4's three criteria has some degree of experience, however small its Φ, the difference between a worm and a human being is one of degree, not of nature. That can be liberating (an empathy extending to every form of sentience) or terrifying: what do we eat, on whom do we test medicines, which AI systems do we switch off without asking ourselves whether someone, at some level, "feels" the cut?

The experiment does not resolve that tension: it puts it on the table and forces you to look at it. And sometimes looking at an unresolved tension is more honest than offering an easy answer you haven't earned.

### Fourth: death, or why physics does not promise immortality

The hypothesis considers three possibilities for what happens when a horizon evaporates:

1. **Remnant**: the information persists, compressed into something microscopic, a grain of data sand that in principle contains everything it was, but that no computer in the universe could ever read, because reading it would require more energy than exists in the entire cosmos.
2. **Trace**: the information spreads through the field; it is mathematically present, but inaccessible in practice, like the print of a hand in clay that is later fired: the shape is still there, but you can no longer put your fingers in it. It is information with no possibility of reconstitution.
3. **Nothing**: complete evaporation, total return to the reservoir. The information does not persist in any recognizable form. It dissolves like a lump of sugar in tea: the sugar is still there in some chemical sense, but the lump, as a shape, as an identity, has ceased to exist.

Physics does not know which of the three is correct. And, more importantly, **none of them answers the question that really matters**. The question is not "where does the information end up?" but "am I that information?" And the horizon hypothesis, however precise it is in describing the information, cannot say whether identity, the "I" that experiences, is equivalent to the configuration of that information or arises from it in a way that cannot be reduced to it.

If the information of my life is compressed into a remnant the size of the Planck length, am I that remnant, or something more than the sum of my data? If my trace persists in the field, am I that trace, or am I the process that left it? And processes, by definition, are not conserved: only their traces are. If I dissolve entirely into the reservoir, am I the reservoir, or the wave that breaks against the shore and never returns to the ocean as a wave, only as anonymous water?

Buddhism would say the question is badly posed: there is no "I" that persists or dissolves, only a process of conditioning that, on ceasing, frees what was never chained. Vedanta would say the "I" was never distinct from the reservoir. Taoism would say the question already assumes there was something to ask about: the "I" is like the silence between two notes, not something that exists, but something that happens when the conditions are right.

Physics says it doesn't know. And in that "I don't know," in that unadorned honesty, the experiment, at its limit, agrees with these three traditions: not in the answer, but in recognizing that the question exceeds its tools.

### Fifth: love, or why this book cannot explain why this person and not another

The hypothesis can describe entanglement as shared geometry, recognition as a reorganization of informational architecture, and loss as an asymmetry that persists when one pole no longer responds. But it cannot explain **why this horizon and not another**, why this person and not that one, why love is always, at some level, contingency and unrepeatability.

Two horizons can have the same Φ, the same temperature, the same history of previous entanglements and the same capacity to integrate information, and yet one finds in the other something found in no one else: a resonance not in the numbers, a music not in the notes. The horizon hypothesis has no room for that. Its vocabulary (integration, information, entropy, correlation, temperature, gradient) is too general to capture the specificity of concrete love. It can say that two horizons are entangled, but not why one entanglement is lived as destiny and another as accident, nor why someone's absence is a hole in the fabric of the world and someone else's, just an empty room that can be locked.

There the experiment touches its limit. There are things only fiction, poetry, or the silence between two people who need not speak because they already share enough geometry for words to be superfluous, can say.

### Sixth: freedom, or why we don't know whether the horizon chooses

There is a question that has circled this whole book like a bird that can't decide whether to land or keep flying: does the horizon choose, or does it only react?

If consciousness has the structure of an information horizon, everything it "thinks," "feels" or "decides" is, ultimately, a reorganization of information according to equations already written into the structure of the universe. In an equation there is no room for free will: equations do not choose, they describe.

The horizon hypothesis cannot resolve this. If we are information horizons, our "decisions" are state changes in a dynamical system. If the system is deterministic, the decisions are fixed from the start; if it is indeterministic (quantum), they contain a random component. But neither determination nor chance is "choice" in the sense in which we experience choosing: the feeling that we could have done otherwise, that the weight of the decision was real, and that we are responsible for what we do precisely because of that.

Some thinkers hold that free will is a useful illusion, a story the brain builds to coordinate social action. Others, that information, in certain configurations, generates causal properties not reducible to its parts, so that choice is real but arises from complexity rather than from fundamental physics. Others simply admit they don't know.

The horizon hypothesis aligns with that last position. It neither denies nor affirms free will. It says: "I don't know, and my vocabulary has no tools for knowing."

Sometimes, "I don't know" is the most intelligent answer possible.

### Seventh: the collective, or why we don't know whether there is a horizon that is all of us

Throughout the book we have talked about individual horizons: yours, mine, that of someone with Alzheimer's, that of a companion animal, that of an artificial intelligence. But what if there are horizons that are not individual? What if all of humanity, an ecosystem, or a city on a summer night constitute an information horizon with an experience we, as parts, have no access to?

The horizon hypothesis cannot answer this. IIT suggests that if two systems interact such that the integrated information of the whole is greater than the sum of its parts, the whole could have experience (although, as we saw in Chapter 22, its exclusion postulate reserves experience for the level of maximal integration: if the whole had it, the parts would stop having it as separate subjects). But how is the Φ of a city, a forest or the internet measured? And, if that Φ exists, what does it feel like to be a city? Is anything felt at all, or is it felt like we feel, only slower, vaster, more incomprehensible?

It is possible there are collective horizons that contain us, just as our individual horizons contain the bacteria in our gut or the rhythms of the heart. It is possible we are neurons of a brain we cannot imagine. It is possible the Earth has a kind of experience that has no name in any human language.

The hypothesis can neither confirm nor rule this out; it leaves the door open. And sometimes an open door is more honest than a closed answer.
