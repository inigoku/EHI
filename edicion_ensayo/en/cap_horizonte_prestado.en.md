The previous chapter left an uncomfortable question open. When a horizon bifurcates, as happens to Tony Stark in *Avengers: Endgame*, the two branches share the same history up to the instant of division, and afterward neither can exclusively claim the identity they shared. It is, all things considered, a clean separation: two new horizons, neither with a claim on the other, each free to keep integrating its own history without the other disputing it.

There are worse ways to spoil a trip to the past. Three stories take the same question (what happens to the inner horizon when someone negotiates with time) into territory the wormhole and multiverse physics of the previous chapter cannot cover: what happens when the arriving horizon does not find an empty body waiting for it, but one already occupied by another; what happens when a horizon stops integrating its history in order and begins perceiving it whole, all at once, as a geography instead of a sequence; and what happens when someone believes they can edit other people's time without that act costing anything to their own.

---

### 1. *All Our Wrong Todays*: the grafted horizon

Elan Mastai imagines, in *All Our Wrong Todays* (2017), a third way of spoiling a trip to the past, different from the two that closed the previous chapter. Tom Barren lives in 2016, but not ours: a technological utopia that began in 1965, the day a physicist named Lionel Goettreider switched on a clean, unlimited energy generator. Half a century later, humanity has flying cars and has cured cancer. Tom's father, obsessed with proving that time can also be traveled, builds a machine and sends his son to witness the founding instant live: the dawn of July 11, 1965, the exact second the reactor first switches on.

Tom arrives, and, through a stupid accident that distracts the physicist at the decisive moment, the generator never switches on as it should. He returns to the machine and comes back to an unrecognizable 2016: ours, the one with fossil fuels and incurable cancer, a world that is, for him, literally the broken version of history.

What is interesting for this book is what happens to Tom's horizon on landing. He does not find an empty world waiting for him, but a body. In this timeline there is another him, John, with an entire life already lived: a mother who died in the utopia and is still alive here, a sister, a partner Tom never knew. Tom does not arrive as Stark's clean bifurcation, two horizons separating from a common point, but as a graft: a complete horizon, with a whole life of memories of a history that no longer exists, loaded onto an interface (a body, a family, an entire biography) belonging to someone else. The two do not share an origin; they share, against all logic, the same body.

It is worth pausing on a detail the novel does not underline, but which says more than any of its plot twists: Tom gradually learns John's gestures. How he pours his coffee, which side of the bed he sleeps on, the nickname he uses with his sister. It is another's horizon filtering, gesture by gesture, into his own, until it is no longer clear where imitation ends and substitution begins. No one decides that transition: it happens, the way anyone who spends long enough inside a life they did not fully choose (a job, a city, an inherited relationship) ends up speaking, without noticing, in the voice of the role they occupy.

The novel does not grant Tom the consolation of a clean, separate new branch, as it did Stark, and denies him even that of a body of his own. It forces him to ask himself, chapter after chapter, whether he is Tom living John's life or whether he has already become, without noticing, John with someone else's memories. The book's answer is less a physical solution than a warning: perhaps there is no difference between fully inhabiting another's horizon and simply being someone else.

### 2. *Slaughterhouse-Five*: the whole millipede

Kurt Vonnegut poses, in *Slaughterhouse-Five* (1969), this chapter's most extreme case: not a horizon that travels through time, but one time stops happening to in order. Billy Pilgrim, an optometrist by trade and a survivor of the bombing of Dresden (the same one Vonnegut suffered, a prisoner of war in that city the night of the attack), "has come unstuck in time": he jumps without warning or control among his childhood, his wedding, the war, his old age, and his own death, which he knows in advance because he has already visited it, none of these jumps being travel in the sense of the previous chapter's readings. There is no machine, no loop to close, no boundary to yield; only a horizon that has lost the sequence.

The Tralfamadorians, the alien species that according to Billy abducts him and explains his own condition to him, perceive all four dimensions at once: for them, a human being is a creature elongated like a millipede, with baby legs at one end and old-man legs at the other, whole and present all at once. Death, for them, is a bad moment among many others, most of them good, and that moment continues to exist, intact, in its place on the millipede, even though the human observer can no longer visit it from any other point in the sequence. Hence the line Vonnegut repeats, without irony, every time someone dies in the novel: *so it goes*.

This book has argued from the start that time is a consequence of how a horizon integrates: the rate of integration determines subjective density. The Tralfamadorians, and Billy through them, describe something that model had not yet had to consider: a horizon that integrated its entire history at once, with no sequence, would have no time at all in the sense this book has given the word, but a kind of geography.

> **In physics this is called:** the block universe. Special relativity already treats past, present and future as equally real, coordinates of a single four-dimensional structure; all the Tralfamadorians add is that someone might, in addition, perceive it whole.

And, if that were true, free will would be nothing more than the price a horizon pays for being able to look at its own millipede only inch by inch.

There is a scene Vonnegut tells almost in passing, without underlining it, and which for that very reason lingers longer than it should: Billy, now grown, tells of his alien abduction on the radio, no one believes him, and he does not argue or insist; he keeps talking with the same calm he would use to describe the weather. He is not trying to convince anyone that time is a millipede: he lives as if he already knew it, and that quiet certainty, neither euphoric nor terrified, is perhaps the most honest attitude fiction has produced before a horizon that no longer controls the order of what happens to it. *So it goes* is not resignation: it is the phrase left to someone who has stopped asking time's permission to hurt.

Billy does not choose to accept Tralfamadorian fatalism: he simply discovers he was already living inside it, every time a chapter of the novel jumps without warning from Dresden to his optometry office and back. Vonnegut, who really did survive the bombing that happens to Billy, offers neither consolation nor escape: he offers a way of naming the fact that certain instants, once they have happened, can no longer be undone, and that the only freedom available is deciding in what order, and with what attention, to visit them again.

### 3. *The End of Eternity*: the edited century

Isaac Asimov imagines, in *The End of Eternity* (1955), the opposite fantasy to Vonnegut's: instead of a horizon that loses control over the order of its own time, an entire organization, Eternity, that believes it has gained control over everyone else's. Its Technicians, like Andrew Harlan, live outside the centuries, in a corridor apart from time, from which they descend into a specific century to introduce a Reality Change: the minimal calculated intervention (an object moved, a sentence said a second earlier) enough to rewrite, without anyone inside the century ever perceiving it, everything that would come after.

Eternity does not deny the boundary condition the previous chapter spoke of: it exploits it. No Change violates a physical law; each merely chooses, among the histories physics allowed equally, the one producing the least suffering. The problem, which the novel takes almost until the end to reveal, is not technical: a horizon cannot edit other people's time without ceasing to inhabit its own. The Eternals, protected from every war, every famine and every catastrophe they themselves prevented before it happened, end up administering a human history with almost no friction and, with it, almost no reason to risk anything.

When Harlan finally discovers what centuries would exist if Eternity had never been founded, he finds a civilization that did suffer its wars and its nuclear failures, and that for that very reason reached the stars. The safety Eternity was built for turns out to be, measured at the scale of an entire species, the same closure a prolonged chronic pain can produce in a single horizon: a system protected from all novelty stops generating the novelty it needs in order to truly keep having a future.

In the end, Noÿs, the woman Harlan falls in love with, who turns out to come from the centuries Eternity cannot reach, convinces him to destroy the organization before it can ever be founded: not to save the future they share, but to give the entire species back the risk Eternity had been stealing from it, century by century, without anyone ever having voted on it.

It is worth lingering a moment longer on the book's final image, because it is the one that truly pays off the whole plot: Harlan, now outside Eternity, looking at a future no one has edited, not knowing whether it will be better or worse than the one he has just destroyed. There is no relief in that image, only, at most, the vertigo any horizon feels on ceasing to have a contingency plan for everything: the terrifying and necessary possibility that things might really go wrong. Asimov does not reward Harlan with a happy ending; he simply gives him back the right for his story, and that of everyone who comes after, to be able to fail.

It is the most uncomfortable paradox this chapter offers: the horizon that believes it has put itself beyond the reach of time, protecting its own and everyone else's from any harm, may end up being solely responsible for that time, now with nothing left to fear, also having nothing left to gain.

### 4. Three answers, one question

Set side by side, these three fictions (and the four from the previous chapter) stop looking like equivalent alternatives and line up along the same question we already asked then: what do they ask of the boundary condition?

*   **Mastai:** He does not ask it to run backward or to branch. He asks something stranger: that two complete histories, with no shared origin, coexist indefinitely on the same substrate. There is superposition without resolution.
*   **Vonnegut:** He asks nothing of the universe's entropy (the bombing of Dresden happens only once, in the order it happened), but of the horizon perceiving it: that it give up sequence and accept geography, trading free will for the ability to look at any part of the millipede whenever needed.
*   **Asimov:** He asks nothing of the physics of a single horizon. He asks something of the physics of everyone else's: that their boundary condition be editable from outside, by someone who has stopped paying the price of living inside it.

Seen this way, none of the three asks for the same thing as *Avengers* or *Interstellar* in the previous chapter: none rewrites an already-closed past or demands a physical channel with no theoretical support. Instead, they ask for three different ways of spoiling not the physics of time, but the relationship between a horizon and its own: sharing it with another without merging, losing the order in which it arrives, or ceasing to be subject to it while imposing it on everyone else.

> **Note to Chapter 48**
>
> **What we do know:** All three novels are consistent with their own internal rules and ask physics for no operation better defined than those of the previous chapter; what they contribute is not a new mechanism, but a different angle on the same question: what happens to the horizon, not the universe, when time stops behaving as expected.
>
> **What we don't know:** Whether the intuition that there is "a single continuous self" inhabiting each body is a truth about consciousness or only the habit of a horizon that has never had to share a substrate, lose sequence or fall outside the time governing it, and that has therefore never had occasion to test whether that continuity was necessary or merely customary.
>
> **Open questions:** If two complete horizons could really coexist in the same body without merging, at what moment would one of the two stop being real? Is the sequence of time a property of the world, or just the way a slowly integrating horizon needs to organize what arrives to it? And can someone truly tend to other people's time without eventually starting to stop inhabiting their own?
>
> **If you take away only one idea:** Time travel was never just a physics problem. It is, above all, a problem of whose body crosses it, in what order what it integrates arrives, and who pays the price when someone decides that another's time belongs to them too.
>
> **Further reading:** Elan Mastai, *All Our Wrong Todays* (2017); Kurt Vonnegut, *Slaughterhouse-Five* (1969); Isaac Asimov, *The End of Eternity* (1955).
