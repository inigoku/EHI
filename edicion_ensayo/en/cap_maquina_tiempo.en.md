There is a question almost every time-travel story avoids asking seriously. The usual question is whether it is logically possible; the one that really matters here is what a horizon is allowed to let cross it. Einstein's equations raise no fundamental objection: they are time-symmetric, just like Newton's or Maxwell's, and mathematically admit solutions in which past and future are interchangeable. Our thought experiment (consciousness as an encapsulated informational domain, that inner horizon we already defined in earlier chapters, which integrates inward what comes in and only lets out a transformed version), applied without cheating to time travel, delivers a more nuanced verdict than "impossible." Traveling to the future is free. Traveling to the past through a wormhole is limited to a loop that actively resists closing. And jumping between branches of a multiverse only works by borrowing, from another theory, an operation that theory does not even recognize.

There is a second question, smaller but just as demanding, that almost no critique of these stories asks: what happens to the inner horizon of the traveler. If consciousness is, as we have argued, an informational domain that integrates what comes in and does not give it back as it entered, then crossing time is not free just because the universe allows it: we also have to ask whether that particular horizon (the Time Traveller's, Stark's, Cooper's) is still the same one on the other side of the leap, or whether it is already, without anyone announcing it, a different one.

Several works help us draw that double map: H. G. Wells's *The Time Machine*, the real physics of wormholes, *Avengers: Endgame*, and *Interstellar*.

---

### 1. The symmetry that explains nothing

That the fundamental laws are time-symmetric is true and, at the same time, misleading: it makes us think the arrow of time should be somewhere in the dynamics, and it is not. As we saw with Hawking radiation and quantum *scrambling*, irreversibility lies not in the law, but in the **boundary condition**. The universe began in a state of extraordinarily low entropy (what philosophers of physics call the "past hypothesis"), and it is that initial choice, not the equations, that gives time its preferred direction. It is the same phenomenon, at another scale, we already saw in Maxwell's equations: they admit retarded and advanced waves equally, but the universe only uses the retarded ones. The law allows both directions; the reservoir, on condensing, chose one.

There is, moreover, a second source of asymmetry, distinct from entropy, that does not depend at all on the boundary condition: even a perfectly deterministic universe, with its entropy already fixed, can deny you the future by another route. In the late nineteenth century, Henri Poincaré discovered that the three-body problem (predicting the motion of three objects attracting each other gravitationally, nothing more) has no exact general solution: microscopic differences in initial conditions, too small to measure fully, amplify without bound until the system becomes unpredictable in practice. The laws remained exact. What stopped being accessible was not the past, which remained determined in principle, but the future those same laws governed.

> **In physics this is called:** deterministic chaos: a system can obey fixed laws and still be unpredictable in the long run, because infinitesimal errors in the initial measurement grow exponentially.

This matters because it changes the question we ask of every time-travel fiction: no longer whether it violates relativity (it almost never does), but whether it asks the boundary condition to undo itself.

---

### 2. The beach at the end of time

Wells's Time Traveller never makes that request. His machine only moves forward: first to the year 802,701, where he finds the Eloi and the Morlocks, a society fractured by its own comfort, and then much further, to a beach under a swollen red sun, near the absolute end, where the only things moving are crab-like creatures on almost motionless sand. It is, probably, the purest heat-death scene ever written: a system that has exhausted its capacity to generate difference, a horizon with nothing new left to encapsulate.

Something happens to the Time Traveller's inner horizon on that journey that deserves to be named apart from the physics of the environment. An informational domain is defined by what it can keep absorbing: novelty, something it did not already know. In the year 802,701 that horizon still fills up: the Eloi, the Morlocks, the ruined architecture of a civilization that forgot why it built, are new data, however horrifying. But on the final beach there is nothing left to absorb: the sun barely moves and the crab-creatures always repeat the same gesture, in an air that no longer carries news of any change. If consciousness is a horizon defined by what comes in, a horizon facing an environment that has stopped generating difference begins to look, functionally, like an empty horizon: it still exists, but has no world left to feed on. The Time Traveller does not die on that beach, but, for the first time in the story, his horizon runs out of work to do.

None of this asks entropy to run backward. The Time Traveller only moves forward, faster and faster, letting the universe finish doing what it was already doing. In the end he returns to 1895 to close the story (a jump technically backward, but only to a point he himself already lived, touching nothing he had not already touched), something that is more a device of the narrative frame than a real incursion into the past. The book's philosophical content, the part that really matters for this experiment, is entirely a one-way trip.

That return deserves a little more attention than it usually gets, because it is not entirely free of causal consequences. The Time Traveller comes back almost to the same moment he left from (for those who stayed behind, barely a few hours have passed) and tells the friends gathered at his house (among them the narrator, who is the one who finally sets the story down in writing) what he saw in the year 802,701 and on the beach at the end of time. That is real information about the future reaching people who, until then, did not have it. But it is not a loop in the strong sense of the term: nothing the Time Traveller recounts alters the conditions that made his own trip possible. He had already built the machine before leaving; no one uses knowledge of the future to build the machine needed to obtain it. It is the exact difference between a story that reveals new information and a story that needs that information in order to begin existing. The second case would indeed be a loop closed on itself, and it is the ground we will walk on, without the courtesy we have shown Wells, in section 7 of this chapter.

### 3. The wormhole and the loop that resists closing

A traversable wormhole only becomes a time machine if you desynchronize its two mouths: accelerate one of them near the speed of light for a while and bring it back: the twin paradox applied to the tunnel's mouth instead of to a person. Crossing it toward the advanced side is physically identical to ordinary time dilation, and fully compatible, because your personal entropy keeps rising without exception along your own worldline. You never touch the boundary condition.

Crossing it toward the delayed side is another story, and Kip Thorne found something revealing: you can never reach a moment before the wormhole itself was built. The closed loop exists only from the instant you make it, never before. Building the machine creates a new horizon, which did not exist before, and travel to the past is possible only within that newly born horizon, never outside it.

And there is more: it is precisely this type of wormhole that led Hawking to propose the **chronology protection conjecture**. When the loop is about to close on itself, the quantum vacuum fluctuations circulating through it feed back and diverge. **It is the same phenomenon that occurs when a microphone is brought close to its own speaker: an instantaneous, exponential feedback that destroys the system.** In this model, the universe does not impose the veto by decree: the system itself becomes unstable through feedback, just as a microphone does not decide to shriek, but is left with no stable state as soon as the signal begins to feed back on itself. No guardian or defensive intention is needed: it is enough that, right at the point where the loop is about to close, physics itself dynamites the conditions that would allow it.

There is, however, another answer to the same question, earlier than Hawking's and, in a way, more generous: the one Igor Novikov had already proposed in the 1980s. If a closed loop does form, Novikov said, physics does not forbid it, as long as the result is self-consistent. The **Novikov self-consistency principle** does not require that the time machine fail to work; it requires that, if it works, the history it produces be exactly the one history that had already happened, never one that contradicts it. This is why the classic thought experiment of the billiard ball that travels back in time and strikes its own earlier version, deflecting it just enough to enter the wormhole at the right angle, always has a consistent mathematical solution: there is no version of the ball that prevents its own trip, only versions that allow it. Thorne and his Caltech collaborators spent much of the 1990s proving, case by case, that such self-consistent solutions exist even for setups deliberately designed to look paradoxical.

Novikov and Hawking, then, are not answering the same question. Novikov describes what would happen if a loop came to exist: only the consistent survives; the contradictory is not even a solution of the equations. Hawking describes whether that loop comes to exist at all: his conjecture bets that it does not, that physics itself destabilizes it before it completes. At bottom they are two ways of protecting the same boundary condition (one filtering what has already formed, the other preventing it from forming), and neither has ever been proved as a fundamental law.

And there a question remains that this chapter cannot close: is chronology protection a law on the same level as the conservation of energy, something the universe respects because its deep structure demands it, or is it an emergent property of the same kind as the arrow of time, a statistical consequence of the low-entropy boundary condition and not of any law explicitly forbidding loops? If section 1 is right and irreversibility resides in the boundary condition and not in the law, chronology protection probably does not reside in the law either: it is the same boundary condition, seen from another angle, defending itself at the only scale it knows how to. But this remains, to this day, a reasonable reading, not a theorem.

And yet, for whoever crossed it, the inner horizon would never break at any point on the journey: it is a single continuous thread of perception entering at one end and leaving at the other, with no branching or duplicate. Unlike what jumping between Everett branches demands, as we will see next, the wormhole loop, if it ever held, would ask no one to stop being who they were. At most it would ask them to coincide exactly with who they were already going to be.

### 4. The branch that isn't you

*Avengers: Endgame* resolves the grandfather paradox with real elegance: traveling to the past does not rewrite your timeline, it opens a new one. Every time someone intervenes, a distinct branch is created, and the entropy of each branch keeps rising without exception within itself. Strictly speaking, this solution does not contradict our entropy argument: no loop ever closes.

What makes it impossible is something else, bigger. For the model to really work, other branches of the universe have to physically exist, along with a real mechanism for jumping between them, and that is not a prediction of general relativity, which describes the geometry of a single spacetime, but an idea borrowed from one interpretation of quantum mechanics (Everett's "many worlds"), with no real theoretical bridge between the two. Worse still: within Everett's own formalism, the branches are not separate universes, with their own budgets of mass and energy, between which one could travel, but relative components of a single state vector that have lost coherence with one another. In that theory there is no operator that takes an object out of one branch and puts it into another. The question "is it compatible with thermodynamics to send matter to another branch?" is not even well posed: the operation has no definition within the physics supposedly sustaining it.

And there is a second loss, more intimate, which we already know from this very experiment: a horizon is defined by what has fallen inside it. When Tony Stark travels from 2023 to 2012 and the branch opens, in that branch there is already a Tony Stark, the 2012 one, who until that year shared with him the entire accumulated history. From the instant of the jump, that branch stops being the traveler's past: what he does there did not happen to him, but to another, who will go on with his own life with an interior that diverges from his. And the Tony who traveled is not rewritten on returning: his 2012 remains the one he lived. The jump does not let him fix his own past, only that of someone who, up to a certain year, was him. That is why, even if branch-jumping were physically possible, it would not achieve what it promises.

It is worth being precise about exactly what is lost, because, besides narrative continuity, what is lost is the very definition of inner horizon this book has used from the start. One horizon is distinguished from another by what has fallen inside it: by its accumulated, unrepeatable, own history. The 2012 Tony Stark of the new branch and the one arriving from 2023 share everything up to 2012; from there, each accumulates his own interior, and neither has access to the other's. Neither is "the original" nor "the copy": the question stops making sense the moment the two horizons begin to diverge. The only real thing is that in that branch there are now two horizons that were once one, and neither can exclusively claim the identity they shared.

### 5. The gravitational bridge and the five-dimensional trap

*Interstellar* fits into this map as a fourth paradigm that negotiates with the horizon in a radically different way.

*   **Dilation (Gargantua):** When the crew descends to the ocean planet, they undergo a massive trip to the future: every hour on the surface equals more than seven years for whoever stays on the ship. It shares with Wells's machine the thermodynamic verdict (Cooper's personal entropy rises without exception and his horizon is never violated), but not the mechanism. Here it is real gravitational dilation, the same physics that forces daily corrections to GPS satellite clocks; in Wells there is no physical phenomenon that explains it, only the narrative premise of a machine that "advances." Both cases come free to the boundary condition, but only one has a real mechanism behind it.
*   **The singularity (the tesseract):** On crossing the event horizon, the story respects the rule that no particle or radiation can escape from there: the horizon swallows the information, as physics demands.
*   **The masterful trap:** To send data to the past, the film postulates that gravity is the only force able to cross the higher-dimensional hyperspace and traverse time. No matter is sent backward and no one's own entropy is undone: gravity is pushed across a five-dimensional space.

And still, something in Cooper's inner horizon does pay a price during the Gargantua stretch, even if not a thermodynamic one. His accumulated history remains continuous (he never stops being himself for a single instant), but his daughter's advances without his own keeping pace. It is not a fracture of the horizon, like Stark's, but something closer to a horizon left behind by another that keeps running: the same kind of asymmetry, on a human scale, that separates a black hole from the radiation slowly emptying it.

This work's topological verdict is that it avoids asking the boundary condition to run backward in our three-dimensional universe. In exchange, it delegates the problem to higher dimensions where time is physical terrain that can be climbed, keeping the three-dimensional horizon intact. But the specific mechanism (encoding classical information in gravitational variations and reading it in the past through a tesseract) has no real theoretical framework behind it: not even brane hypotheses with gravity leaking into extra dimensions propose a channel like this. At bottom it is the same undefined operation we criticized in *Avengers*, only disguised here as a visual device instead of a multiverse.

This criticism should not be confused with a reproach to the whole film. Kip Thorne himself worked as scientific consultant and developed new equations to render Gargantua's gravitational lensing with a precision no previous special effect had achieved: that image of the black hole is real physics, not artistic license. What has no theoretical support is what the plot needs that black hole to let Cooper do.

The tesseract deserves the same inner-horizon analysis we applied to Stark, and here the result is even more demanding. Cooper does not jump to another branch: he remains, at every moment, the same continuous horizon that entered Gargantua. What the film asks of him is something stranger: that this horizon, without dividing, reach back within its own history and deposit information at a point in itself that had already happened and was already closed. It is not a bifurcation like *Avengers*'s, where at least there are two distinct horizons after the jump, but a single horizon that means to rewrite a datum it had itself already finished encapsulating. Stark's problem was that the operation of jumping between branches is undefined in the theory supposedly supporting it. Cooper's is even more basic: no known horizon, his or any other physical system's, has an operation for writing onto its own already-sealed past. Love, the film says, is the force that crosses dimensions. Physics, more humbly, can only say that no horizon rewrites itself, not even for love.

### 6. Four answers, one criterion

Set side by side, the four fictions stop looking like equivalent alternatives and line up along a single question: what do they ask of the boundary condition?

*   **Wells:** Nothing. It only moves forward, for free. His inner horizon empties itself of novelty until it runs out of work, but it never divides or breaks.
*   **The backward wormhole:** It asks the boundary to give way in a newly created loop; the universe responds with destructive quantum feedback. The horizon of whoever crossed it, had it been possible, would have remained a single continuous thread.
*   **Avengers:** It avoids asking that, but demands jumping between components of a quantum state as if they were physical places, breaking the horizon's personal continuity: one becomes two who never speak to each other again.
*   **Interstellar:** It respects the three-dimensional horizon, but asks for the same undefined operation as *Avengers*, only here it doesn't even need a second horizon: it asks a single one to write onto its own already-closed past.

Seen this way, the four answers do not only say what they ask of the universe's entropy. They say, just as clearly, what they ask of a single consciousness's entropy: to let it empty out in peace, in one piece; to split it into two that never cross again; or to ask it to correct itself from a place that does not exist.

Each fiction negotiates with the horizon in its own way. Wells is the only one that doesn't even need to negotiate: it simply moves forward.

---

### 7. When the impossible really is asked for

The four previous cases share something worth not taking for granted: none of them, not even *Avengers* in its most generous version, asks the boundary condition to truly run backward. To see where the limit lies, we need an example that does.

*Looper* (2012) does it explicitly and, to a point, faithfully to its own rules: when the future version of an assassin escapes, every wound inflicted on his young version thirty years earlier appears, in real time, on the old man's body: fingers disappearing, memories erasing. It is not a new branch opening without touching the previous one, as in *Avengers*: it is the only timeline that exists in the film, retroactively changing what had already happened to someone who was already there, living the consequences in the present. It is exactly the operation the rest of this chapter has been ruling out again and again for lacking support: information flowing from the future to the past and unambiguously rewriting a state thermodynamics had already closed. The film itself knows this doesn't hold up (a character wryly warns that if they start drawing timeline diagrams they'll fry their brains) and uses it as a narrative device, not as serious physics. But it is useful for exactly that reason: it shows, by contrast, that most time-travel fictions that do work, this chapter's four included, carefully avoid asking for anything like this, if only by narrative instinct.

*Primer* (2004) deserves a quick mention for the opposite reason: despite its reputation as the most confusing time-travel film ever made, it actually asks nothing illegitimate of the boundary condition. Its nested loops are intricate, but self-consistent: every version of every character who enters a box was already accounted for, without exception, in the single timeline that exists. No one erases anything; no one rewrites a past that had already happened differently. The sense of impossibility the film leaves comes not from physics breaking, but from a human brain not being well designed to keep track of so many self-consistent copies at once. *Looper* breaks the rule. *Primer* only complicates it to the limit of the viewer's patience.

*Tenet* (2020) deserves a more careful analysis than the previous two, because it does not ask for a causal loop or a rewriting of the past, but something more radical and, at the same time, more disguised: directly inverting the sign of an object's or a person's entropy, leaving intact the single timeline everyone exists in. "Inverted" objects and people do not travel to another moment: they experience the same instant as the rest of the world, but with their own thermodynamic arrow pointing backward, so that, seen from outside, they move backward while the universe around them keeps moving forward. The film is, moreover, scrupulously faithful to Novikov's principle: its characters repeat almost as a mantra that "what's happened, happened," and neither pincer of the final assault ever changes what was already known would happen. In this, *Tenet* is more disciplined than *Avengers* or *Interstellar*. But the price of that discipline is that it never explains where the inverted boundary condition comes from the first time: the Algorithm that allows inversion arrives from the future, built with knowledge that could only be obtained once the Algorithm itself was already working. It is exactly the originless loop Wells's return to 1895 avoided, here scaled up to an entire technology. *Tenet* does not ask the boundary condition to yield within a single life, as *Looper* does. It asks for a second boundary condition, of opposite sign, to exist, without ever saying where the first one comes from the first time it appears.

---

### 8. The horizon that is me writing this

Perhaps the reason these four answers and these three counterexamples draw us in so much is not the physics they put into play, but what they let us glimpse about the horizon of whoever is watching. Each of us is, right now, a Cooper with no ship, sealing instants we will never touch again, at a rate of twenty-four per second. There is no tesseract for the horizon that is me writing this, nor for the one that is you, reading it a while later. I don't know what to do with that thought. I leave it here, unresolved, because I'm not sure resolving it is what's called for.

> **What we don't know:** Whether some mechanism (quantum or otherwise) generally prevents a closed timelike curve from ever forming; Hawking's chronology protection conjecture is unproven.
>
> **Open questions:** If the past hypothesis explains why entropy grows, what explains why the universe began with such low entropy? Is that question different from asking why a horizon exists at all? And is chronology protection a fundamental law, or just that same boundary condition, seen from another angle?
>
> **If you take away only one idea:** Traveling to the future was never the problem (we already do it a little every time a GPS satellite corrects its clock); the problem was always asking a horizon to give back what it had already swallowed.
