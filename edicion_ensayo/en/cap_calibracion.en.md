Chapter 31 described two selections that operate on the reservoir before a horizon exists as such: one decides which configurations are stable enough to last, and the other (the bias of the immediate environment at the moment of condensation) decides which specific form that horizon takes among the stable ones. Neither asks permission, and neither can be undone.

This chapter applies that same machinery to three questions culture has almost always treated as if they were one: whom a horizon desires, who it knows itself to be, and what body it has. They are three distinct axes, and none of them is chosen or is an error of calibration.

### Orientation as bias, not choice

No one remembers deciding whom they desire. That in itself is a piece of data: decisions leave an introspective trace (the moment when the options were weighed), and sexual orientation leaves none, in any direction, for anyone. What research over recent decades has been documenting is the specific shape of that bias, without ever finding a switch.

Even before asking about its origin, it is worth asking about its shape. In 1948, Alfred Kinsey, Wardell Pomeroy and Clyde Martin proposed a seven-point scale (from 0, exclusively heterosexual, to 6, exclusively homosexual) after thousands of interviews that resisted the dichotomy the questionnaire itself had originally assumed. In 1985, Fritz Klein split that scale into seven dimensions (attraction, behavior, fantasy, emotional preference, social preference, self-identification and lifestyle), each measured in the past, the present and the ideal, because many people occupy different points on each dimension and at each stage of their lives. Neither tool is perfect (the very idea of a "scale" still assumes a single axis, when real experience may look more like a cloud of points than a line), but both agree on something that can be stated before asking about any cause: there is no reason to expect a continuous variable to resolve neatly into two categories. Culture asked for a switch; the evidence delivered a dial with seven positions and, later, seven more dials.

Bailey and Pillard's twin studies, in 1991, found that when one identical twin is gay, the other is too in around 52% of cases; among non-identical twins, the figure drops to about 22%, and among adoptive brothers with no genetic relationship, lower still. The progression (more concordance the more genome is shared) points to a real heritable component, but 52% in genetically identical twins also means that almost half the time the twin with exactly the same genome does not share the orientation. Genetics influences, but does not dictate.

In 1993, Dean Hamer believed he had located part of that influence in a region of the X chromosome called Xq28, after studying gay brothers. The finding became famous ("the gay gene," the press announced, simplifying what Hamer never claimed), but George Ebers's attempt at replication, with a different sample, did not find the same signal. The largest genome-wide association study carried out to date, that of Ganna and colleagues in 2019, with nearly 500,000 people, confirms the general shape of that first finding and refutes the detail: there is no single *locus*, but at least five regions of the genome with a weak statistical association, which together explain between 8% and 25% of the observed variation and do not allow a particular person's orientation to be predicted from their DNA. Orientation, like most complex behavioral traits, is polygenic: the bias of Chapter 31 is not written in a single gene an editor could strike out, but spread throughout the architecture, in a way no current instrument can read with individual precision.

> **In genetics this is called:** a polygenic trait with low individual predictive power: the signal is real in the population and almost useless as a prediction in a specific case.  
> **In everyday life it's like:** a person's height: we know it depends largely on their parents' genes and yet no genetic test done at birth can say exactly how tall they will be.

There is a second line of evidence, stranger and better replicated than Xq28 itself: the fraternal birth order effect. Ray Blanchard and Anthony Bogaert documented, and later studies in very different populations have confirmed, that the more older biological brothers a man has, the greater the statistical probability that he is gay, an effect that does not appear with older sisters or with adoptive or step-brothers raised in the same house without a biological relationship, which rules out purely social explanations of that particular correlation. The best-founded hypothesis is immunological: the mother's immune system might generate, pregnancy after male pregnancy, a growing response against a Y-linked protein involved in the brain development of the fetus, cumulatively altering how that development is calibrated in successive sons. It is not a settled certainty (Vilsmeier, Kossmeier, Voracek and Tran argued in 2023, combining probability calculus, simulated data and a multiverse meta-analysis, that part of the effect might be an artifact of the way samples are selected), but it points, like the genetics, to the same place: something that happens before birth, beyond the reach of any later decision, upbringing or "correction." If the mechanism is confirmed, it would not be an isolated oddity within this chapter, but one more instance of what Chapter 15 described as the mother-child bond; this time not as a metaphor, but as maternal biology directly shaping the child's architecture before any conscious interaction between the two exists.

All this evidence (Bailey and Pillard's twins, the fraternal birth order effect itself) comes overwhelmingly from gay men, and not by chance: according to the available data, fraternal birth order is not associated at all with women's orientation. Lisa Diamond followed about a hundred women for more than a decade and documented a different pattern: not an orientation fixed from birth, but attractions that shift within a range over a lifetime, tied to specific bonds more than to a single calibration of origin. This does not bring choice back in through the back door: Diamond insists that no one chooses the range of that fluctuation. What it does suggest is that the bias of Chapter 31 may operate differently depending on the system: in some horizons it fixes a single configuration from the origin; in others, it fixes a range within which the specific configuration keeps moving, without anything external touching it.

### Identity as the internal model, not the interface

Chapter 3 described encapsulation: a software object keeps an internal state that no one outside can touch directly, and the only thing visible from outside is its public interface. The body a horizon is born with (its anatomy, the sex a doctor notes on a form a few minutes after birth) is, in that vocabulary, the interface. Gender identity is the internal state: the model the horizon has of itself, which even it does not access through direct introspection, but discovers over time, as it discovers any other persistent fact about its own architecture.

That interface and internal state coincide most of the time says nothing about which of the two is real: both are. It only says that, in most systems, the bias of the environment of condensation and the bodily interface ended up aligned. When they are not, there is no error in the internal state or in the interface, but a discrepancy between two layers of the same system that, in most cases, do not even communicate directly. It is like the tag someone else sticks on a suitcase at the airport: it can be wrong without changing what is inside.

In 1995, Zhou, Hofman, Gooren and Swaab published in *Nature* the finding that the central subdivision of the bed nucleus of the stria terminalis (a brain region involved in sexual behavior, whose size differs systematically between men and women) was, in trans women, statistically indistinguishable in size from that of cisgender women, regardless of the hormones received in adulthood and of the participants' sexual orientation. It is the first structural finding of this kind, and it must be cited with the same caution the scientific community has applied to it ever since: the sample was small, replication in living subjects (rather than post mortem) is methodologically difficult, and later neuroimaging studies have produced uneven results, some in agreement and some not. What is supported, far more solidly than by a single neuroanatomical finding, is the decision of the World Health Organization itself: in 2019, gender incongruence was moved entirely out of the chapter on mental and behavioral disorders of the International Classification of Diseases (where it had been, under various names, for decades) and into a chapter on sexual health. It was still real; what changed is that the accumulated evidence no longer supported calling it a disorder.

### Sex, body and reproduction: axes that don't travel together

Chapter 32, in discussing surrogacy, in vitro fertilization and ectogenesis, already described a principle worth making explicit here: reproductive capacity, sexual anatomy, gender identity and orientation are four variables that, in the overwhelming majority of horizons, go together, and precisely for that reason most human cultures have treated them as a single thing with four names.

They are not. There are people with intersex anatomy (chromosomal, gonadal or genital variations that do not fit neatly into the two categories most administrative forms offer), and their mere existence shows that not even biological "sex," taken with the rigor biology itself demands, is strictly binary.

The dispute over how many people are intersex is itself a lesson in what it means to draw a boundary. In 2000, Anne Fausto-Sterling calculated that 1.7% of births showed some intersex variation; two years later, Leonard Sax repeated the calculation with a narrower definition (only truly discordant chromosomes and phenotype, or genitals that could not be classified as male or female) and obtained 0.018%, almost a hundred times less. The difference lies not in the data, but in whether Klinefelter syndrome or late-onset congenital adrenal hyperplasia count as "intersex," which Fausto-Sterling included and Sax did not. Neither figure lies: both depend on where one decides the category begins. It is like fever: how many people in a city "have" one changes depending on whether the cutoff is set at 37.5 °C or 38 °C, without anyone's temperature having moved.

The best-documented case of a specific intersex variation comes from a group of families in Las Salinas, in the Dominican Republic, described by the endocrinologist Julianne Imperato-McGinley in 1974. Because of an inherited deficiency of the enzyme 5-alpha-reductase, some children with XY chromosomes are born with ambiguous or female-looking external genitals; they are raised as girls (name, clothing, treatment) and, at puberty, a second wave of testosterone completes the development of penis and testes the first had left half done. In the region they were known, with a frankness medicine took decades to match, as *güevedoces*: "balls at twelve." Most of them then adopted a male identity, despite more than a decade of explicitly female upbringing. It is one of the most cited pieces of data, and one of the most uncomfortable for any theory that reduces gender identity to the upbringing received: the interface can be rewritten by social decree for years without the internal state discussed in the previous section changing with it.

That same tension had, on another continent, a tragic version. In the 1960s, the psychologist John Money argued that gender identity was essentially learned: it was enough to assign a sex in infancy, operate on the genitals to match that assignment and raise the child accordingly. David Reimer was not born intersex (he lost his penis in a botched circumcision), but Money used his case as public proof of that thesis: he was reassigned as a girl and raised as "Brenda," with the corresponding surgery and hormones. He never felt like a girl. He went back to living as a boy as soon as, in adolescence, he was told what had happened, and he took his own life at thirty-eight after a life marked by that story. The case, presented for years as a success before its real outcome was known, served in the meantime to justify thousands of genital "normalization" surgeries on intersex babies, not to address any immediate medical risk, but to make their bodies fit before they could have a say in them.

In 2015 Malta became the first country to ban by law such non-consensual surgeries on intersex minors except in cases of real medical need; Germany, Greece, Iceland, Spain and Portugal have since adopted similar restrictions, and the 2017 update of the Yogyakarta Principles, discussed below, explicitly recognizes the right to bodily integrity against such interventions. It is the same logic of translation and rejection that runs through the rest of this chapter, applied this time not to the signal a horizon emits outward, but to its own interface: someone deciding, scalpel in hand, that ambiguity could not wait until the horizon was old enough to have a say about its own body.

Returning to the other three variables from the start of this section: there are fertile and infertile people on both sides of any of them, without fertility determining anyone's identity or orientation. There are people whose gender identity does not match their anatomy and whose sexual orientation (whom they desire, not who they are) is a completely different question. Treating these four axes as if knowing one automatically settled the other three is not just imprecise: it is the same kind of mistake a doctor would make by deducing a patient's blood type from their height.

### Discrimination as a failure of translation

Every horizon needs to translate itself: to turn its inner signal into something the outside world can receive. The reading "The translator," later on, studies what happens when that translation is missing through absence; here it is missing through rejection. Discrimination against a horizon because of its orientation or identity is the active decision, sustained institutionally for centuries, not to translate a signal that arrives perfectly clear, and to declare instead that the signal itself is corrupted.

So-called conversion therapy is the best-documented case of what happens when that refusal becomes active intervention: the attempt to forcibly recalibrate a system that was never miscalibrated. The American Psychiatric Association, the World Health Organization and virtually every relevant medical and psychological association have condemned it as ineffective and harmful. The study by Turban and colleagues, published in 2019 on the basis of the largest survey of trans people ever carried out in the United States (more than 27,000 responses), found that those who recalled having been subjected to such conversion attempts were more than twice as likely to have attempted suicide in their lifetime; if the exposure had occurred before the age of ten, the likelihood was multiplied by four. The harm did not depend on whether the person applying the "therapy" was a licensed professional or a religious guide with no clinical training. The mechanism of the harm lay not in the method, but in the premise: the conviction that there was something to correct.

### Rights as recognition, not concession

In 1990 the World Health Organization removed homosexuality from its list of diseases; that day, May 17, is commemorated today as the International Day Against Homophobia, Transphobia and Biphobia. In 2006, a group of twenty-nine jurists and human rights experts from twenty-five countries met in the Indonesian city of Yogyakarta to draft principles on how existing international law (the law that protects dignity, non-discrimination, private life) applies to sexual orientation and gender identity; a 2017 update added sex characteristics and the right to bodily integrity. Neither document invented a new right: both merely declared that rights already recognized for any horizon applied to these ones too.

Perhaps this is the most precise description of what a right means in this terrain: not a concession by a majority to a minority, but the belated recognition that the translation had been owed all along. The speed of that recognition remains radically unequal between countries (in some, orientation or identity are still grounds for criminal prosecution; in others, marriage, parentage or legal gender change have been settled for decades), and that inequality gives the exact measure of how much translation is still missing through rejection, country by country.

### What this chapter does not resolve

This chapter does not fully explain any of the three variables it describes. It cannot, because neither can the science that studies them yet, and this book has tried, chapter after chapter, not to claim more than the evidence supports. What it can say, with the same precision with which Chapter 31 separated stability from bias, is this: that a horizon does not choose the way it was calibrated, just as it did not choose the reservoir from which it condensed; that the absence of a complete explanation is not evidence that something is miscalibrated, but only that the calibration is more complex than any simple explanation can capture; and that the right question, faced with a horizon whose calibration does not match the majority's, was never "why is it like this?" but the same question this book has asked, with different nuances, in almost every one of its chapters: what does this particular horizon need for its translation into the outside world to reach it whole?

> **What we don't know:** To what extent the outcome of David Reimer's case (a single case, not a controlled study, though extraordinarily well documented) can be generalized to the intersex population in whose name similar surgeries were justified for decades; where, if anywhere, the line should be drawn between intersex variation and variation within the typical, given that the classification boundary itself (Fausto-Sterling versus Sax) depends on a definitional decision and not on an additional biological fact science could yet discover; why Hamer's original finding on the Xq28 locus (1993) was not replicated in later studies with different samples; whether the fraternal birth order effect reflects entirely a prenatal immunological mechanism or whether part of it is a sampling artifact, as Vilsmeier et al. (2023) have argued; how much of Zhou et al.'s finding on the stria terminalis holds up in neuroimaging studies of living subjects, with results so far uneven; what exact fraction of the variation in any of the three variables (orientation, identity, biological sex) corresponds to prenatal factors versus later developmental factors, a question current science cannot settle with the available evidence; and how the pattern of fixed bias, documented mainly in men, relates to the pattern of fluctuation within a range that Diamond documented in women: whether they are the same mechanism seen at different amplitudes or two genuinely different mechanisms.
>
> **Open questions:** Is the near-total absence of a single determining cause, in all three variables, a temporary limit of research or a structural property of genuinely polygenic and multifactorial traits? Does the ethical or legal criterion for recognizing a right change if the underlying biological evidence changes? Can a society recognize the validity of a calibration without fully understanding its origin?
>
> **If you take away only one idea:** No horizon chose how it was calibrated. The question worth asking is never why it was calibrated that way, but what it needs for its translation into the world to arrive whole.
