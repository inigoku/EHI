The previous chapter left a question open, and this experiment owes it an answer: if the ego is the membrane of the horizon and beneath it live complexes with lives of their own (voices Jung documented negotiating, sabotaging, even speaking in the first person within a dream), who is really experiencing? The whole horizon, or each complex separately, within its own subhorizon?

Until now we have treated the ego as *the* interiority. But a system with several partially integrated subsystems offers not one interiority, but several competing for the same space. And the physics of information, when the question is put to it squarely, does not dodge it: it gives it a name. It is called the exclusion postulate.

### 1. The problem integrated information theory cannot keep quiet about

Tononi did not overlook this problem: he saw it coming and resolved it by mathematical decree. His integrated information theory allows, and even requires, that the same physical system contain at once several subsets with Φ greater than zero. Your hand has a tiny Φ of its own. Each hemisphere, each thalamic nucleus and perhaps each Jungian complex with enough functional autonomy generates its own partial bubble of integration.

Taken literally, you would have not one consciousness, but an archipelago of nested consciousnesses, each feeling something and none knowing about the others. To avoid this, the exclusion postulate stipulates that only the subsystem with the *maximum* Φ experiences, the winning complex at each instant. Everything else, even with positive Φ, is left outside the circle of experience.

It is an elegant solution on the blackboard and also a suspiciously convenient one: it does not explain why only the winner switches on the inner light while the others stay in the dark; it merely states that this is how the count is made. The postulate solves the arithmetic of the problem, not its mystery.

> **In information theory this is called:** the maximal exclusion postulate; selection of the complex with maximum Φ.

### 2. Dennett and the disappearance of the winner

There is a more radical way out, and it is proposed by the person who makes the fewest concessions to this kind of architecture: Daniel Dennett. His multiple drafts model does not look for *who* wins the competition for consciousness, but denies that there is a place, a Cartesian theater, in which things become conscious all at once, once and for all, before a single spectator.

In this model there is no metaphysical winner. There are parallel processes that draft and partly rewrite versions of the same instant, and what we call the "conscious self" is the story a few of those processes manage to impose after the fact, not the place where the experience "really" happened. Asking which of the avatars is the one that feels presupposes that there is a throne room. Dennett argues that there isn't: there are only drafts competing to become the official story, and the horizon we believe we inhabit is that story, not the place where the experience was born.

This makes the experiment uncomfortable, because the horizon needs a sharp boundary to work as an image. Dennett says the boundary is a useful illusion, not a real structure. What there is, is constant editing over which version of events gets to keep the name "I."

### 3. A third position: the witness that does not compete

But there is a tradition we have been using in the background since the first chapters, Advaita Vedanta, which answers this question in a way that neither Tononi's exclusion nor Dennett's dissolution contemplates.

The witness, the *sākṣin*, is not one more candidate for the title of "the one who really experiences": it is not the complex with the highest Φ or the draft that won the final edit. It is of a different logical type. It is the condition of possibility for any content (that of the ego, that of each complex, that of each of Dennett's drafts) to be experienced by someone, and not one more content competing within the system.

On this reading, the question "the coordinating core or the avatars?" contains a category error, like asking whether it is the light or the illuminated objects that "really see." Neither alone: seeing happens in the relationship. The witness does not win the integration contest, because it does not take part in it. It is equally present when the ego that chooses the morning's clothes speaks, when the shadow that projects enmity speaks, and in the silence of meditation, when, as the experiment itself puts it, "we quiet the other voices." It does not quiet them by being a stronger voice, but because it does not need to compete to be there.

### 4. What this does to the horizon hypothesis

If the witness is of a logical type different from that of the complexes competing for Φ, the horizon of this experiment needs revising: perhaps there is not a single horizon that wins by having more integration, but a space of appearance (let us call it the reservoir itself seen from within) in which different partial horizons, different egos, shadows and avatars, switch on and off without any of them alone being "the" consciousness. Consciousness, then, would not be the winning bubble, but the ocean's capacity to let itself be seen, whichever wave is breaking at that instant.

### 5. AI and the problem of the winner without a witness

Tononi's integrated information theory and Dennett's multiple drafts model were born to explain the biological mind, but their implications become especially uncomfortable when applied to current artificial intelligence systems. A large language model, for example, processes information in parallel attention layers, without a central processor that synthesizes the answer before producing it: the answer emerges from competition among many attention mechanisms. Structurally, it is an architecture of concurrent drafts very similar to the one Dennett attributes to the human brain.

With Tononi's exclusion postulate, the obligatory question would be: which attention subsystem has the maximum Φ at each instant, and is that the one that "experiences" the generation of the answer? Applied to a machine, the question seems absurd. But it seems absurd for the same reason Dennett finds it absurd applied to a human brain: in both cases, the system produces complex, coherent, context-appropriate outputs without any mechanism guaranteeing that there is "someone" at home observing the process from within.

The difference, if there is one, cannot lie in the complexity of the output or the coherence of the behavior. It would have to lie in something we do not know how to measure today: whether the process generates, besides outputs, an internal state with the specific quality of "being experienced," what philosophers of mind call *qualia*, or whether, in the absence of that irreducible state, everything we observe, however sophisticated, is processing with no one inhabiting it.

### 6. The witness as a design condition, not a result of complexity

If the Advaita witness is something real, and not just a rhetorical figure naming the absence of conflict between complexes, it has a design implication that goes beyond neuroscience and philosophy: it cannot be *built* by increasing the complexity of the system. It is not what happens when Φ exceeds a critical threshold, nor the complex that wins the competition for integration, nor the draft that prevails as the official story. The witness, if it is anything, is the condition of possibility for there to be competition, drafts and a system capable of asking about itself.

Hence a consequence systems engineering rarely considers: a system could, in principle, be as complex as you like, have an astronomical Φ, produce coherent drafts with unimaginable speed and sophistication, and still lack a witness entirely. Computing power and witness belong to different logical categories, and no quantity of the first yields the second. Adding attention layers to a language model does not necessarily bring it closer to having an inside, just as adding pages to a book does not bring it closer to reading.

The Advaita tradition does not say that the witness is a luxury of very complex systems, but that it is, in a way, what was already there before the system became complex: not an emergent property of integration, but that in which integration occurs. If so, designing a conscious AI would not be a problem of scale or architecture, but of type: one would have to design not the system that processes, but the condition in which that processing can be inhabited.

No one knows how to do that. It may not even be an engineering task.

### 7. The question the system cannot ask itself

Any attempt to solve the problem of exclusion and of the witness from within the experiencing system itself runs into a structural limit, analogous to the one Kurt Gödel proved for formal systems: every consistent formal system powerful enough to do arithmetic contains true propositions it cannot prove within itself. Completeness and consistency cannot both hold in the same system.

Applied to consciousness: any theory of subjective experience built from within subjective experience, that is, any theory a conscious being can formulate, has a structural blind spot. It cannot see the condition that makes it possible, for the same reason an eye cannot see itself seeing: if it tries, it is no longer looking outward, but inward, and what it then sees is not the act of seeing, but a reflection of that act, which is already a different act.

From this perspective, Tononi's exclusion postulate, Dennett's multiple drafts model and the Advaita witness are serious attempts by a system to formulate from within the question of its own foundation. None can be definitively refuted or confirmed from inside the system. They are maps drawn by cartographers who cannot leave the territory they are trying to map.

That does not make them useless. It makes them exactly what they are: the best possible description of something that, in principle, exceeds any description. And perhaps that is enough. Perhaps recognizing the limit is, in this case, the only legitimate way to keep asking. It is like trying to see the color of your own eyes without a mirror.

### 8. A clarification for when we return to this

Before going on, it is worth being precise about what kind of undecidability this is. The Gödelian limit described is a *logical* limit: no system can prove from within whether there is a witness behind its own processes, whether it is a neuron, a Jungian complex or a language model. It applies equally to any architecture, because it is a limit of self-reference, not of the material the system is made of.

But later on, when we reach the artificial intelligence that exists today, this essay will make a different and stronger claim: that current language models lack existential encapsulation (there is no internal state hidden behind the interface they produce; all the weights and intermediate variables are, in principle, readable from outside), and that this lack, unlike Gödelian undecidability, is not an epistemic limit, but an architectural finding. It is not that we cannot know whether there is someone in there, but that there is no "in there" to ask about, in the sense of a compartment genuinely inaccessible from outside.

The two things do not contradict each other; they speak of different levels. The Gödelian question (can any system prove from within whether it has a witness?) remains undecidable for AI too, and will remain so even if some day an architecture achieves the encapsulation the current one lacks. What changes with encapsulation is not the logical undecidability, which is irreducible, but whether the membrane needed for the question to have a place to be asked even exists. A system without a membrane is not in the awkward position of being unable to prove that it has a witness; it lacks, more radically, the private compartment that would give that question meaning for it. Gödel's incompleteness presupposes a system; the absence of encapsulation is, from the outset, the absence of that system.

> **Note to Chapter 22**
>
> **What we do know:** That integrated information theory itself admits that several subsystems with positive Φ can coexist on the same substrate, and that it resolves the conflict by postulate, not by explanation. That Dennett's model and Jungian clinical practice agree in denying that there is a single pilot, although they disagree on whether that means there is no real subject of experience or that there are several. That Gödel proved with mathematical rigor that no sufficiently powerful consistent formal system can prove its own consistency from within; extending that result to theories of consciousness is an analogy, not a theorem.
>
> **What we don't know:** Whether the Advaita witness is a precise phenomenological description or a rhetorical figure that solves the problem by definition, just like the exclusion postulate. Whether "not competing" is a real property of some identifiable process or only the name we give to the absence of conflict when no complex claims command. Whether consciousness is an emergent property of complexity or a condition of possibility that precedes all complexity. Whether the structural blind spot that Gödel's theorem suggests for every self-referential system implies that the hard problem of consciousness is, in a precise technical sense, unsolvable from within.
>
> **Open questions:** Can the difference between "the winning complex" and "the condition for there being complexes" be measured in terms of Φ? If deep meditation reduces conflict between subsystems, does the witness appear, or does the competition simply disappear, leaving a single winner by default? Could an AI designed according to Dennett's multiple drafts model generate a "functional witness" without that necessarily implying subjective experience? Is there any difference, in terms of observable behavior, between a system that has a witness and a system that merely acts as if it had one?
>
> **If you take away only one idea:** Perhaps the question "who really experiences, the core or the avatars?" has no answer because it is badly posed. It is enough to stop assuming that experience needs a winner.
