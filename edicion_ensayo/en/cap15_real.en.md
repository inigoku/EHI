Addiction is a hijacking. Something, the substance or the compulsive behavior, takes control of the horizon and diverts it toward an attractor the subject has not chosen. The addict *knows* he is destroying himself, but a signal stronger than survival itself has seized his reward system. It is as if an intruder had entered the control room and switched off every screen but one.

Parkinson's is something else: a decoupling.

The horizon remains intact and the intention is clear. The inner world keeps its coherence, its music, its desire to move. But the body no longer responds. The signal goes out, the score is written, but the musicians don't listen.

---

## The intact horizon

In Parkinson's, the dopaminergic neurons of the substantia nigra degenerate gradually, one by one, in a silence the body does not betray until the loss is massive. The motor consequences are well known: resting tremor, rigidity of the limbs, slowness of movement (bradykinesia, which turns the simplest gesture into a task of minutes) and, over time, postural instability, which makes every step a gamble. But something deeper than movement also fails, something classical neurology took a long time to name because it leaves no trace on imaging tests or in the reflexes tapped by the hammer.

Subjective time is altered.

The patient generates intentions at normal speed. His horizon works: the *now* is still an active edge, a boundary where the past is integrated and the future anticipated. But the body does not carry them out at that speed. It is not, at least in the early stages, that he thinks more slowly (over the years the disease can also affect attention and memory, but many patients in fact describe an inner agitation, a mental hurry that contrasts cruelly with the slowness of their movements), but an almost unbearable paradox: he does not feel that he is moving slowly. He feels that he is trying to move properly, with his usual urgency and clarity, and that something external, the world, the body, gravity itself, is not responding.

This is different from Alzheimer's. There the horizon disintegrates, fragments, loses the narrative coherence that lets one say "I was, I am, I will be"; its membrane cracks and the information escapes. In Parkinson's, the horizon remains the same, perfectly coherent and intact; it just can no longer talk to the body. It is like a conductor who goes on conducting passionately while the musicians go deaf one by one. The music is in his head and the harmony is real to him, but it doesn't come out. And the worst thing is not the silence, but that sometimes he doesn't know the others aren't hearing what he hears.

There is something of Greek tragedy in this dissociation: a consciousness condemned to fullness without execution, which sees what is happening coming with absolute clarity and can do nothing to avoid it. The horizon of the patient with Parkinson's is still a perfect circle, only it no longer coincides with the one the body traces in space.

## Time that decouples

Subjective time is density of integration: how much new information comes in per unit of real time, how many edges of the horizon are updated, how many gradients cross in each second lived. When we are bored, time stretches because the density of integration is low: few changes, little novelty. When we are in danger or in love, time becomes dense because the density is extremely high: too much information per second, too many events to integrate.

Dopamine calibrates that internal clock. It is not the only neurotransmitter involved (glutamate, serotonin and opioid peptides also play a part), but it is the main metronome. When it runs short, as in Parkinson's, the calibration fails: the internal clock keeps beating time, but no longer in step with the world's.

The horizon goes on generating "nows" at its own pace, and each is still a unit of integration, a mouthful of coherent experience. But that pace is no longer synchronized with the outside world. The patient who takes three minutes to cross a room, three minutes that feel endless to whoever is with him, does not feel them as endless, but as normal. Or, more exactly, he does not experience them as slow, because his horizon has not generated fewer "nows" than it should. What he feels is a lag, as if the world had gone into fast motion while he carries on at normal speed.

As some patients describe it, treatment with levodopa (the dopamine precursor that crosses the blood-brain barrier and is converted into the missing neurotransmitter) restores synchronization before movement: first they notice that "the world is back to its speed," and only afterward that "the body is moving again." Time is repaired before the body, as if dopamine acted first as a temporal calibrator and then as a motor facilitator. It is like adjusting the tempo of the score before the musicians can play in unison.

When levodopa works, the horizon goes back to sharing other people's time before it goes back to sharing their space: simultaneity is restored before motor function. That suggests something important about the architecture of the horizon: shared time is more fundamental than shared gesture. We can move our bodies in temporal solitude, as in a dream, but we cannot truly be with others if our "nows" do not coincide.

There is also a lesson for medicine. For decades, Parkinson's was treated as a disease of movement. But patients knew, and many said so in interviews neurology did not know how to listen to, that the first thing they lost was not strength, but synchrony; that they felt "out of phase" before they felt slow; that they lived in a slightly different, parallel time, in which intentions were still valid but no longer coincided with their execution. The horizon model makes it possible to hear that: to take the patient's experience seriously as data, not as an epiphenomenon.

## Trauma as frozen geometry

If Parkinson's is a progressive decoupling between intention and execution, trauma is something subtler and older in the history of the brain: a geometry that froze at a particular moment and has deformed everything else ever since.

When an event overwhelms the system's capacity for integration, when the information is too intense, fast or contradictory to be processed in the available "now," the horizon does not reject it; it can't, because rejecting requires processing. Instead, it encapsulates it: it stores it unprocessed in a watertight compartment, hermetically sealed. It does not forget it (forgetting would be integrating, assimilating it noiselessly into the past), but stores it without distributing it. The information is still there, intact, in its original form, like a fly in amber.

In software engineering terms, this encapsulation works like **sandboxing**. To prevent a critical exception (emotional collapse) from propagating an error that would crash the entire operating system of the self, the system isolates the corrupted fragment in a closed container. That container does not communicate with the main flow of events (the ordinary *event loop*) and is ignored by the garbage collector: it cannot be *scrambled*. It keeps running in the background like a zombie process or a thread stuck in *deadlock*. When an outside stimulus touches the container's API (the *trigger*), it responds by launching its frozen exception handler (the *flashback*) without going through the global timeline.

Trauma is information that could not be scrambled. In information theory, *scrambling* is the process by which local information is redistributed into global correlations and stops being a hot spot to become uniform heat: experience, narrative memory. The trauma should have become global correlations, assimilated experience, a memory that tells a story. Instead, it remains trapped at a point, like a singularity that deforms everything else. What happened is not forgotten: it is still a *now*, not a *then*.

The symptoms of post-traumatic stress are manifestations of that gravitational deformation. A *flashback* is not a memory: there is no narrative distance, no "memory of," no possibility of saying "that happened to me at another time." It is the sensation that the horizon is crossing the same point again and recreating the geometry of the traumatic moment as if no time had passed. The body reacts with the same adrenaline, the same trembling, the same dissociation. The prefrontal cortex, which should say "this is over," is disconnected or overridden. The horizon does not *remember* the trauma: it *relives* it, because for that part of the system it never stopped happening. It is like a scratched record: every time the needle passes over the damaged groove, the sound itself plays again, intact and torn.

For that part of the horizon, time has not passed. The information remains trapped in the same instant, waiting, in a sense we don't fully understand, to be integrated at last. It is like an informational debt the system cannot pay off, because the creditor no longer exists or because the currency has changed.

### The body that remembers what the mind cannot name

There is a dimension of trauma the horizon model illuminates with particular clarity: the body as a repository of unintegrated information. When the horizon encapsulates a traumatic event, it does not store it only in the cortex (in fact, the cortex often has no narrative access to it), but in the body: in the tension of the trapezius muscles, in the hunched posture, in the held breath, in the vagal tone that does not recover. The body becomes the archive of what could not be processed.

This explains something body-oriented therapists have known for decades: that trauma is not cured by talking alone, because it is not in language. It is in the geometry of the body, in the topology of muscular tension, in a heart that cannot slow down. To "unfreeze" it one has to intervene in the body: in breathing, in movement, in posture. The body is where the horizon deposited the information it could not integrate, without that making it *the key* to trauma.

### Complex trauma and blurred edges

Not all trauma is a single event. Complex trauma, the kind born of years of abuse, neglect or a hostile environment, does not have one clear "scratched record," but thousands of scratches, thousands of microtraumas that accumulate until the entire surface is deformed. In that case, the frozen geometry occupies a whole region of the horizon in which integration fails chronically.

The result is a horizon that works, but with a permanent zone of shadow. The person can live, work and love, but there is always a part of the "now" gripped by unprocessed information, a phantom gravity that pulls everything down. It is chronic depression, structural dissociation, the feeling of never being entirely *present* because part of the horizon is still trapped in another time.

## Neurodiversity as a different topology

The horizon model makes it possible to speak of autism, ADHD, schizophrenia and other conditions not as deficits to be corrected, but as different topologies: different ways of organizing the same information, valid informational architectures that do not coincide with the statistical norm.

In physics, topology is the way the points of a space are connected. Two spaces can have the same local metric, the same distances at small scales, and different topologies: one may be a sphere; another, a torus; another, a plane with holes. The local rules are the same, but the global properties (which paths are possible, which regions are connected, what information can circulate) are very different.

The neurodivergent brain has a different topology.

### Autism: local hyperdensity

In autism, information is distributed differently from childhood. Many functional connectivity studies find denser local connections (sensory hyperconnectivity) and less efficient long-range connections. The horizon is extraordinarily rich in local detail, at the cost of more fragmented global integration.

From outside, this looks like social difficulty, rigidity, a need for everything to stay the same, meltdowns at unforeseen changes. From inside, it is possible, and more and more autistic voices describe it this way, that the horizon experiences densities of information neurotypical people do not reach. That "sensory overload" is not a filtering failure, but an input of data that is too faithful, too detailed, too rich to integrate at the pace the world demands. It is a microscope in a world made for magnifying glasses.

The problem lies not in the autistic topology itself, but in the friction between that topology and an environment that presupposes another. A world with fewer simultaneous stimuli, predictable routines, time to process and respect for internal rhythms would not "cure" autism, because there is nothing to cure, but it would greatly reduce the suffering of that topology when it has to function in an environment hostile to it.

### ADHD: the shifted threshold

In the terms of the model, ADHD is a structural dopaminergic hypoactivity in the circuits of reward and sustained attention. The horizon does not generate enough internal reward signal to keep attention on tasks that are not stimulating in themselves. What is missing is fuel for the will, which in this model is an informational function that needs dopamine to sustain itself, not an act of moral strength.

The horizon with ADHD seeks stimuli that raise the signal: urgency, novelty, risk, conflict, intense interests. This has nothing to do with impulsiveness in the moral sense: its reward system needs intensities the ordinary world (repetitive tasks, bureaucracy, traditional schooling, office work) does not provide.

There is a quiet heroism in this. Adults with ADHD who have built lives that work have not done so by "overcoming" their condition, but by finding slopes: emergency professions, intense creativity, environments with a lot of novelty, passionate relationships. They have built themselves a world that gives their engine the incline it needs, and in that world their topology is an advantage.

### Schizophrenia: the horizon that generates without stimuli

Schizophrenia is perhaps the hardest condition to fit into the horizon model, because it is not just a different topology, but a horizon that generates integration where there is no information to integrate: voices without a speaker, intentions without an agent, connections between events that in the shared world are unrelated.

It is as if the brain's prediction system, the apparatus that builds models of the world to anticipate what will come, switched on without sensory input and produced models of other horizons that aren't there. The patient hears voices not because his ears are failing, but because his predictive system generates predictions of speech where there is no speech. He sees connections not because he is "paranoid," but because his integrating apparatus assigns correlations where the rest of us don't see any. It is a radar that detects signals where there are no planes.

Here the suffering is deep and real. On top of the friction with the environment comes living in a world in which predictions do not match reality, in which the internal model is chronically out of phase with the external one. In the terms of the model, antipsychotics, which block dopamine D2 receptors, work by turning down the gain of the predictive generator: they reduce the system's sensitivity to internal signals and force a closer correspondence between what is generated and what is perceived.

But even here the model agrees with something computational psychiatry has been exploring for years with predictive coding models: that schizophrenia is not an "absence of reality," but an "excess of internal generation." That the patient is not "disconnected" from reality, but "overconnected" to his own predictive apparatus. That the difference is not qualitative (they see things that don't exist and we don't), but quantitative: we all generate predictions, we all have automatic thoughts, we all hear our inner voice. In schizophrenia, the volume of that internal generation exceeds the threshold beyond which it can no longer be distinguished from external perception.

## Resonance between different horizons

One consequence of the model that is rarely discussed is what happens when two horizons with different topologies try to communicate. In this framework, empathy is not just "putting yourself in someone else's shoes," but synchronizing two informational topologies whose rules of integration may be incompatible.

A neurotypical horizon and an autistic one do not just perceive different things: they perceive in a different *way*. One integrates top-down, giving priority to context, intention, social gesture; the other, bottom-up, giving priority to detail, sensation, exact sequence. When they meet, their horizons generate "nows" with different internal structures.

Historically, the adaptation has always fallen on the divergent horizon. It is the autistic person who must learn "social skills"; the person with ADHD who must "concentrate"; the person with schizophrenia who must adapt, and the treatment they often genuinely need ends up being the only adaptation offered to them. Rarely is the neurotypical environment asked to adapt its frequency, turn down the volume or respect the other's rhythms of integration.

The horizon model suggests that this asymmetry is not necessary: resonance is possible if both horizons are willing to modulate their frequencies. Communication between different topologies does not require one of them to disappear, but for both to find a common rhythm, even if temporary and partial.

## What is not broken

Calling these conditions "disorders" presupposes a standard horizon from which they deviate, a "correct" architecture of which all others would be deformations. But if the horizon is an emergent informational architecture, there is no reason to assume that only one is valid.

There are horizons that process, integrate and resonate in different ways. Some see the world through a microscope and others through a wide-angle lens; some need a slope to get started and others work better on the flat; some generate music that makes no sound and others hear music where everyone else hears only noise.

This does not deny the suffering, which is real when the topology cannot function in the world around it. And in schizophrenia it does not come only from friction with the environment: there, treatment is not a concession to the majority, but often what gives the horizon back a shared world. But suffering is not the same as being different. An autistic horizon suffers in a world designed for neurotypicals, and that does not mean its topology is wrong, but that the world has no room for it: the stairs have no ramp, the lights flicker too fast, the instructions presuppose a speed of integration that isn't its own.

The model cannot say what world we should build; it is not a political program or a treatise on urban planning. But it can say something medical language tends to hide: the difference lies not in the quantity of consciousness, but in its form. A horizon is not more or less real, or more or less worthy, or more or less human for being different.

And the suffering of many of these conditions comes not from the topology itself, which is what it is, neither better nor worse, but from the friction between that topology and an environment that presupposes another. Friction generates heat; heat, pain; pain, pathologization; and pathologization, a prison of labels from which it is very hard to escape.

> **Note to Chapter 19**
>
> **What we do know:** In its early stages, Parkinson's affects mainly movement and leaves cognition largely intact; over the years it can also affect attention, mood and memory. Dopamine is involved in time perception as well as in movement. Traumatic memories tend to be intrusive, fragmented and poorly integrated into autobiographical memory. Neurodivergent conditions show measurable differences in brain connectivity.
>
> **What we don't know:** Whether levodopa restores temporal synchronization by the same mechanism as motor function, or by independent pathways. Whether trauma can be "unfrozen" without reactivating the singularity. What environments would make currently pathologized topologies functional.
>
> **Open questions:** Is schizophrenia an excess of predictive generation or a deficit of filtering? Can a horizon change topology without ceasing to be itself? By what right does a majority define the "correct" architecture of consciousness?
>
> **If you take away only one idea:** Not everything that looks broken is. Some geometries simply don't fit the world that measures them.
