Txiki had a particular way of looking: neither the erratic gaze of an animal scanning for threats nor the empty gaze of an animal processing stimuli with no one behind it. His was stiller: a sustained, directed attention that recognized you and stayed. That *was there*.

He was a medium-sized mongrel, short cinnamon-colored coat, a white patch on his chest that looked carelessly drawn. He had no pedigree and no special training. But when he looked at you, there was something in that look you could not explain with reflex circuits. No one had trained him to look at you like that: no immediate reward, no command, no operant conditioning explained that staying. He was there, with you, in a shared present that needed no translation.

For decades science called this projection. Animals were sophisticated biological machines, but machines all the same. Descartes wrote in the seventeenth century that animals lacked a rational soul: they were *bêtes-machines*, beast-machines, complex but empty. Consciousness worked like a switch, on or off, all or nothing, and the switch was reserved for humans. That position had little of the scientific about it in the modern sense; it was theological and philosophical, and it served to justify the use of animals without burdening the conscience with guilt.

But machines don't look like that; they process. Txiki, by contrast, seemed to *witness*.

### The spectrum

The question "is this being conscious?" is a linguistic trap, because it assumes that consciousness is binary: you either have it or you don't, like a light switch on the wall. And some things don't work that way.

William James wrote in 1890 that consciousness is not a thing but a process, and that this process admits of degrees. "There is no metaphysical leap in nature," he said. Darwin would have found absurd the idea that subjective experience appeared suddenly, without precedent, in a single species. Evolution does not work with binary leaps: it works with gentle slopes, selective pressures accumulated over millions of years, small advantages that multiply. If consciousness had a switch, where would it be? In *Homo erectus*? In *Australopithecus*? In the fish that crawled out of the water? Seen in the light of evolution, the question itself becomes absurd.

A thermostat processes information: input, processing, output. But there is no one doing the processing, no point of view at all. Cutting it in two does not produce two thermostats with half the experience each. It produces two pieces of plastic and metal.

The worm *Caenorhabditis elegans* has 302 neurons and 7,000 synaptic connections, all of them mapped. It is the only complete nervous system humanity has charted in its entirety. With those 302 neurons, the worm looks for food, flees from heat, avoids toxins and responds to damage with behaviors that in more complex animals we would call pain. It has no brain: it has a network of neurons spread through its body. Is there experience there? Probably something: very small, very simple, so different from our experience that we have no words to describe it. But "probably something" is a long way from "definitely nothing."

The bee, with a million neurons (a network the size of a fingernail), learns flexibly, recognizes individual human faces, communicates geographical information through symbolic dances that include corrections for the apparent movement of the sun, and under stress shows negative cognitive biases, what in humans we would call mood. Bees that have been through a conflict with a bee from another colony behave more pessimistically in behavioral tests. That is an experimental finding.

The octopus, with 500 million neurons distributed through its arms, defies our intuitions about where the mind lives. Two thirds of its processing happens outside the brain, which lets each arm carry out complex sensory and motor tasks autonomously. What is it like to be an octopus? We don't know. Perhaps its experience is not unified like ours and is closer to a choir than to a soloist, where each voice has something to say and none of them fully dominates.

The dog, Txiki, shares with humans the basic neurological systems of emotional experience: amygdala, hippocampus, a complete limbic system. Neuroimaging studies show that a dog's brain responds to the smell of its human with the same activation of the caudate nucleus that humans show for people they love. This is not a resemblance: it is the same circuit, with the same chemistry and the same architecture. The Cambridge Declaration on Consciousness (2012), signed by leading neuroscientists, states that non-human mammals, birds and many other animals possess the neurological substrates that generate conscious states. It does not say they behave as if they were conscious, but that they *are*.

The question is no longer whether there is consciousness, but how much, of what kind, and with what implications.

### Anesthesia as a natural experiment

There is an experiment we run hundreds of thousands of times a day without thinking about its implications: general anesthesia.

Before anesthesia, the patient is awake, conscious, with memory, with fear, with hope. After the injection, something changes. The brain does not stop working: neurons keep firing, the heart beats, the lungs breathe. What comes undone is integration: the regions of the brain stop communicating with one another. Consciousness does not "switch off" like a light bulb; it crumbles like a house of cards when you pull out the cards in the middle.

Neuroscientists can measure it. Under deep anesthesia, functional connectivity between the prefrontal cortex and the default mode network drops dramatically. Information stops flowing in an integrated way. The patient is not "sleeping deeply": they are in a state where consciousness, as an emergent property, has stopped emerging. When the anesthesia is reversed, integration returns, the house of cards is rebuilt and the "someone" comes back.

It follows that consciousness is less like a switch than like a dial, one that can be turned down to zero and then back up again. There is no precise instant at which it disappears or returns, but a gray zone, a blurred threshold, in which the patient can hear without remembering, feel without knowing they feel, be partly present without being completely absent. That does not rule out thresholds: water heats continuously and still boils at a hundred degrees. Later on (chapters 6 and 23) the book will distinguish between the degree of integration, which works like a dial, and the closure of a horizon, which happens only once; for now it is enough to see that the yes-or-no question comes too early.

### The problem of other minds

If a dog is conscious, what about a newborn baby? Or someone in a vegetative state? Or a person in deep sleep, without dreams or a narrative self?

The baby cannot speak. It cannot say "I feel pain" or "I see colors." But its eyes follow the light, its body tenses in the cold, its heart rate changes with its mother's voice. Is that enough to infer experience? Almost all of us take it for granted: of course the baby feels. But the inference is the same one we make with the dog: we observe a behavior and attribute a subjective experience to it, without direct access to that experience.

The vegetative state is more unsettling. Some patients show brain activity that, in certain tests, suggests they can hear and process information. In isolated cases, patients in a vegetative state have answered questions inside a functional MRI scanner: imagining playing tennis to say "yes," imagining walking through their house to say "no." Their brain was working, even though from the outside there was no one talking, moving or giving any sign. Were they conscious? Everything suggests that sometimes they were, in part, and in a way our instruments are only beginning to detect.

### What makes something conscious?

A camera has more processing elements than many animal brains. A modern computer processes more information per second than the human brain on many metrics. And yet the intuition persists that in the human brain someone is home and in the computer no one is. Why?

Giulio Tononi proposed that conscious systems do not just process information: they integrate it in a way that cannot be reduced to the sum of their parts. If you split a camera's pixels into two halves, each half processes the same information as before: nothing is lost, because there never was any integration. The surgically divided brain, as in the famous patients whose corpus callosum was severed, does not give rise to two people with half an experience each, but to something more unsettling: under certain conditions, two semi-independent processing systems inhabiting the same body, each with its own point of view.

Tononi formalized this with phi (Φ): the information a system generates as a whole, over and above what its parts would generate independently. Phi is low in a thermostat, high in an awake human brain, intermediate in a dog and strangely distributed in an octopus. And it plummets under anesthesia.

| System | Phi (approx.) | Experience? |
| Thermostat | ~0 | No (borderline case) |
| C. elegans worm | Very low | Something, minimal |
| Bee | Low to medium | Something more, flexible |
| Octopus | Medium, distributed | Something different, choral |
| Dog (Txiki) | Medium to high | Recognizable, emotional |
| Awake human | Very high | The most complex we know |
| Brain under anesthesia | Drastic drop | Where does the "someone" go? |

## [SIMULATION PHI]

> **In physics this is called:** Phi (Φ), integrated information; Integrated Information Theory (IIT).  
> **In everyday life it's like:** an orchestra. Each musician plays their part, but when they play together something arises that none of them plays alone. A recording of each musician on their own is not the symphony. The symphony is in the relationships.

But IIT is not the only theory. Global Workspace Theory (Baars and Dehaene) proposes that consciousness emerges when information becomes globally available in the brain: it enters a "workspace" shared by multiple systems. What matters is not that the information is more integrated, but that it stops being private and becomes available to the rest of the brain. Attention is the spotlight that decides what enters the workspace. Consciousness is what happens inside.

The two theories, IIT and the global workspace, are not incompatible. They might describe the same phenomenon from two angles: IIT from the structure of information, Global Workspace from the dynamics of its broadcast. Neither has been proven, both generate testable predictions and both point in the same direction: consciousness does not work like a switch, but like a dial with many positions.

> **Note to Chapter 1**
>
> **What we do know:** Animal consciousness is no longer a scientific taboo. The Cambridge Declaration (2012) was a turning point. IIT is an active and much-debated theory, with defenders and critics. General anesthesia produces measurable changes in brain connectivity before local activity disappears. Non-human animals show neural correlates of emotional and cognitive experience.
>
> **What we don't know:** Where exactly to draw the line. If a thermostat has Φ>0, does it have "some" experience? The question remains open. Whether IIT and the Global Workspace describe the same phenomenon or different ones. Whether consciousness can exist in non-biological systems.
>
> **Open questions:** Can a machine reach a Φ high enough to be "someone"? Where is the threshold between "processing" and "experiencing"? Is an octopus's distributed consciousness "one" experience or "many"? What happens in the gray zone of anesthesia?
>
> **If you take away only one idea:** Consciousness works like a dial, not a switch, and the dial gives a faint light where we once thought there was total darkness. Txiki was someone who was there. And if a dog can be there, the universe is more populated than Descartes believed.
>
> **Further reading:** Tononi (2008), "Consciousness as integrated information"; Cambridge Declaration (2012); Baars (1988), Global Workspace Theory; Dehaene (2014), *Consciousness and the Brain*; Seth (2021), *Being You*.
