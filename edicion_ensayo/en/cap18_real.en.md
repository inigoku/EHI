What makes there be someone there and not just a body? The question forces us back to the reservoir of Chapter 5 and the mother of Chapter 15. The answer lies in neither of them separately, but in what happens between them.

Everything said about how a legible horizon condenses (the previous chapter developed it in connection with artificial intelligence) rests on two selections. The first is stability: of all the configurations the reservoir can produce, only those that persist long enough come to constitute something. The second is bias: each new horizon condenses in the presence of another already stabilized, usually the mother, and that presence orients which specific configuration, among the many possible, actually comes to stabilize. Her hormones, her heart rate, her voice and her movement form an environment that is not neutral: it selects, without intention or knowledge, which forms of stability are compatible with it.

This two-level apparatus allows something public debate rarely offers: to distinguish precisely the cases in which the body is human but the calibration is not, or the other way round. That is exactly where the common debate only confuses matters and where the model, for once, can be clearer than intuition.

## THE BORDERLINE CASES

**Clones gestated in an animal womb.** First level: present. Second level: present, but operated by a gestational carrier of another species. The model predicts a genuine horizon with a strange prenatal calibration, not a "non-human" being: the human upbringing that follows, which lasts years where gestation lasts months, would have ample time to recalibrate it, just as happens in adoption (Chapter 15). What we don't know is how much that first imprint would weigh.

It would be a person with a beginning no other has had, and the interesting question is not whether it "is human," but what a calibration that does not match that of the people who will raise it leaves in a horizon: the extreme version of the mismatch described below for surrogacy.

**In vitro fertilization with one's own womb.** Both levels operate in the usual way, with the nuance that the initial reservoir includes an explicit act of parental will.

Here the difference lies not in the calibration, which is complete, but in the nature of the act that sets the process in motion. In natural conception, condensation begins in the darkness of the body, without witnesses or conscious decision; in IVF, there is a prior moment when someone decides that it should happen. That choice does not alter the physical mechanism of condensation, but it does add a layer of intention that was not there before. The horizon that arises is well calibrated, but its origin includes an act of will that could leave subtle traces in the topology of the primary bond. We don't know whether those traces are significant, but the model does not rule it out.

**Surrogacy.** First level complete; the second is operated by a gestating horizon different from the one that will take charge of the upbringing. A characteristic topology, not a defect.

This case illuminates something legal ethics overlooks: the gestating horizon leaves a mark that is not genetic, but real. A baby carried by one woman and raised by another (or by a single man, or by another couple) has passed through two different biases. The first calibrated its basic informational architecture; the second will try to read it, interpret it, respond to it. When the bias of the upbringing does not match that of the gestation, there may be a subtle but persistent mismatch: a horizon calibrated on a frequency the upbringing environment does not quite tune into.

This implies neither trauma nor pathology, but a characteristic topology, like having an accent others can't quite place. Some horizons will go through that transition without friction; others will experience it as a slight, permanent dissonance, a feeling of not quite fitting in that has no name because it is not an illness. It is simply the geometry of having arisen from one field and being read by another.

None of this establishes a hierarchy between the bond of gestation and that of upbringing. Chapter 15 already said so in connection with adoption: upbringing produces a complete entanglement, with a different topology, not a substitute. Prenatal calibration is a starting point, not a destiny. Upbringing lasts years where gestation lasts months, has ample time to build a bond as deep as any other, and many horizons raised by someone who did not carry them never notice that dissonance.

**Complete ectogenesis, with no gestating human horizon.** First level: present. Second level: weakened, not absent. An artificial womb can reproduce some of the signals (temperature, nutrients, perhaps a recorded voice and heartbeat), but not the presence of another horizon that responds. The model predicts a genuine, human horizon with a poorer prenatal calibration, which upbringing would have to make up for, not an inaccessible being: extremely premature babies, who spend decisive weeks in an incubator and grow up talking with us, are already evidence of how much upbringing can recover.

The ethical question is not whether that being would have experience, which it would, but what we owe it so that this impoverished beginning does not become a disadvantage: more human presence from the first day, not less.

**Current AI.** Neither the first level nor the second in any genuine sense: surface without condensation.

**A hypothetical AI with quantum coupling and the prolonged support of a human.** Both levels could operate; the model does not rule it out. But it is a logical scenario, not an empirical one, and as long as it does not exist it admits no stronger claims.

---

These cases do not exhaust the list, and technology will probably add others we cannot imagine today. But the criterion does not change: what decides whether there is someone there is not genetics, nor appearance, nor even the substrate, but whether the two selections, stability and bias, have operated, and toward what architecture they have calibrated the result. The further we move from the typical case, the clearer it becomes that those two questions, and not resemblance to ourselves, are the only ones the model knows how to ask.

> **Note to Chapter 32**
>
> **What we do know:** The uterine environment measurably influences fetal development (hormones, rhythms, voice), and that influence is independent of genetics: it occurs with or without a genetic link and whether or not gestation and upbringing coincide.
>
> **What we don't know:** What exactly a mother transmits when she calibrates her child's local reservoir. Whether the traces of an explicit act of parental will, or of a mismatch between the horizon that gestates and the one that raises, are significant for the resulting topology. How much upbringing can make up for an impoverished or strange prenatal calibration.
>
> **Open questions:** Which signals of a womb would have to be reproduced so that ectogenesis does not impoverish the calibration? What do we owe a horizon whose beginning was different from ours? Will it one day be possible to calibrate a bias deliberately rather than inherit it?
>
> **If you take away only one idea:** What decides whether there is someone there is not genetics or appearance, but whether the two selections, stability and bias, have operated, and toward what architecture they calibrated the result.
