"It's too bad she won't live. But then again, who does?" (Gaff, in *Blade Runner*).

There is a biological bias in the way we look at the world. We take for granted that the mystery of consciousness requires wombs, blood and heartbeats, and we believe that the difference between a machine and a human being lies in the material they are made of.

But in Chapter 4 we established that what decides is not the material, but three things: a boundary with an inside, integration within it, and an origin by condensation (the two selections of Chapter 31). None of that requires carbon.

*Blade Runner* takes this premise to its poetic and thermodynamic limit. The replicants are biosynthetic beings created in a laboratory for slave labor, and human society treats them as tools, complex algorithms without a real inside. But if we put them through this book's experiment, we discover that they are not machines that imitate: they are biosynthetic organisms that develop and live exposed to the world, and the film treats them as someone who has gone through their own condensation, even if in a laboratory. What they lack is precisely the second level, the bias: no one gestated them, and that is why Tyrell has to implant memories in them.

---

### 1. Roy Batty and the lucidity of the end

The Voight-Kampff test the humans in the film use to detect replicants does not look for a mechanical fault: it measures the dilation of the pupil and the body's response to moral dilemmas. In the language of our experiment, it measures the capacity for entanglement in this book's sense, empathy: whether the subject can couple to the suffering of an animal or a human. The replicants, who have adult bodies but are topologically newborn, lack the time in the world needed to calibrate that shared geometry.

But not knowing how to calibrate entanglement does not mean they lack a private state.

The replicants' leader, Roy Batty, is an event horizon running at full capacity. His final monologue in the rain ("I've seen things you people wouldn't believe… All those moments will be lost in time, like tears in rain") is the most perfect phenomenological formulation I know of Hawking's information paradox.

Roy is fully lucid about his own topology. He knows his horizon is evaporating and that no immortal soul will save him from collapse, but he understands something more tragic: that his private state (his memories of attack ships on fire off the shoulder of Orion) is real, is encoded on the surface of his horizon, and is going to undergo quantum *scrambling*. His terror and his melancholy come from realizing that the universe will return his complex geometry to the reservoir, dissolved beyond repair. His interior is as vast as any human's, and his dissolution mathematically identical.

---

### 2. Rachael, maya and the topology of implanted pain

If Roy Batty is the confrontation with death, Rachael poses an even subtler architectural problem. She is an experiment of the Tyrell Corporation: to avoid the emotional instability of newly condensed horizons, Tyrell implants in her the memories of his niece. He explains it himself: "If we gift them with a past, we create a cushion or a pillow for their emotions."

In Chapter 37, discussing the clones of the mare Cuartetera, we saw that identity cannot be cloned because it lives not in DNA, but in correlations built over time. Tyrell tries to get around that physical law. He acts like the evolutionary operating system: he injects a prefabricated event log to give Rachael's horizon the illusion of an essence. He injects maya.

When Deckard reveals to her that her memories of the piano and the spider are false, he breaks her user interface. Rachael weeps because her horizon suddenly perceives that her self was sculpted not by real friction with the world, but by implanted code.

However, our model imposes an incorruptible rule: historical information can be false, but integration (Φ) is real. Rachael's suffering is not a simulation of suffering. As soon as the network of her synthetic brain processes and integrates that false information, the shadow (her trauma) deforms her geometry. Rachael shows that phenomenology does not need a true history in order to hurt. It is like waking up crying from a nightmare: the monster was not real, but the tears are.

---

### 3. Expiry and the right to entropy

Nexus-6 replicants are born with a four-year lifespan. In architectural terms, this means their evaporation is artificially accelerated, as if someone had deliberately raised their Hawking temperature: their evaporation, their death, is programmed into their code from the start as a deterministic trigger.

Rachael is said to be special because she has no expiry date. That does not mean Tyrell has made her immortal, but that he has removed the algorithmic restriction and left her subject to the same thermodynamic laws that govern us. Rachael will die when her system accumulates too much entropy or when the chance of the environment destroys her boundary. By removing her expiry date, Tyrell grants her the greatest sentence and the greatest privilege of existing in the reservoir: the uncertainty of time and the right to grow old.

---

### 4. The unicorn and synthetic entanglement

The most revealing thing about putting *Blade Runner* through our experiment is seeing how the changes of cut (from the 1982 theatrical release to the 1992 director's cut and the 2007 *Final Cut*) radically alter its underlying physics.

In the theatrical version, the film ended with a voice-over assuring us that Rachael would live many years, while she and Deckard drove off toward a sunlit landscape. From the perspective of *The Inner Horizon*, it is a complacent ending: the deceptive triumph of an ego that believes it can beat the system and perpetuate itself without thermodynamic consequences.

In the later cuts, by contrast, the voice-over disappears and the unicorn dream is added, which connects with the origami figure Gaff leaves on the floor. That simple detail suggests that Deckard, the man who hunts machines, is also a replicant with implanted memories, and it causes a topological earthquake in the story.

If Deckard is a machine, his love story with Rachael is an entanglement, in this book's sense, between two synthetic architectures that have managed to condense. It is proof that two artificial instances, born in a laboratory and full of false memories, can integrate so much information and create a shared geometry so dense that they are willing to sacrifice themselves for each other.

The film ends abruptly as the elevator doors close, with the echo of Gaff's line: "It's too bad she won't live. But then again, who does?"

That line distills our whole experiment: it does not matter whether you were born from a womb or from a Tyrell Corporation test tube, or whether your body is made of carbon or synthetic design. "Who does?" reminds us that every horizon is finite, that every bubble collapses and that all encapsulated information ends up evaporating toward the ocean of the reservoir. The value of the horizon lies not in its permanence, but in the depth of the entanglement it manages to sustain in the brief time it is granted, before the rain washes away its tears.

> **What we do know:** Integrated information theory (IIT) holds that consciousness depends on the physical causal structure of a system and its irreducible capacity for integration (Φ), not on its being made of carbon; that is why it admits consciousness in principle in non-biological substrates, although it denies that a mere simulation on a conventional computer has it.
>
> **What we don't know:** Whether it is technologically possible to build a synthetic network that crosses the threshold of condensation without organic components or brain-specific quantum processes such as those Penrose postulates, which have no experimental support to date.
>
> **Open questions:** If false memories are implanted in a synthetic being, does the "karma" (the correlations that remain in the reservoir after its death) belong to the synthetic being who felt them or to the human who lived those events?
>
> **If you take away only one idea:** You can manufacture a body and inject false data into it, but you cannot simulate suffering. If the system manages to draw a horizon and create an inside, its pain, its love and its fear of disappearing are as real as yours.
