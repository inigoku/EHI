## [ILLUSTRATION 10: "The bonded horizon"]
*Two bubbles touching. At the point of contact the skin becomes transparent. Inside each bubble, different landscapes that overlap in the contact zone. Particles of light traveling between them without crossing the space.*

---

There is an experience almost everyone has had and very few have managed to put into words. It happens in conversations you don't want to end, in afternoons with someone you hadn't seen for years, when you look at the clock and three hours have gone by without either of you looking at your phone. Time has not disappeared: it has changed density. More has happened per hour than usual.

The opposite experience exists too: a meeting that drags on without purpose, a wait without news. Time does not speed up; it thickens, and every minute weighs like ten.

The same clock, and two opposite experiences.

---

### Time as a property of the horizon

If consciousness has the structure of a horizon, its time is an internal property: not an external parameter the horizon records, but a consequence of the way it integrates. The system's rate of integration, how much new information it takes in per unit of chronological time, determines the density of subjective time.

A four-year-old integrates at a very high rate: every stimulus reconfigures the system, every experience is new, the horizon is constantly expanding. That is why a summer afternoon in childhood has a density an adult summer afternoon rarely reaches. The child is not happier: they process more per unit of time.

Old age reverses the process: the horizon has deeply consolidated patterns and few inputs generate new integration. The elderly are not lying when they say time passes faster: for every hour on the clock, less happens in their horizon. It is like reading a book for the tenth time: the pages turn faster because you already know what happens.

Pain and boredom confirm the model from the other side. Pain overloads the system with a signal it can neither process nor ignore, and integration folds in on itself in a loop that goes nowhere. Boredom appears when the system does not receive enough stimuli to sustain integration, and the horizon spins idly. In both cases, chronological and subjective time come drastically apart.

**Flow**, the state Csikszentmihalyi documented in artists, athletes and surgeons, is the opposite case. The rate of integration is at its peak, the system absorbs and processes without friction, and time disappears.

### The brain as butterfly

The neuroscientist David Eagleman proposed an image that illuminates all this. The brain, he suggested, lives in a world of its own making and generates its internal time from the information it processes. When something new happens, it "writes" more memory per unit of real time, and when it remembers it reads that memory back: more details per unit of time translate into a sense of longer duration.

This explains what happens in falls: people who have had that kind of accident say time slowed down enormously. Eagleman put it to the test: he dropped volunteers from about thirty meters wearing a device on the wrist that displayed numbers too fast to read under normal conditions. If time really slowed down, they would be able to read them. They could not. What happens is that extreme fear generates a much greater density of memory: when remembered, the fall seems longer because there are more "frames" per second.

This forces us to separate two clocks that ordinary language confuses. One is lived time, the duration felt while something is happening; the other is remembered time, the duration reconstructed afterwards. Density of integration acts on them in opposite directions. While integrating at full speed there is no attention left to watch the clock, and the hours slip away; but each of those hours leaves a lot of memory written, and looking back it seems long. Boredom does the opposite: it drags while it lasts and, in memory, takes up hardly any room. That is why flow makes time disappear and yet such an afternoon is remembered as full; and why the summers of childhood, lived without looking at the clock, are remembered as immense. When this book says that integration determines the density of time, it is speaking above all of remembered time; what disappears in flow is lived time.

### The chemistry of time

The rate of integration is regulated, minute by minute, by neurotransmitters:

| Molecule | Effect on time | Analogy |
|----------|----------------|---------|
| **Adrenaline** | Maximum emergency integration. Dense time, but out of threat. | The accelerator of fear. |
| **Noradrenaline** | Arousal and novelty. The other is a new, unpredictable stimulus. | First infatuation: everything happens for the first time. |
| **Dopamine** | Anticipation, not pleasure. Calibrates the internal clock against the external one. | The central pacemaker: "is it worth it?" |
| **Oxytocin** | Social calm. The horizon stops being on guard. | The social handbrake: "I'm safe with this person." |
| **Cortisol** | Chronic stress. Degrades the capacity for resonance. | The noise that drowns out the music. |

> **[SIDEBOX: Parkinson's and time]**
>
> Parkinson's disease progressively destroys the dopaminergic neurons of the substantia nigra. Its best-known result is bradykinesia: slow movements, at times almost impossible ones.
>
> What almost no one mentions, and patients describe with disconcerting consistency, is that from the inside that slowness is not perceived. The patient who takes three minutes to cross a room does not feel it takes three minutes, but that they are walking at their normal pace. Their horizon orders the movement at the usual speed; it is the body that fails to execute it.
>
> Some patients say that with levodopa time seems to fall back into place even before movement has fully improved; if so, time synchronizes before the body does.

## [ILLUSTRATION 11: "The time of the bond"]
*Two overlapping hourglasses: one for chronological time and another for the density of shared time.*

### Memory as the builder of time

We do not remember time as we lived it: we reconstruct it. Every time we recall something, the brain rewrites it; it adds details that were not there, leaves out others that were, tints the memory with the mood of the moment. Memory is a story under constant revision.

This has important consequences for the perception of time. Two people who lived the same thing (a trip, an argument, a rainy afternoon) can remember it with very different durations. Neither is lying: their horizons integrated it differently and, in reconstructing it, generate different densities.

There is a disorder that illustrates this dramatically: Capgras syndrome. Patients recognize their loved ones visually (the face is identical), but feel that they are impostors, because recognition is not accompanied by affect: the circuit that links visual perception to emotional memory is damaged. If the model is right, the consequence would be unsettling: for these patients, time with their loved ones would lose density. The hours would pass without anything "happening," because the emotional integration that gives time its weight would be missing.

### Time in chronic pain

Acute pain dilates time: every second expands to fill consciousness. Chronic pain does something more insidious. After months or years of constant pain, the system stops registering it as novelty; the pain becomes background, a baseline, and with it time flattens. The days become interchangeable: morning the same as afternoon, Tuesday the same as Thursday.

> **In physics this is called:** habituation: the system stops responding to constant stimuli because they bring no new information.  
> **In everyday life it's like:** living next to a motorway: at first you hear every car, but after a month the noise disappears, even though it is still there.

Patients with chronic pain describe it with a precision physics ought to take seriously: "It's not that time passes slowly or quickly; it stops passing." The horizon, saturated by a signal it can neither process nor ignore, stops generating the novelty that gives time its density. The result is a kind of temporal death: the person is still alive, but time has stopped happening.

### The two-person system

When two people talk face to face, their patterns of brain activity converge. The **neural coupling** that Uri Hasson documented at Princeton shows that the listener's brain does not merely process what the speaker says but anticipates their patterns of activation. And the better the listener understands what they hear, the further ahead they get: in some regions, their activity *precedes* the speaker's by tenths of a second; it does not follow it, it anticipates it.

When two strings vibrate at related frequencies, they produce harmonics that were in neither of them: the two-string system has properties the one-string system lacks.

The same happens with two horizons in sustained resonance. The two-person system integrates more information than the sum of what each integrates separately; its joint Phi would be greater than the sum of the individual Phis. The Phis do not add up: entanglement opens new channels of integration that exist only in the relationship.

If the rate of integration determines the density of subjective time, and if the two-person system integrates more than the sum of its parts, the time of the bond is denser than that of either of the two alone: not longer on the clock, but fuller for those who live it.

Those three hours of conversation you don't want to end do not just seem denser: for those who live them, they are.

### Time bases: synchronizing internal clocks

In the engineering of distributed systems and telecommunications, two independent devices that need to coordinate cannot trust that their internal clocks run at exactly the same speed. Each microprocessor has its own **internal time base**, a local oscillator governed by a quartz crystal, and because of microscopic physical imperfections those crystals drift (*clock drift*). If two systems process or exchange data without synchronizing, their time bases fall out of alignment and the transmitted information is corrupted.

To coordinate, devices use an **external reference time base**: a master clock or a central synchronization signal, like the one provided by the NTP or PTP protocols. The local oscillators continuously adjust to that external pulse, and so their internal times match in phase and frequency.

Something very similar happens in the human bond. Each brain has its own internal time base: neural oscillators and neurochemical dynamics that set the pulse of its subjective time. But when two horizons interact closely, their time bases couple. For that *phase-locking* they use a common external reference: the physical presence of the other, the rhythm of breathing, the tone of voice, the rhythm of turn-taking, the gaze.

That external signal acts as the relationship's master clock. Thanks to it, each horizon adjusts its speed of integration, and time stops being an isolated experience and becomes a shared temporal base.

When the physical channel is interrupted and the reference disappears (with distance or silence), drift returns: each time base recovers its own rhythm and the time of the two comes apart again.

### Why distance hurts physically

When two horizons have resonated long enough, each begins to model the other in growing detail: their rhythms, their frequencies and their ways of responding become part of the prediction system. If the bond is interrupted, the horizon that remains active keeps an internal structure that expects signals that no longer arrive. The channels of integration that resonance opened stay open.

The feeling of absence is not the mere observation that someone is missing: it is the horizon processing silence where there should be signal.

Separated from the horizon it resonated with, the time of the one who remains becomes slower, heavier, less dense. There is as much to do as before, but less to integrate it with.

### The topology of the bond: messages and design relationships

If the self and the other are encapsulated domains interacting through their horizons, the structure and temporality of their connection can be modeled with the design relationships of object-oriented programming (OOP).

#### Message passing
In the original conception of OOP, Alan Kay's, what matters is communication between objects. Two encapsulated objects never access each other's internal memory or share `private` variables; they communicate exclusively by sending each other **messages**.
In human interaction, message passing is the only possible route. We never access the other's neural states directly (there is no direct reading of their memory). We send messages (words, gestures, silences) that cross the physical channel and reach their public interface, and it is the receiver who, inside, runs their private functions to interpret them and update their own state.

#### Coupling relationships: association, aggregation and composition
In the modeling of complex systems not all connections are equal; each describes a different dependency between life cycles:

*   **Association**: the most basic connection. Two independent systems know each other and hold a mutual reference. A circumstantial relationship is an association: the objects interact for specific tasks, but their life cycles are autonomous and do not suffer if the other disconnects.
*   **Aggregation**: a whole-part relationship in which the components keep independent life cycles. If the whole dissolves, the parts survive intact. A group of friends is an aggregation: if the group stops existing, each individual goes on functioning normally and can associate with others.
*   **Composition**: the strongest relationship of belonging. The life cycle of the parts is inseparably tied to that of the whole: if the whole is destroyed, the parts are destroyed with it. In a symbiotic bond or one of deep codependence, the relationship becomes composition, and if it breaks, the internal structure of each horizon collapses, because its state invariants depended on the existence of the joint system.

#### Inheritance and overrides
**Inheritance** is an "is-a" relationship. An object inherits the methods and data structure of its base class, and can use or modify them.
In the construction of identity, inheritance is the family and cultural legacy. We are born with base classes loaded with default event handlers (attachment patterns, automatic responses to threat). Growing up does not consist in programming an operating system from scratch, but in selectively overriding the inherited methods, redefining the behavior without breaking the structure that supports it.

#### Synchrony and asynchrony
The speed and density of the time of the bond depend on the communication protocol:

*   **Synchronous communication**: a blocking call. The sender sends a message and halts its own processing while it waits for the reply. An intense face-to-face conversation is synchronous: the two systems enter a closed loop in real time, each waiting on the other's reply. It is the only protocol capable of generating phase-locking and synchronizing the time bases.
*   **Asynchronous communication**: a non-blocking call. The sender sends a message (a letter, a text message) and carries on without waiting for confirmation; the receiver will process it when it has free cycles. It allows efficient decoupling in time, but lacks the resonance and temporal density of synchronous coupling.

### Social time

Time is also collective. We are the only species that synchronizes its internal rhythms on a massive scale. Clocks, timetables, calendars and time zones are technologies of temporal coordination that allow millions of individual horizons to function as a larger system.

But that synchronization has a cost. Social time is an average: we eat at two because it suits most people, not because you are hungry at two; we work nine to five because productive coordination demands it, not because your body performs best at those hours. The result is a chronic mismatch between internal and external time that chronobiologists call "social jet lag."

> **In physics this is called:** phase forcing: an oscillating system forced to synchronize with a frequency that is not its own.

Studies show that this mismatch has measurable consequences for health: higher risk of obesity, diabetes, depression and cardiovascular disease. How hard the work is matters less than this: the horizon is forced to integrate on a time that is not its own.
